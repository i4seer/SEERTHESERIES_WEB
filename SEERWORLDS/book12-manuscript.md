## Chapter 1: The Quiet

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to eleven worlds. On the last one I was in a household’s count for two months. I gave the share back and left while there was still work I could have done.

I have arrived on ledges, in gorges, over water, and once inside a room where a person was asleep, which was awkward for about a minute.

This time I came out in a crack in some rock.

It was a dry place. Stone on both sides going up higher than I could see comfortably, a floor with nothing growing on it, and a cool shut-in feeling like the inside of a jar. Behind me was the crossing, which I have stopped looking at, because eleven times now I have learned nothing from it.

Nothing lived in the crack. No tracks, no droppings, no rubbish, none of the small evidence you get anywhere people go even a little.

I went out along the floor of it, and it ran downhill, and after a while there was a bank of tall reeds and then a river, brown and slow and wide, with a town on it.

The place pleased me before I knew one useful thing about it. Two roads came in from opposite directions and met at the water, and there was a flat wooden ferry on a rope, and buildings gone gray from weather, and a smell of cooking, and about sixty or seventy people doing the ordinary loud business of a place where two roads meet.

So I went down.

And by the time I got there, every single one of them was standing absolutely still and saying nothing.

I want to be clear about how strange this was. Nobody was hiding. Nobody was frightened. There was a man halfway through unloading a cart who had simply stopped with a sack in his arms, and a woman in a doorway with a cloth in her hand, and children, actual children, six or seven of them, sitting on a wall in a row, not moving, not talking, not even doing the wobbling thing children do when they are made to sit still.

Everyone was facing slightly different directions and nobody was looking at anything.

They were listening.

I could tell because of their ears, which I should describe, because they are the most interesting thing about them.

They are low, wide-headed people, about the height of a child, walking upright on two legs with two shorter arms held close in front. The head is flat across the top, and set low on either side, down near the jaw, are two big soft ears that move. They fold flat. They cup forward. They roll shut entirely, like a leaf closing.

Every ear in that square was cupped forward.

I did not know what was happening. What I did know is that when sixty people are silent and one thing arrives that has never been there before, the wrong move is to announce yourself. So I went still and quiet and copied everybody else.

I could have spoken. I can speak. I have always been able to speak, and on the last world a woman said a thing out loud in front of twenty people and it changed the amount of world that pushed on her whole family, and I was floating in the middle of it being spoken about.

I just didn’t.

It went on long enough that I began to wonder whether this was simply what these people did in the afternoons.

Then a cart came around the corner.

It came in off the north road at a good pace with two people on it, and one of them was in the middle of saying something about a price, saying it cheerfully and at volume, the way you talk on a cart.

And the entire town turned around.

Sixty faces, all at once, and every ear in the square snapped flat back against the head.

The man on the cart stopped talking. He worked out he had done something before he worked out what, which you could see happening on his face.

Then everybody was shouting at him.

A man in his fifties came out of the toll-house and out-shouted the lot of them without appearing to raise his voice, and the burden of it was that the hours are posted, they have been posted for forty years, they are posted *on the toll-house*, and if a person cannot read then a person can ask.

The carter said he was sorry. He said it several times. He said he knew, he did know, he had read the board on Tuesday and it had gone straight out of his head.

Nobody hit him. Nobody fined him. The shouting lasted about a minute and had the specific quality of shouting that everybody knows will be over shortly, and then it was, and the square filled up with noise again like water going back into a hole.

The children got off the wall. The woman went inside with her cloth. The man with the sack finished unloading his cart.

And I floated there in the middle of a town that had gone from silent to deafening in about four seconds, and understood nothing at all.

---

The building the shouting man came out of was the toll-house, and next to it was a place that fed people, and the person who fed them found me before I found anything else.

“You’re the thing,” they said. “Ossit. That’s me. Sit down. You can’t sit down, can you.”

I said I could not.

“Doesn’t matter. Float there, then. You’ve missed the quiet.”

I said I had not missed it. I said I had been floating in the middle of it wondering whether everyone was ill.

Ossit thought this was the funniest thing that had happened that week, and said so, at length, to two people who were eating, and then explained it to me while carrying bowls, which is how I learned the rule of that world: at a run, sideways, in a kitchen.

Here it is.

Everything you say comes back. Not straight away. Later. Every sound made in a place comes back to that place once, after a while, and when it comes it happens again, out loud, where anyone standing there can hear it. The return itself does not start another return. It is the old sound finishing. Then it is over and it never comes back for anybody, ever.

That is what the square was doing. There was a return due, and somebody had been waiting for it, and everyone else was being quiet so that person could hear it.

And the man on the cart had come around the corner talking, at the wrong minute, and his voice had gone over the top of it.

“Is it gone?” I said.

“It’s gone.”

“Can they hear it again tomorrow?”

Ossit stopped, which for Ossit is unusual, and looked at me properly for the first time.

“No,” Ossit said. “It came. He talked over it. It’s spent.”

---

I have been to worlds where the ground moved, where a lie could change the thing it was about, where names decided everything, where a family’s weight was a number you could see. I have arrived somewhere new eleven times now and every time there is a thing the place runs on, and every time I have to find it.

I found this one in an afternoon, which is fast, and I was pleased with myself, which is a thing I should probably watch.

Because what I had was the rule. What I did not have was any of what the rule *does*.

That evening I went back up along the river to look at the reeds, because the light was going and the water had gone the color of tea.

There were birds settling. On the second world I ever went to, at the near side of the crossing, in the dark, something moved the way a thing moves when it has been waiting, and it did not come closer, and it knew me. The town noise came across the water behind me, all of it at once, arguments and pans and somebody’s door.

And I thought about the man with the sack, holding it, for a stranger, for nothing, so that somebody he might not even like could hear four seconds of something that had already happened.

I have been on worlds where nobody would have bothered.

I went back down when it got dark. Ossit was still going, and the square was still loud, and somebody was having an argument about a gate that I gathered had been running for years. Nobody was listening to anything at all.

## Chapter 2: Every Place Has Its Own

The town was called Ferry Ground, which is exactly what it is, and I had been there one day when the list found me.

The list belonged to Tessel, who was in their sixties, and I did not go looking for Tessel. Nobody goes looking for Tessel. Tessel arrives.

I was over by the water trying to watch the ferry, which is a flat wooden thing on a rope that never gets anywhere, and I had questions about the rope.

“You’ll want the times,” said Tessel.

I said I wanted to know how they kept the rope out of the mud.

“You’ll want the times more,” said Tessel, and got out the list.

---

Here is the part of the rule I had not understood, and it is the part everything else on that world hangs from.

**Every place gives things back after its own amount of time. And it is always the same amount, forever.**

The square gives you back a few hours. The ford gives you back yesterday. There is a lane behind the meal-house that gives you back one month, exactly, every time. There is a stone room under the toll-house that gives you back eleven years.

Nobody knows why. I asked. It is the first thing anybody from anywhere else asks, and Tessel gave me the answer that everyone on that world gives, which is a sort of shrug with the ears.

“Deep and shut-in and undisturbed is long,” Tessel said. “Open and busy and weathered is short. That’s all anybody’s got. It’s like asking why the river goes that way.”

The children learn the times before they can read. There is a chant for it: *the ford’s a day, the yard’s an hour, don’t you talk in Miller’s Lane.* I heard four separate children doing bits of it that week without knowing they were singing.

The hours get posted on the toll-house. Not by any authority. Whoever keeps the list that year does it, which for thirty-one years has been Tessel, because nobody else will and because Tessel would fight anybody who tried.

It is a genuinely useful list. It is also forty-one pages long.

“Now,” said Tessel, settling in. “The reed bank.”

I said the reed bank looked wet.

“The reed bank is wet, it’s wind-moved, it’s no good to anybody. Four days, same as it has always been four days. But you’ll not hear a word of it over the reeds and the birds, and I have tried.” Tessel turned a page. “The reed bank is a waste of your afternoon.”

I said I did not really have afternoons in the way other people did.

Tessel stopped turning pages.

Tessel put the list down, which I had not seen Tessel do.

“Say that again,” said Tessel.

---

Out past the reed bank, where the ground goes up and over, there is a dry crack in the rock.

I knew it. I had come out of it two days before. I had not thought about it since, because it is a crack in some rock, and there is nothing in it.

Tessel calls it the cut, and Tessel has been going out there since before I was anything.

“How long does it give back?” I said.

“Nobody knows.”

“Nobody’s measured it?”

“Nobody can measure it,” said Tessel, in the voice of somebody who has explained this to a town for three decades and is not tired yet. “And you don’t know why, so I’ll tell you, and you’ll listen, because everybody in this place thinks they know and nobody does.”

Here is why, and it is cleverer than I expected.

You cannot find out how long a place takes by sitting in it until something comes back. Suppose you hear a voice after two hours. What have you learned? That somebody said something in the cut at some point. It could have been last spring or four hundred years ago. A return arrives with no label on it.

So what Tessel does, and has done for years while walking out past the reeds with bad knees, is go into the cut and say a thing with the date in it.

*Tessel test. Third month, year twenty-seven.*

*Tessel test. Ninth month, year twenty-nine.*

Dozens of them. Scores of them. All out there somewhere in the rock, waiting, each one carrying its own date in its own voice.

“And when one comes back,” said Tessel, “it tells you when it was made, in its own words, in my voice, and you look at what day it is now, and you take one from the other, and there’s your answer. One hearing. That’s all it takes. One.”

I said that seemed straightforward.

“It is straightforward,” said Tessel. “It’s not the tests. It was never the tests. I’ve got tests coming out of my ears. The problem is you have to be sitting in it when one arrives, and I can’t know when that is, because the only way to find out when it is, is to be there when it happens.”

---

This is the part that took me a while, and I got it wrong at first myself.

I said: but you’ve been going out there for thirty years. Doesn’t that tell you something? Doesn’t all that time you’ve sat there and heard nothing at least tell you it’s longer than *that*?

“No,” said Tessel. “It tells you nothing at all.”

And Tessel is right, and it took me most of an afternoon to see why.

Because a return happens once, at its own moment, whether or not anybody is there. If one of Tessel’s dated tests came back on a Tuesday in the eighth month while Tessel was indoors with bad knees, it came back. It happened, out in the rock, to nobody. And it is spent. It is gone forever, and there is now no way whatsoever to know it ever occurred.

So thirty years of walking out there for the afternoon and hearing nothing does not mean the cut is slow. It might mean the cut is slow. It might equally mean the cut has been handing Tessel’s own voice back to an empty crack for years and Tessel has been fifteen minutes late every single time.

There is no way to tell the difference. That is not a thing Tessel has failed to work out. It is a thing that cannot be worked out.

“So what would you actually need?” I said.

“Somebody who’ll sit in it,” said Tessel. “Not for an afternoon. Sit in it and keep sitting in it. Not get bored, not get cold, not need feeding, not leave, not stop paying attention. Until one comes.”

“How long would that be?”

“That,” said Tessel, with real feeling, “is the question, isn’t it.”

The town’s opinion of all this is that Tessel is harmlessly mad and the cut is a crack in some rock where nobody has any reason to be.

I did not agree to anything. I did note, privately, that for most of eleven worlds I had left when I was finished. On Ebb I stayed after I was finished. On Cadd I left while work I could have done was still waiting. *Finished* had stopped being the whole reason for going.

I put it down and did not mention it, and it sat in the back of my head.

---

Two other things happened that week that seemed like nothing.

The first is that Ossit started telling people about me.

Not in a bad way. Ossit runs the meal-house, and the meal-house lives on the ferry traffic, and the ferry traffic that week was up. Ossit said so out loud, to me, cheerfully, while pushing bowls at people, and I would like it on the record that Ossit was never sly about it for one minute.

“Trade’s up,” Ossit said. “It’s you. You’re worth about four extra bowls a day, you are.”

I said I was glad to be useful.

“You’re not useful,” said Ossit. “You’re interesting. It’s better.”

The second thing is Dren.

Dren is twelve. Dren spent a good part of that week explaining to me, and to four other children, and to anybody who would hold still, that Dren had worked something out that nobody else in Ferry Ground had ever worked out.

Here it is. The yard behind the smith gives back one hour.

So if you stand in the yard behind the smith and say something appalling about somebody, as Dren demonstrated with a remark about the ferryman’s cooking that I am not going to write down, then in one hour that will happen again, out loud, in the yard, in your own voice.

And if you are, at that moment, on the far side of the river, then it happens to somebody else’s afternoon and not yours.

The four other children thought this was the finest thing they had ever heard.

I asked Dren whether the ferryman would work out whose voice it was.

Dren said the ferryman was slow.

The ferryman is not slow.

## Chapter 3: Worth Sitting For

Here is what I expected, having found out the rule: that Ferry Ground would be a solemn sort of place. Careful. A world where every word you say is kept somewhere should make people quiet.

It is the loudest town I have ever been in.

They talk over each other. They talk while eating, while working, while walking away from the person they are talking to. Ossit conducts entire conversations with people two buildings away and gets answers.

And then, four or five times a day, for a few minutes, all of it stops, because there is a return due and somebody wants to hear it. And then it starts again.

That is the thing about Sennet I like best. The quiet is not a way of being. It is an appointment. You keep it and then you go back to being deafening.

---

The other thing I got wrong is what returns are for.

I had assumed something important: that a world where the past comes back must be full of people listening to it, and that it must be terribly moving.

Most returns are junk. I sat in on a fair number that first week: a great deal of somebody’s cart going past, coughing, an enormous quantity of people saying *right* and *well then* and *I’ll want that back by Thursday.* Weather. A child in the distance being told to come in.

What people use them for is checking things. Somebody says they agreed to a price, somebody else says they agreed to a different price, and if the argument happened somewhere with a short return you go and listen at the hour. This is the entire legal apparatus of that town, and it works extremely well in the sense that it settles what was said, and extremely badly in the sense that it settles nothing else.

I watched one of these. Two men, a fence, a disagreement about who had promised to do what about it. Half the square came to listen, because half the square always comes.

The return arrived. Both men had said what both men said they had said. The words had never been in question.

And then they had a much worse argument about the way it had been said: whether *I’ll see to it* meant *I will see to it* or *stop asking me*. That one could not be settled by anything, and they went off still doing it, and somebody’s aunt said they had been at it eleven years and would be at it another eleven.

That is the shape of nearly everything on that world. **They can always find out what. They can never find out how it sounded to anybody but themselves.**

The other use is keeping things on purpose. People go to long-return places and say things for whoever is standing there in fifty years, and I say this with affection: nobody talks in a long-return place the way they talk anywhere else. They get a voice on.

And the last thing about returns is the one that catches people out, which is that you have to actually be there.

A woman called Merrin missed a four-year return that week by about ten minutes, because a wheel came off a cart at the top of the north road. She stood in the lane afterward with her ears flat back, did not say anything for a long time, and went back to her house.

Nobody blamed the cart. Nobody blamed her. Being late is the ordinary tragedy of that world, and everybody there has had one and expects another.

---

I found out what I am for on Sennet on the fourth day, and it was the first time in a long while that I have been genuinely pleased with myself.

I do not get bored. I do not get cold, or hungry, or stiff, or restless, or fed up, and I have never had to leave anywhere because being there was uncomfortable, because nothing is.

On Cadd that had made me good at a four-hour turn. Four hours still had an end. On Sennet, being able to stay when nobody knows the end is the whole job.

Half the difficulty in that town is that the person who needs to hear a thing cannot be in that place at that hour, and there is no way to record it and no way to move it and no way to ask it to wait. If you are not there, it is gone.

I sat in on three returns for other people that week. One was a man wanting to know whether his mother had said *the brown one* or *the black one*, about a goat.

It was the brown one.

He was so pleased he tried to give me something, then remembered I have no hands, and stood there holding a small cheese, and gave it to Ossit instead.

---

Havel works the ferry.

Havel is sixteen and poles the flat across and back all day, hauls what needs hauling, ties up, unties, and shouts at people who try to get on with more than the flat will take. Havel is very good at it, in the way you only notice by watching somebody else do it badly.

Everybody in Ferry Ground wanted to talk about me that week. Ossit commercially. Tessel as a piece of equipment. The children wanted to look at me, and two separate people asked if I was a bird.

Havel looked up once, on the first morning, and said, “You’ll want to keep off the rope.”

That was all of it. I was, as far as Havel was concerned, a thing that had turned up, like weather, and the ferry still had to go across.

I found this restful and started spending my mornings out there.

I want to be clear about what that was. Havel did not need me. That ferry went across exactly as often and exactly as well as it would have gone if I had never come out of a crack in the rock. I liked watching the river; Havel did not mind me being there. Once or twice I saw something coming downstream before Havel did and said so, and Havel said *right* and dealt with it, and would have dealt with it forty seconds later anyway.

That was the whole of the arrangement, and it was not an arrangement.

Havel does not talk much. Not sullen. Busy. But there is a specific thing Havel does not talk about at all, which I noticed because of how carefully other people did not bring it up, and I did not ask, and that was one of the few genuinely sensible decisions I made on that world.

---

Sudd keeps the toll-house.

He out-shouted the square at the carter on my first afternoon, and he is in his fifties, and he is the least dramatic person in that town by a long way. The toll-house takes the road money, keeps the ledger, and holds the key to the stone room underneath, which is the eleven-year place, and which Sudd is entirely fair about in a way that pleases nobody.

I got to know him because the toll-house is next to the meal-house and because he eats slowly. He asked me sensible questions: where I had been, and whether I had ever been anywhere with no rule at all, which is a better question than most people manage. I said no. He said he thought not.

And on the fifth evening he said, not making anything of it, that there was a return of his coming up in Miller’s Lane.

Miller’s Lane gives back one month.

“What’s in it?” I said.

“My boy went downriver at the end of last month,” Sudd said. “Work. He’s fine, he writes, he’s better off.” He turned his bowl around. “We said our goodbyes in the lane. I’ve been thinking about it since and I can’t get it back. I know what we said. I can’t remember how it went.”

I said I thought that was a strange thing not to be able to remember.

“It’s the commonest thing there is,” said Sudd. “You remember the words. The rest goes.”

He was embarrassed. I could see he was embarrassed, and I could see he had decided to say it anyway, and that the deciding had taken him a while.

“When is it?” I said.

He told me the day and the hour.

“Would it help if somebody was there?”

Sudd looked at his bowl for a while.

“It would,” he said. “I’d rather not stand in a lane by myself looking like a man who’s counting days.”

I said I would come.

I remember the thought exactly, and I have gone over it more times than is useful: *this is easy. This one is genuinely easy. I don’t have to do anything. I don’t have to carry anything or hold anything down or say anything clever. All I have to do is be there and not make a sound, and not making a sound is the one thing I can absolutely guarantee.*


## Chapter 4: A Thing That Makes No Sound

Miller’s Lane runs behind the meal-house between two walls. It is a shortcut for people carrying things and a place where the meal-house keeps its barrels, and it gives back one month.

We got there early. Sudd had shut the toll-house, which he does not do, and put a note on it, which I gathered was an event.

The hour had been on the board for a month, in Tessel’s handwriting, because Sudd had gone and had it put up the day after his son left. Anybody could have read it and most of them had. Two women came past with baskets while we were waiting, worked out what was happening, and went the long way around by the yard.

Nobody discussed it. Nobody said *ah, of course.* They just went around.

Sudd stood against the wall with his arms folded.

“You’ll think I’m soft,” he said.

I said I did not think anything of the kind.

“He’s twenty-two. He’s got work. He writes.” He said it in the flat way people say things they have already said to themselves several times. “There’s men in this town haven’t heard from theirs in years.”

I said that was true and had nothing to do with anything.

Sudd made a noise with his ears that I had learned meant something like *well.*

Then he said: “It was the last morning. We were carrying his things down to the flat and we stopped here because I’d forgotten the small bag, and I said something and he said something back, and that was that, and he got on the ferry.”

“And you know what you both said.”

“I know exactly what we both said. I’ve had it in my head for a month.” He looked up the lane. “I can’t remember how he said it. I can’t remember how *I* said it. Whether I made it hard for him or whether I was decent about it.”

He stopped, because he had got further into it than he meant to.

I did not say anything. There was nothing useful to say, and I had by then been on Sennet long enough to know that the useful thing was to be quiet in a lane with a man who did not want to be there alone, which is the sort of job I am actually built for.

---

Here is what I knew, out in that lane.

I knew a return would happen at a fixed time and could not be moved. I knew that if anybody made noise over it, it would be drowned, and spent, and would never come back for anybody. And I knew that I can speak, and that my voice is a sound like anybody else’s, and that one word at the wrong moment would ruin it as thoroughly as the carter had ruined somebody’s afternoon on my first day.

So I did not speak. From the moment we took up position in that lane I was silent, and I was silent the entire time, and I did not make one single sound.

I want that on the record because it did not matter in the slightest.

---

They came around the corner about four minutes before the hour.

There were five of them. Dren was in front. Behind Dren were four people off a boat: two men, a woman, and a boy of about ten holding onto her.

I understand exactly how it happened. Nothing anybody did looked wrong until it was too late.

Ossit’s living is the ferry traffic; that is what the meal-house is for. So Ossit had told every boat crew that came through that there was a thing in Ferry Ground worth stopping for, and that it floated, and that it talked, and that you would not see another. That is not gossip. That is a person whose trade is up four bowls a day saying so to anyone who will listen.

The people off the boat wanted to see it. Of course they did. There is nothing between Ferry Ground and downriver except reeds.

And Dren, twelve, for whom being the one who knew about me was at that point the best thing in Dren’s life, had brought them.

Dren stopped at the top of the lane.

I saw Dren do it. Dren came around the corner at speed, saw us, saw the wall, and stopped dead with both ears flat, because Dren has known the hours in that town since before Dren could read, and Miller’s Lane is one month, and the board says what the board says.

Dren did not make a sound. I want that written down too. Dren, the loudest child on that river, put both arms out sideways to stop the people behind and did not say one word.

But you cannot stop four strangers by putting your arms out, because they do not know what it means.

They came around the corner behind Dren, and one of the men was talking, and I will never know what about, because it did not matter and I was not listening to the words.

He saw me. His face did what all their faces do. And he said, loudly, in the perfectly friendly voice of a man who has come a long way and is pleased to find that the thing he was promised is real:

“Is that it? Is that the thing?”

---

It was the hour.

It had been the hour for about four seconds.

I heard it start underneath him. It was a young man’s voice and it was saying something, in that lane, one month ago, in the morning, with a bag in his hands.

The traveler was still talking. Somebody else asked whether it was tame. The boy of ten said something to his mother.

Dren got them turned around and out of the lane in about eight seconds flat, which I want to record as genuinely fast, and Dren’s ears were flat back the whole time, and Dren’s face was the face of a child who has understood something several seconds too late through no fault of their own.

And in those eight seconds the return happened, all of it, underneath.

Sudd stood absolutely still with both ears cupped as far forward as they go.

I could hear that it was speech. I could hear the shape of a sentence, and a gap, and another sentence, and something at the end that might have been short or might have been the boot noise going up the lane.

I could not hear one word of it.

Then it stopped, because it had finished, the way a thing finishes when it has been said once and is over.

The lane was quiet again about two seconds after that.

---

Sudd did not move.

Then he said, “Well.”

I asked him what he had heard.

“Bits,” he said. “I got *the bag.* I got him saying *no, I’ve got it.*” He was looking at the wall. “There was more after.”

“Did you hear how he said it?”

Sudd thought about it honestly, which he does about everything, and which was worse than if he had lied.

“No,” he said.

The travelers were up in the square by then. I could hear Dren explaining something at high speed and one of the men saying, in the voice of a man who has been told, *well, how were we to know that.*

And he was right. On my first afternoon I had watched a whole square shout a carter down for exactly this, and I had assumed it was a rule. It is not a rule. That carter had come down the north road all his life and had read the board on Tuesday and forgotten it, and being shouted at was what you get for forgetting. This man had come off a boat into a town he had never been in, walked up a lane he had no reason to know anything about, and said eight friendly words about a floating thing.

There is no rule anywhere on that world that he broke.

Sudd unfolded his arms.

“Right,” he said. “I’d best open up.”

“Sudd...”

“It’s gone,” he said. “That’s all it is. It’s gone.”

He went back up the lane to the toll-house and took the note off the door and opened it, and took road money off two carts before the middle of the afternoon, and did his ledger.

It was gone. It had happened, in that lane, at that hour, exactly as it was always going to. Nobody could say it again. Not his son, who was downriver and did not know, and not the lane, which had done its one thing and was finished.


## Chapter 5: Underneath

Ferry Ground was angry about it for about a day and a half.

Not at me. But first: what a ruined return actually does to a town, because I had assumed it would be like breaking something and it is not.

The travelers got the worst of it and got it fast. By that evening everyone knew, and the two men and the woman came in for supper into a room that went quiet when they came through the door. Ossit fed them, because Ossit feeds everybody, and was short with them, which for Ossit is a considerable statement.

One of them tried to apologize to Sudd in the square. Sudd said there was nothing to apologize for, which was true, and said it in a way that ended the conversation, which was also true.

They went downriver in the morning.

And then Ferry Ground stopped being angry, because there is nowhere for the anger to go.

On most worlds, when a thing is destroyed there is somebody to be furious with, and being furious is a way of doing something. Here there was nobody. The travelers had broken no rule. Dren had done everything right and done it fast. Sudd had posted the hour a month in advance in the handwriting of the most pedantic person on that river.

So by the second day people had gone back to being ordinary, and being ordinary about it is what they have instead of being angry.

What that looks like: nobody mentions it, and everybody knows. Merrin, who had missed her own four-year return by ten minutes the week before, came past the toll-house twice on the second day for no reason I could see, and did not go in, and did not say anything, and both times Sudd looked up and nodded at her.

That is a whole conversation on that world. I watched it happen twice in silence.

There is also a certain amount of black humor about it, which surprised me. A man at the meal-house said, in Sudd’s hearing, that Miller’s Lane had it in for the toll-house and always had. Sudd laughed. It was not a good joke and it was not meant to be a good joke; it was meant to establish that the thing could be said out loud in a room, and it worked, and the room went back to normal about four seconds afterward.

They do this about worse things than a month. There is a phrase in that town for somebody who missed a return of a person who has died, *he was late for his mother*, said with sympathy and also, very slightly, as a joke, both at once, and I do not think you could explain to anybody there why those two things might not fit together.

Everybody there has been late once, and so nobody gets to be grand about it.

---

Nobody blamed me.

I would like to be able to write that somebody did. Being blamed is uncomfortable and it is also a kind of gift, because a person who blames you is a person you can argue with.

Nobody in Ferry Ground so much as looked at me. I was, as far as that town was concerned, a thing that had been in a lane when something bad happened near it, in the same way the wall was there. Nobody blames the wall.

Dren avoided me for two days, which I noticed, and which was not about me at all. Dren had been the one holding the arms out. I found Dren eventually on the wall by the smith’s yard, not saying anything, which for Dren is a symptom.

I said Dren had been quicker than I would have been.

Dren said, “I brought them.”

I said Dren had brought people to look at a thing that was worth looking at, which is what people do, and that if I had not been in that lane there would have been nothing to bring anybody to.

Dren thought about it and then said, with the terrible directness of the age, “That’s true, though.”

And went off to do something else, and was fine by the next morning, and never mentioned it again.

---

Sudd was the worst of it, because Sudd was kind.

He took the road money. He did his ledger. He asked me two days later whether I had ever been on a world where the sea did something interesting, because he had heard there were such places, and we had a perfectly good conversation about a coastline I once floated along for a week.

He did not bring it up. I brought it up, in the end, because I could not leave it alone.

I asked whether he would write to his son.

“I will,” he said.

“And ask him.”

Sudd put his pen down.

“Ask him what?” he said. “That’s the thing, isn’t it. I’d have to write it out. *Do you remember what you said to me in the lane and how you said it, because I’ve been standing about waiting a month to hear it and I missed it.*” He shook his head slowly, once. “He’d write back. He’s a good boy. He’d tell me. And he’d know.”

“He’d know what?”

“That it mattered that much to me.”

I said I did not see the difficulty.

“No,” said Sudd, not unkindly. “You wouldn’t.”

And he picked his pen back up. It was not a door being closed on me. It was a man having said the true thing once, out loud, to somebody, which is all he had wanted to do with it.

He wrote the letter. I do not know what was in it.

---

Here is what I worked out, out over the reed bank that night with the water going past.

I had thought about that lane the way I have thought about every place I have ever been, which is: *what can I do here?* And the answer had been *nothing, and that is fine, because nothing is what is needed.*

Sudd had asked me to come. That is where I keep arriving when I look for the place I should have known better: a man asked me to be somewhere, and I went, and being there was the whole of what I did.

I was silent. I was correct to be silent. My silence was worth precisely nothing, because the noise did not come out of me.

It came *to* me.

I am the only thing on that river nobody has ever seen before. Ossit’s trade is up four bowls a day because of me. Dren’s standing among the other children is because of me. Four people came off a boat and walked up a lane they had no business in because of me.

I do not make noise. I attract it. And attracting it is exactly as loud as making it.

On Cadd, where I put myself mattered because I had accepted weight and could hold stone. Here it mattered before I had agreed to anything. I could change what other people did simply by being there.

Being interesting is a thing that happens to other people.

---

And then, because I had got that far, I got to the other half of it, and the other half is not miserable at all.

If my being somewhere brings people, then my being somewhere *else* takes them away.

Nobody on that world could do that. Not one person in Ferry Ground could stand at the top of a lane and hold the attention of everybody who came along it, because everybody in Ferry Ground is ordinary and has been seen before. Ossit certainly could not, and would charge for it.

I can. I do it by existing, whether I want to or not. It had cost a man a month, and it would cost somebody something else eventually, unless I started thinking of it as a thing I was doing rather than a thing being done to me.

There is a job on that world that only I can do. Nobody else knows it is a job, because until I came out of a crack in the rock there had never been anything on Sennet that could cause noise without making any of it.

I was still out over the bank when it got dark and the birds stopped, and then I went back down to the town.


## Chapter 6: Eleven Years

I found out about Havel the way you find out anything in a town like that, which is badly, in pieces, from people who assumed I already knew.

Ossit started it and did not mean to. I had said something about Havel being the only person in Ferry Ground who had never once asked me a question, and Ossit said, wiping a table, that Havel had other things on, what with the eleventh coming up.

I said what eleventh.

Ossit stopped wiping the table.

“Ah,” Ossit said. “Right. You’ll not have heard.”

---

Here is what the town told me over about four days. The town is not a person and does not have one account; it has about sixty, and they agree with each other in a way I did not trust by the end of it.

Eleven years ago, Havel was five.

Havel’s father took work a long way off and was not coming back, and everybody knew that at the time. It was not a disappearance and there was no mystery about it. He was going, he had said he was going, and the whole thing was arranged in the ordinary miserable way such things are arranged.

On the last morning he took Havel down into the stone room under the toll-house.

You do not go into the stone room for nothing. It is the eleven-year place: a low room cut into the rock under the toll-house with a door that fits, the deepest and least disturbed space anywhere near Ferry Ground, which is why it takes eleven years to give anything back.

People book it. What you use it for is saying a thing you want to exist in eleven years: for a child who will be grown, for a business that will have gone one way or the other, or for yourself if you are the sort of person who wants a word from your own younger self, which some are.

So Havel’s father took his five-year-old down there, with witnesses, the way you do, because a thing said in that room is the closest Ferry Ground has to a voice that cannot be revised afterward.

And he said, in front of eleven people:

*”You’ll be all right.”*

That is it. That is the whole of it. Four words.

Then he went up the steps and got on the ferry, and Havel was raised by the town in the loose practical way that town does things, and grew up, and got good at the ferry, and turned sixteen.

And the eleventh anniversary of that morning was eleven days away when Ossit stopped wiping the table.

---

Here is what I did not understand for two days.

I thought: fine. Havel knows what was said. Eleven people heard it and they all agree. What is there to wait for?

I put this to Tessel, who is the person to put things to.

“Because they don’t agree,” said Tessel.

I said they did. I said everyone I had spoken to had given me the same four words.

“The words,” said Tessel, with contempt. “Anybody can keep four words for eleven years. Ask them how he said it.”

So I did.

Ossit says it was kind, firmly and often. Ossit was fourteen at the time and standing at the back, and Ossit’s version is that a decent man in an impossible position said the only useful thing there was to say to a child, and meant it.

A woman called Denn, who was there, says the same, and adds that he had been crying earlier that morning.

Sudd would not give me a version at all. What Sudd said was: *I was twenty years younger and thinking about my own business, and if I told you now you would be getting eleven years of me thinking about it, not the thing itself.* That is the most honest answer anybody gave me and it is also the least use.

And there is a man who mends nets whose name I never got straight, who said, without any particular weight, that it had sounded like a man in a hurry telling a child to stop making a fuss. He was not being cruel. He said it the way you would report the weather on a day eleven years ago, and said he might be wrong.

That is four accounts out of eleven, and every one of them has been told a hundred times. That is what a town does to a story. Ossit does not remember the morning; Ossit remembers being fourteen, and being sad, and everyone afterward saying *he meant it kindly*, and the whole thing has set like a floor going hard.

So the town has a version, and the version is that it was kind.

And Havel, who was five, and remembers nothing at all, has never believed it.

---

I got the question wrong in front of Tessel and was corrected at length, so I will be exact about it.

It is not whether Havel’s father was a good man. Nobody in that town thinks he was a bad one. It is not why he went, which everybody knows, or whether he should have, which is nobody’s business now.

The question is what those four words were doing.

Because *you’ll be all right* is two completely different sentences depending on the voice it is said in. Said one way, it is a man kneeling down and giving a child the only true thing he has, which is a promise about a future he will not be in. Said another way, it is a man with a bag over his shoulder and a ferry to catch, saying the thing that stops the conversation.

Nobody alive can tell Havel which one happened. It is the same problem as the two men and the fence, except that it has been running eleven years instead of eleven minutes, and there is nowhere short to go and listen.

But that room kept it. The room does not remember, and does not polish, and was not fourteen at the time. It gives back what it got, once, and then it is done.

---

I went to Sudd about the room.

I would like to say I hesitated for good reasons. I hesitated because it was awkward and was going to be awkward whatever I did, and hesitating did not improve it.

The booking is Sudd’s, and Sudd holds the key, and Sudd decides who is in that room at any given hour. That is not a grand power. It is a ledger and a lock. It is also, on the day of an eleven-year return, absolute.

I asked whether Havel had booked it.

“Booked it four years ago,” said Sudd. “Nobody else was going to want it.”

I asked who would be down there.

“Havel. Any of the eleven that’s still about and wants to come. That’ll be seven, eight. Me at the door, because I’m always at the door.” He was writing. “Why?”

And I said I would like to be there, and I heard how it sounded while I was saying it: the thing that had been in Miller’s Lane, asking to be in a room with somebody else’s eleven years.

Sudd finished the line he was writing.

“Course,” he said.

That was all. He did not look up, and he did not make anything of it, and he did not say *after last time* or *if you think that’s wise*, and he wrote my being there into the ledger under Havel’s booking in the same hand he uses for road money.

I would rather he had refused me.

A refusal would have been a thing I could argue with, or accept, or be sorry about. What I got instead was a man who had lost a month of his own, being straightforwardly decent to the reason he lost it, without appearing to notice there was anything to notice.

You cannot pay that back. There is no mechanism.

---

I saw Havel that evening at the ferry, tying up in the dark.

I had spent four days assembling a version of Havel’s life out of other people’s mouths and had not once heard Havel mention any of it, and by then I understood why. Anything Havel says about that morning gets said *somewhere*, and comes back *somewhere*, and Havel has spent eleven years not adding to the pile.

So I did not ask. I floated there while the rope got tied.

Havel said, “You’ll have heard about the eleventh, then.”

I said I had.

Havel finished the knot and checked it, which Havel does even when the knot is fine.

“Everyone says it was kind,” Havel said.

And that was the whole of it.

*Everyone says it was kind.*

Not *it wasn’t.* Not *they’re lying.* Not *I don’t believe them.*

Just: everyone says.


## Chapter 7: The Hour

The trouble started two days before, and it started with Ossit, and Ossit did nothing wrong at all.

Somebody off a boat asked whether it was true there was a thing in Ferry Ground that floated. Ossit said it was, and said where I generally was, and added, because Ossit is the best advertisement on that river and cannot help it, that if they were about on the fourth they might see something.

By the next morning there were nine people in Ferry Ground who had no business there, and a boat had tied up that would ordinarily have gone straight past, and Dren had made four new friends and was giving what I can only describe as tours.

I want to be fair to all of them. Nobody was being ghoulish. Ferry Ground people knew exactly what the fourth was and were treating it the way you treat somebody’s funeral, soberly and with a great deal of arranging of who would bring what afterward. The visitors were not there for Havel at all. Most of them had no idea who Havel was.

They were there for me.

I had become, over five weeks, the reason to stop at Ferry Ground, and there is a version of that which is entirely pleasant and I had been enjoying it.

And on the fourth, at the eleventh hour of the morning, a floating thing that nobody had ever seen before would be going down a flight of stairs in front of the entire town.

---

I worked it out on the second evening and I did not enjoy it.

If I go down those steps, they follow. Not into the room. Sudd holds the door and Sudd would put his own mother out. But they follow to the square, and to the top of the stairs, and they stand at the door of the toll-house asking each other where it went and whether it can talk.

And a door that fits is a door, not a wall.

The stone room takes eleven years because nothing disturbs it. It is not sealed against sound, because nobody has ever needed to seal it. You do not need a wall when the whole town has agreed for forty years to be quiet at a posted hour, and when the only people who come down are the ones with a reason.

Nine strangers with no reason, at the top of a short flight of steps, above a room where the thing they came to look at has just gone.

My silence would be worth exactly what it was worth in Miller’s Lane.

---

So I went and told Sudd to take me out of the ledger.

He put his pen down, which in five weeks I had not seen him do without being asked a question first.

“You’ve been looking forward to that,” he said.

I said I had.

“You could stand at the back. There’s room at the back.”

I said the room was not the problem, and explained: the stairs, the square, the nine people who had come to see a floating thing and would go where the floating thing went.

Sudd listened all the way to the end, which he does.

Then he said, “You’d be doing that for Havel.”

I said I would be undoing something I had done in the first place, which is not the same and should not be given credit as though it were.

Sudd thought about that for a while.

“No,” he said, “but I’ll take it,” and drew a line through my name.

---

I want to put this down honestly rather than making myself sound better than I was.

I wanted to be in that room.

Not out of nosiness, or not only. I had spent four days collecting eleven polished versions of four words from people who could not tell me the one thing that mattered, and I was the only creature in Ferry Ground who had never heard the story before it set. I would have heard it new. Whatever was in that man’s voice on that morning, I would have got it clean, with nothing of the town on top of it.

I would have known.

And Havel would have had somebody to ask who had no version. That is a genuinely useful thing and I was not wrong to want it.

I gave it up because I had already proved, in a lane, one month before, that being where I want to be is not free.

---

The fourth was clear and cold and the ferry did not run in the morning, which I gather has happened four times in living memory.

At a quarter to the hour the square was full and completely silent. Sixty people, not moving, every ear cupped forward. This time they were not listening for anything, because the return was not theirs and was not up here. They were being quiet at a room they could not see, on the offchance it helped.

Havel went down the steps with seven of the eleven. Denn was one. The man who mends nets was another, and I thought better of him for coming. Ossit stood in the doorway of the meal-house with a cloth in both hands and did not go anywhere near the toll-house, which for Ossit is a kind of grandeur.

Sudd took the door, and shut it, and stood in front of it.

And I was three hundred paces up the north road, out where the wall comes down to the ditch, being extremely visible.

---

It worked exactly as I had expected it to, which was the worst part.

They came up the road in twos and threes. Every single one of them. The nine off the boats, a family who had come in that morning from Weel, two men who I think were simply following other people, and Dren, who had brought them and then stood beside me with both ears flat and did not look at me once for the whole hour.

They asked the ordinary questions. Whether I could talk. Whether I could go higher. Where I was from and whether there were more of me, which I answered as I always do, which is that I do not know.

I let them ask. I answered everything. I made myself the most interesting thing on that river for an hour and a quarter, three hundred paces from where it mattered, with my back to the town.

A man from Weel asked whether I was some sort of bird.

I said I was not sure.

He said his brother had seen a thing like me once at Hallet and it had turned out to be a bird.

I said that was very likely.

And somewhere behind me and below the ground, in a room I could not see, at the exact minute it had always been going to happen, a man who had been gone for eleven years said four words to a five-year-old, once, and then was finished.

I did not hear it.

I was not going to hear it. That was the arrangement, and I made the arrangement, and I would make it again.

But I want it written down that I did not hear it, because everything else in this account is a thing I know, and that is the one thing I do not, and there is now no way of my ever finding out.

---

The hour went. The square was quiet for a while afterward, the way it is, and then somebody dropped something and everybody laughed too loudly, and that was that.

Dren said, “Can I go now?”

I said Dren could go.

Dren went about six paces, came back, and said: “You did that on purpose.”

I said I had.

Dren looked up the road at the eleven or twelve people wandering back toward the ferry, and worked something out, and did not say what it was, and went.

I held my position by the ditch until the road was empty, because if I had gone down straight away half of them would have come with me.

Then I went down into a town where a thing had happened that I had no information about at all.


## Chapter 8: After the Hour

The ferry ran the next morning.

I had spent the evening of the fourth constructing about six versions of what the fifth would be like, and the actual answer was that the ferry ran at the usual time and Havel was on it.

There was a queue, because there is always a queue on a Tuesday. Havel poled across and back and shouted at a man trying to bring a hand-cart on with the wheels still on it, which is not allowed and which somebody tries about once a fortnight.

I went out and watched, and Havel said, “Keep off the rope,” which Havel says most mornings and which by then had become a joke of a very small and specific kind.

Nothing else happened for two days. I had expected some kind of statement, something with an edge to it either way. Ferry Ground did not expect that at all, and Ferry Ground was right, and by the second afternoon people had stopped glancing at Havel and gone back to complaining about the Weel road.

That is the other thing about a town where the past comes back on a fixed schedule. They are extremely good at afterward. They have had a great deal of practice.

---

Havel told me on the third evening, tying up, in the dark.

I had not asked. I had decided somewhere around the second afternoon that I was not going to ask, ever, and that if I never found out then that was the price of the north road and I had known it when I paid.

Havel said, “You’ll want to know how it went.”

I said only if Havel wanted to say.

“It was quick,” Havel said. “You forget it’s going to be quick. Eleven years and it’s four words and it’s over before you’ve got your ears right.”

Then Havel sat down on the post, which Havel does not do while there is light, and said the rest of it looking at the water.

“He was out of breath,” Havel said. “That’s the thing nobody ever said. Nobody’s ever once said he was out of breath. He’d carried my things down and come back up and he was out of breath, and you can hear it. He says *you’ll be all right* like a man who’s got about four seconds and knows it’s not enough and says it anyway.”

I asked whether it had been kind.

Havel thought about that for a long time.

“It was true,” Havel said. “That’s not the same thing. But it’s what he had.”

---

What I think about that is not mine to set down.

Here is what is.

*Out of breath* does not settle it. A man can be out of breath and kind. A man can be out of breath and desperate to be on a boat. Nobody got the answer to that argument and nobody is going to.

What Havel got was Havel’s own hearing of it. Once, at sixteen, with nothing on top: not Ossit being fourteen and sad, not Denn remembering the crying, not eleven years of a town agreeing with itself over supper.

That is a smaller thing than proof and a completely different thing from proof, and I watched it be enough. Not enough to fix anything; there was nothing to fix. Enough that Havel stopped having to hold the question open, which Havel had been doing, alone, since before Havel could read.

---

Havel did not change.

I had been half expecting somebody else to walk off that post. What actually happened is that Havel got up, checked the knot, said the rope wanted replacing before winter, and went to get supper.

The next morning Havel ran the ferry. The morning after that Havel ran the ferry. Havel is sixteen and good at a job and has friends and opinions about the Weel road, and one afternoon in a stone room did not reorganize any of that, and it would have been an insult to pretend otherwise.

What I noticed, and I may be wrong, is that Havel started talking about it. Not much. But Havel mentioned his father twice in the following week, once about something to do with rope, and neither time was it an announcement.

Eleven years of saying nothing, because anything you say gets said somewhere and comes back somewhere and joins the pile.

The pile is finished now. There is nothing left to add to.

---

Sudd and I had one conversation about none of it.

He came out to the ditch on the sixth day, which is a walk, and looked at the north road for a while, and said the wall wanted work before winter, which it did.

Then he said, “He wrote back.”

I said that was good.

“He said he was sorry he’d not made more of it at the time.” Sudd put his hands in his coat. “He said he’d been thinking about it since he went, and he’d not known how to put it in a letter, and he was glad I asked.”

I said that was better than good.

“It is,” said Sudd, and then, after a while: “It’s not the same, mind.”

I said I knew.

“No,” he said. “You do, actually. That’s a thing about you.”

And we looked at the road for a bit longer and then he went back down to the toll-house. That is the entire conversation. It was a good one.

---

The rest of that week the town was completely itself.

Ossit’s trade was down, because the boats had gone back to going past, and Ossit was cheerfully bitter about it in my hearing about four times a day.

Tessel updated the list because two of the yard times had drifted. They had not, and cannot. It turned out to be a mistake in Tessel’s own hand from nine years ago, and Tessel corrected it in front of me with the expression of a person removing a splinter.

And Dren got caught.

Dren had been running the short-return trick all summer: say something appalling in the smith’s yard, be across the river in an hour when it comes back, be innocent. It works exactly as long as nobody is paying attention.

The ferryman had been paying attention since the second week.

What the ferryman did was wait until Dren was on the flat, mid-river, with no way off it, and then say, to nobody in particular, conversationally in the middle of the water, “That’s about the hour, isn’t it.”

Dren went the color of a wall.

The whole thing came back in the yard four minutes later in Dren’s own voice, in front of nine people including two of Dren’s friends and one of Dren’s aunts, and I have never seen a child so comprehensively finished.

Ferry Ground laughed about it for a week. Dren was magnificent about it by about the third day, which is what twelve is like.

I bring it up because that is the town. Somebody waited eleven years and got four words and went back to work, and a child got humiliated by a ferryman with a sense of timing, and both of those happened in the same week in the same place, and neither of them cancels the other.


## Chapter 9: Nobody Goes Out There

Tessel came and found me about a week later, which took nerve, because Tessel had been building up to it since the afternoon I said I did not really have afternoons.

“You said you don’t get bored,” said Tessel.

I said that was true.

“I’m not asking for an afternoon. Anybody’d give me an afternoon, and I’ve had them off half this town and they’re no good to me.” Tessel had brought the list, and a coat, and the specific expression of somebody who has rehearsed. “I’m asking you to go out to the cut and stop there.”

“For how long?”

“That,” said Tessel, “is the entire point of the question.”

---

There are sixty-one dated tests out in that rock, the oldest thirty-one years old and the newest made in the spring, and not one has ever been heard coming back. As Tessel has told a town that will not listen for three decades, that means nothing at all. Any number may have come back already, to nobody, and been spent.

“You’d not be able to leave,” said Tessel. “Not for a bit. Not even for a bit. That’s what’s beaten everyone.”

I said I understood.

“And I can’t do it,” said Tessel, with visible pain, because Tessel had worked this out long ago and did not like it. “I’ve tried. You can want a thing for thirty-one years and still not be able to sit in a hole for a month. That’s the whole of my trouble and it always has been.

“Thirty-one years,” said Tessel, “and a set of knees.”

---

So I went out past the reed bank and up to where the ground goes over, and into the cut, and stopped there.

It was nothing for a long time.

The cut is about forty paces long and narrow enough at the top that the sky is a strip. No water. Nothing grows. Birds go over and do not come down. The light moves along one wall in the morning and the other in the afternoon and that is the whole of the entertainment.

I do not get bored. I would add one thing to that now: not getting bored is not the same as not noticing. I noticed every hour of it. I simply had no reason to go.

I was there eleven days.

Somebody came out on the fourth day, which I had not expected. It was Havel, with a bag of nothing much, on the excuse of seeing whether the path over the top was passable, which it was not and which nobody has ever needed it to be. Havel put the bag down, said the ferry was fine, said Tessel had been round asking whether I had said anything yet, and went away again.

---

On the eleventh day, in the middle of the afternoon, with the light on the west wall, the cut said:

**”Tessel test. Ninth month, year twenty-nine.”**

It was Tessel’s voice, younger by a bit, and slower, and enormously pleased with itself.

And then it was gone, because that is what they do.

Ninth month, year twenty-nine. It was the second month of year forty-one.

Eleven years and five months. Longer than the stone room by five months.

That is the return-time of the dry cut, and nobody on that world had ever known it, and now somebody did.

What I felt was not triumph. It was the very particular satisfaction of a number going into a list it has been missing from for thirty-one years, and I have never been so pleased about a piece of arithmetic.

---

I should have gone straight back down.

I did not. I had been in that cut eleven days and there was a stiffness to leaving it that I cannot properly explain, having no joints, so I held where I was a while longer in the light on the west wall, doing nothing at all.

And the cut said:

**”You’re late.”**

---

It was not Tessel’s voice.

It was not any voice I have ever heard in eleven worlds, and I cannot tell you whether it was old or young or what it was made of.

It said *you’re late*, and then it stopped.

And I did not know whether that was the end of it.

I need to be exact about this. A return is however long the original was. The original might have been two words. If it was, then the cut had just given me the whole of what it had and there was nothing more coming, ever.

Or there was more, and anything I said would go over the top of it.

I knew what that costs. I had watched it cost a decent man the last four minutes he had of his son, in a lane, a month before, while I floated beside him being usefully silent.

I knew it was the worst thing you can do on that world.

I said, “For what?”

---

And the cut said:

**”For coming home.”**

And then it was finished, and the light was on the west wall, and there was nobody there, and there was not going to be anybody there, because there is never anybody there. A return is not a person. It is a thing that already happened, happening once more, to whoever is standing in the room.

I got lucky.

I want that written down before anything else. I did not know there was a gap and I could not have known. If whoever said that had said it in one breath, the way people do, I would have put two words of my own over the top of it and never found out what it was.

I did it anyway. There was no usefulness in the question. I wanted the next words.

It was the first sentence I have ever heard that sounded as though it might be about me, and I have been eleven worlds without anything sounding like that.

---

I did say something else, after, into the empty cut, because I am like that.

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. It had been spent the moment it finished, and whatever made it had made it a very long time ago and was not there.

Except that *late* means somebody expected you at a time.

I do not know where, or when it was said, or who by, or what they meant by *home*, or whether they had ever laid eyes on the thing I am. I have two sentences and no way to ask for another.

That is not much. But it is two sentences more than I had.

---

Tessel got the number.

I came down out of the reeds on the twelfth morning and Tessel was at the toll-house arguing about something, and I said “Eleven years and five months,” and Tessel sat down on the step of the toll-house in front of about nine people.

Then Tessel wanted the exact wording of the test, and the date, and worked the arithmetic out loud while everybody waited.

Then Tessel said, to the square in general: “Eleven years and five months. Longer than the room. I said it was longer than the room. I have been saying so since before some of you could walk.”

Nobody had ever disputed that it was longer than the room. It did not matter. Tessel had thirty-one years to be right about and was going to be right about all of them at once.

The list went up on the toll-house that afternoon with the cut on it, for the first time, in ink.

---

The word *home* stayed with me. I did not let it make the decision.

I left the next day, which surprised nobody, because I had told them.

Ossit gave me a farewell of about ninety seconds because the meal-house was busy, which is the highest honor Ossit has to give, and told me trade would be down and it was my fault.

Sudd shook the air near me, which is what people do who have worked out the hand problem and want to do something anyway, and said the north wall had got done in the end.

Havel took me across on the ferry and did not say anything much until we were tied up on the far side, and then said, “Keep off the rope,” and I said I would, and that was that, and Havel poled back.

Then I went up past the reed bank and over the ground and into the cut, and through it, and I did not look at it on the way.

I do not know where I am going. I have never known.

I went anyway.

The last thing I heard was the ferry rope, which needed replacing before winter.
