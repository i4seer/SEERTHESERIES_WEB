## Chapter 1: The Door

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I know more than I used to. I can move. I can look at a thing until I understand it. I know that worlds have rules, and that the creatures living in them mostly cannot tell you what those rules are, because you do not explain the floor to somebody standing on it.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I arrived underwater.

It was warm and shallow. The floor was pale stone, close enough below me that I could see the grain of it, and the surface was close enough above that the light came down in broken, moving pieces. Light crawled over everything on the floor. Nothing was ever still.

I could hear a great deal. Somewhere far off, two voices were arguing about a basket. I could hear every word, but I could not see anyone at all.

Then I noticed the shells.

They were everywhere, spaced across the pale stone like stones in a field. Each one was about as big as a chair. They were bone-colored, ochre, or dull green, banded in rings, and some had small plants growing on their backs.

They did not move.

I waited a while to be sure. They did not move.

Then something small and pale shot past me, turned around, came back, and stopped in front of me so suddenly that silt came up off the floor.

“You’re new.”

It was about the size of a hand, soft, and had more little paddling limbs than I could count while it was moving. It held a small, tightly wrapped bundle against itself.

“Yes.”

“I haven’t seen you. I see everybody.” It swam a full circle around me without appearing to think about it. “What are you carrying?”

“Nothing.”

“Then what are you *for*?”

Nobody had asked me that before.

“I don’t know.”

“Well, come on,” it said, and went.

I went.

## Chapter 2: Nit

Its name was Nit.

I learned that a long way into the conversation, because Nit did not stop talking long enough for either of us to say a name. It told me about the basket argument, which it had also heard. It told me who was wrong. It told me that it had been wrong last week about something else and that everybody had made too much of it.

It swam as if straight lines were something it had never learned to use.

We passed shell after shell on the pale floor. Some were close together in clusters. Some sat in rings with a gap in the middle. As we went, arms came out of the shells — two long arms each, pale and strong — and did things: sorting, reaching, pulling at what grew near them.

One of them lifted an arm as we passed. Nit slapped it with a limb without slowing down.

“That’s Coll. He’s all right.”

“He didn’t get up.”

Nit did not answer, because we had arrived.

The shell in front of us was dark green and old. Nit swam straight to the top of it and stopped, and for the first time since I had met it, it went quiet and formal.

There was a plate in the upper face of the shell.

It was hinged and ringed by a thick rim.

It opened.

Not far. Just enough. Nit pushed the bundle through, said something polite that I did not catch, and the plate closed again.

Then Nit was off, and I had to move to keep up.

“What was that?”

“What was what?”

“The door.”

“That’s her door,” Nit said, in the voice you use for someone who has asked what water is.

“What’s it for?”

Nit stopped.

It turned around in the water and looked at me — I say looked; Nit has no face, none of them do, but there is no other word for what it did.

“It’s her *door*,” it said again.

“I heard you.”

“That’s — ” Nit made a helpless movement with several limbs at once. “Everyone’s got one.”

“What’s behind it?”

“Her home.”

“Who’s part of her home?”

“Everyone she’s welcomed into it.”

I floated there and turned that over.

“How many is that?”

“How would I know? That’s hers.” Nit sounded genuinely shocked, and then immediately stopped being shocked, because it had thought of something more interesting. “Wait. Where’s yours?”

“My what?”

“Your door.”

“I don’t have one.”

Nit came back toward me fast.

“You haven’t settled,” it said. “That’s all. You’re loose. You’ll get one.”

“I don’t think I will.”

“Everyone gets one.”

“I don’t think I’m going to settle.”

Nit laughed at me. It was a burst of clicking that came out through its whole body, and it went on much longer than I thought the joke deserved.

“Everybody says that,” it said.

## Chapter 3: Being Someone

I spent the rest of that day working out the arrangement of the world, which is what I do.

The shells were the adults. They did not move. Not slowly — not at all. I watched one for a long time to be certain, and it reached, and it turned a little on its base, and it worked at the weed growing around it, and it never once went anywhere.

The small fast ones were the young. There were maybe thirty of them and they were everywhere at once, carrying things, shouting news across open water, hanging in clusters and then bursting apart.

Everything that moved on that bank was young.

Everything that stayed was old.

Between them, all day, went baskets and bundles and messages and gossip, and I understood before evening that the young were not children the way I had seen children elsewhere. They were how the world got anything from one place to another.

I asked Nit how old you had to be to settle.

“You just know,” it said. “You get the feeling.”

“What feeling?”

“That you’re ready.” It did a fast loop, which I had learned meant it was pleased. “I’ve nearly got it. I’ve picked my place. It’s past Coll, by the yellow weed, where the water comes warm off the shallows. I’ve had it picked for a year.”

“What happens after that?”

“Then I’m set.”

“And then you can’t move.”

“Right.”

I waited for the rest.

There wasn’t any.

“Ready for what?” I said.

Nit turned its whole body toward me, and I understood that I had said something absurd.

“For *being someone*,” it said.

## Chapter 4: The Edge

Late in the day I went out past the last of the shells, where the pale floor stopped being pale and began to slope, and the water got colder against me.

There was one more shell out there. Well past the others. Dark, banded, low to the floor, with a crust of growth on the back that nobody had cleaned.

It was counting.

I heard it before I reached it. A voice, quiet, not talking to anyone.

“...four. Five. Six, and a broken one. Seven.”

I came around the front of the shell.

“What are you counting?”

The counting stopped.

There was a pause that went on slightly too long.

“Weed,” she said. “Coming off the shallows. It comes past in the afternoon.”

“How many today?”

“Seven.”

“Is that a lot?”

“It’s fewer than yesterday.”

I liked her immediately.

Her arms were out, working at something, and they were bigger than the arms I had seen all day. Her door was in the top of her shell, shut flat, with a thick rim around it. I looked at it, because I had spent the day being told that this was the most important thing about anybody, and it was the first one I had been able to look at for more than a moment.

It was smaller than the others.

Noticeably. Nit’s shell-friend Coll had a door you could have put a basket through. This one was a slot.

“Is your door supposed to be that narrow?”

The arms stopped moving.

She did not shout. She did not tell me to go away. She did not do anything I could have written down as an answer.

The rim came down flat, which it already was. Then she pulled her arms in.

Then there was a shell on the floor with nobody talking out of it.

“I asked a question,” I said.

Nothing.

I waited a long time. The light went orange and then went out of the water altogether, and out past the edge the floor dropped away into cold and dark and I did not go and look.

“I’ll come back,” I said.

She did not answer that either.

## Chapter 5: The Welcome

Nit found me the next morning and told me I had done something stupid, without knowing what I had done.

“Everyone knows you don’t go out to the edge.”

“Why?”

“Because there’s nothing out there.” Nit did a loop. “Come on. Sennet’s welcome is today.”

I did not know what that meant, so I went.

They had gathered around a shell near the middle of the bank, close in where the good weed was and where the shells sat only a few body-lengths apart. There were sixteen or seventeen set adults within reach of each other, and every loose one on the bank hanging in the water above, and the noise was tremendous.

In the middle was an old ochre shell with a wide door, and a loose Kelt no bigger than Nit going around and around above it in a way that even I could tell was nerves.

“That’s Sennet,” Nit said. “The big one’s her mother.”

“What’s happening?”

“She’s going in.”

The talking stopped, more or less, and the mother’s voice came up out of the shell. It was not a special voice. It was the voice of someone saying something they had thought about for a long time.

“I welcome Sennet into my home,” she said. “I say it in front of you.”

And then everyone around us said it back. Not together, not neatly — a ragged wave of voices going out across the shells, *she welcomes Sennet, she welcomes Sennet, she said it in front of us* — passing shell to shell down the bank until I could hear it repeating a long way off among creatures who could not possibly have seen anything.

The door opened wide.

Sennet went in.

It was very quick. She was inside for less time than it takes to say what happened, and then she came out again, and the door shut, and the noise came back all at once, twice as loud as before.

I turned to Nit.

“That’s it?”

“What did you want?”

“She came out.”

“Course she came out. She lives here.” Nit was already moving off toward where somebody was handing out food. “She’s not going to *stay* in there.”

“Then what changed?”

Nit stopped, and for once it took the question seriously, which I have since decided was because the answer was so large that even Nit could feel the size of it.

“She’s part of her mother’s home now,” it said. “Forever. That’s it. That’s the whole thing. Doesn’t matter where Sennet goes, doesn’t matter if she settles at the other end of the bank, doesn’t matter if she dies first. Her mother’s home has a place for her now. It always will.”

“What if they fall out?”

“Then she’s still part of the home.”

“What if her mother didn’t want her to be part of the home anymore?”

Nit looked at me for a second.

“You don’t get to,” it said.

## Chapter 6: Wide Doors

I spent that morning going along the bank and looking at doors, and I learned more from that than from anything anyone told me.

Wide doors were common in the middle. The shells in there sat close, and the arms could reach one another, and all day long things went from shell to shell — food, shells of water, small objects I could not identify, and constant talk.

Out toward the edges the shells got further apart and the doors got smaller.

I noticed that. I want it on the record that I noticed it that morning, and that I thought it was about age, because the shells further out looked older, and I did not think about it again for a long time.

Marr was two along from the edge — an ordinary shell, ordinary door, an enormous appetite for other people’s business. She talked to me for an hour without ever quite asking who I was.

“You’re the one that came in with Nit.”

“Yes.”

“No door on you at all.”

“No.”

“Well.” She said it comfortably. “You’ll settle.”

I had decided by then to stop arguing about that.

“Can I ask you something?”

“You can ask.”

“How does someone become part of a home?”

Marr laughed — a set Kelt laughs with the arms; the whole front of her went loose and jangling.

“How does someone become part of a home,” she repeated. “Well now. Because you *welcome* them. That’s how. Nobody becomes part of your home unless you welcome them.”

“Nobody?”

“Nobody.” She was already moving on to something else, because it was the most obvious statement she had made all morning and she had made it without any weight at all. “Now. Have you seen what Coll’s done with his weed?”

---

Then the water changed.

I did not know what was happening. The light went thin, and something came through the water that I felt rather than heard, a long low pull, and every arm on that bank came in at once.

“Bad water,” Marr said. “Small one. Won’t come to anything.”

Silt started to lift off the floor in slow curls. The light went brown. The loose ones stopped playing and scattered — not in a panic, just quickly, the way you get out of the rain.

Three of them shot past me toward Marr, and Marr’s door swung open, and they went in.

Two more went into Coll.

One went into the shell beyond him.

I watched Nit hesitate, decide it had time, and then not have time, and vanish into a shell it clearly did not know very well.

Then it was quiet, and the silt turned over, and it went on for a while.

When it cleared, the doors opened, and they all came out again, and one of them was complaining loudly about the smell of somebody else’s home.

Marr was still talking to me. She had not stopped talking, even during. She flicked a dismissive arm at the small ones streaming back out into the water.

“They always come out,” she said. “You can’t keep them anywhere.”

I went back out to the edge that evening.

She was there. Of course she was there. That is what I kept failing to understand at first — that when I decided to go and see her, I was deciding for both of us, because she had no way of not being at home.

“I said I’d come back.”

“You did.”

“I’m back.”

“I can see that.”

She was working at a piece of weed with one arm. The other was tucked. She did not stop.

“You didn’t answer my question.”

“No.”

“I asked whether your door was —”

“I know what you asked.”

“Are you going to answer it?”

“No,” she said.

She went on with the weed.

I stayed a while. She did not tell me to go, exactly, and I have wondered since whether that was the beginning of it.

Then she said, “Go on, then,” and I went.

I came back the next day.

## Chapter 7: There's You

Her name was Rell. It took four days to get that.

I got it because I asked Marr, not because she told me.

“Rell,” Marr said, and there was something in the way she said it. “You’ve been going out there?”

“Yes.”

“Well.” A pause. “She’s all right. She’s just — she doesn’t. You know.”

“I don’t.”

“She doesn’t *want* anything,” Marr said, and she meant it as a criticism, and I could not see why.

I went out to the edge every day for eleven days and mostly she did not talk to me.

That was all right. I did not go out there to be talked to. I went because it was the only part of the bank I did not understand, and because she was the only creature on it who had not immediately told me I would settle.

The eleventh day she said, “You’re still here.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I thought about it.

“Nobody comes out here,” I said.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because there’s nothing out here.”

“There’s you.”

Rell did not say anything to that for long enough that I thought I had done the wrong thing again. Then she made a short sound with her arms, and went back to work, and I understood that I had not.

## Chapter 8: Nobody Within Reach

She was cutting weed off her own rim, which is work a Kelt should not have to do alone.

I learned that later. On the bank proper, your neighbors clean your back for you. Growth accumulates where a Kelt cannot reach, and if it accumulates far enough it starts to seal the shell, and the standard remedy is that somebody within arm’s reach does it for you every few weeks for your whole life.

Nobody was within arm’s reach of Rell.

She had a system. She had a tool — a bit of shell with an edge on it — and she had a way of hooking her arm back over herself that must have hurt, and she got maybe two thirds of what needed doing.

The other third had been there for years.

“I can do that,” I said.

“No.”

“I can reach it.”

“I said no.”

I let it go for four more days, and then a piece of the growth on her back came loose in the current and she could not get at it, and it hung there, and she worked at it for most of a morning and could not do it.

“Let me get that.”

She was quiet a long time.

“You’ll do it wrong,” she said.

“Then tell me how.”

She told me how. It took a long time and she was extremely particular. I did it wrong twice and she said so both times.

When it was done she said, “That’s better,” in a voice that suggested it cost her something, and then, immediately: “That doesn’t mean anything.”

“All right.”

“I mean it. You’re not — this isn’t —”

“All right,” I said again.

She went back to the weed.

## Chapter 9: Everything's Something

After that I carried things.

Not much. Nobody came out to the edge, which meant that anything Rell needed from the bank, she went without. She would not ask the loose ones. I worked out why eventually: asking meant one of them would have to come out here, and then they would talk about having come out here, and Rell had spent a very long time arranging her life so that nobody talked about her at all.

I was different because I was going to be out there anyway.

So I carried. Weed she could not grow at the edge. A shell of the good grit from the shallows. Once, a piece of tool-shell from Coll, who was surprised to be asked and did not ask why.

She kept it exact. Every single time.

“What do I owe for that?”

“Nothing.”

“Everything’s something.”

“I don’t need anything.”

“Then I’ll owe you,” she said, and it was not friendly. It was bookkeeping. She was making sure that whatever was between us stayed a thing she could put a number on.

---

She counted.

I had noticed the first day. Weed going past, in the afternoon, out loud, when she thought nobody was there. But it was not only weed. It was everything. She counted the shells she could see from where she was fixed, which was nine. She counted days of bad water in a season. She counted how many times a particular loose one had come within a hundred body-lengths of the edge that year, and the number was four, and she did not appear to like knowing it.

One afternoon she said “Eleven” out of nowhere, and I said “Eleven what,” and she said “Weed,” and I said “It was seven the first day I came,” and she stopped working.

“You remember that.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I remember all of them,” I said. “Seven. Nine. Nine. Four, there was bad water. Twelve. Six.”

Rell did not say anything for a while.

“That’s a stupid thing to do,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I do it too.”

“I know,” I said. “That’s how I knew to.”

“Does anybody else do it?” I said.

“No.”

“Have you asked?”

“I’m not going to ask,” Rell said. “It’s a stupid thing to do.”

Neither of us said anything else about it, then or ever. But she counted out loud the next afternoon while I was there, which she had not done since the first day, and she did not stop when she noticed.

## Chapter 10: Something Moved

That was the day I stayed late.

I did not plan it. The light went, and I was still out at the edge, and I had drifted maybe forty body-lengths off into the dark water past her shell where the floor drops.

I do not think she knew I was there. I think she believed I had gone in with the light, the way everything on that bank did.

The dark out past the edge is total. There is nothing to see and nothing coming, and the cold comes up off the drop, and I understood something about her position that I had not understood in daylight: she spent every night of her life fixed at the border of that.

Then, behind me, I heard her door open.

I turned around.

It was open wide. Much wider than I had seen it — wider than I would have said that narrow slot could go, and the effort of it was audible; she was making a sound I had not heard her make.

There was light inside. Not much. A dim warmth, the color of nothing in particular.

And in the light, in the opening, something moved.

It was small. It was pale. It had a great many little limbs, and it came up to the gap from the inside and stayed there, not coming out, and one of Rell’s arms came over and rested against the rim beside it.

It did not made a sound.

I did not move at all.

It stayed like that for a long time — the open door, the small pale thing in the gap, the arm beside it — and then the shape went back down into the dim warmth and was gone, and the door came shut, slowly, and with difficulty.

I stayed out in the dark water for a long time after that.

I had spent two weeks being told that once you welcome someone into your home, they stay part of it forever. I had watched Sennet’s mother say the welcome out loud in front of forty people so that everyone would know it had happened.

There had been no saying for that.

There was nobody on this bank who did not know everything about everybody, and nobody had ever said one word about anyone staying in Rell’s home.

## Chapter 11: Get Away

She knew, the next morning.

“You were out there.”

I could have said no.

“Yes.”

“All night.”

“Most of it.”

“You were watching me.”

“Yes.”

She did not shout. I thought she might. What she did was worse and took less time.

“Get away from my shell,” she said.

I went.

She shut flat for six days.

I went out twice more. Both times there was a shell on the floor with the arms pulled in and no voice coming out of it.

The second time I said, “You can’t go anywhere.”

She did not answer, which was the point. She could not leave. She could not turn her back. She could not walk out of a room.

She had one way of refusing anybody, and I was standing in front of it.

## Chapter 12: Find Out

So I did what I do, which is find out.

I was careful about it, which did not help.

I asked Nit who was part of whose home.

“Course they are. Everyone’s part of somebody’s home.”

“Who’s part of Marr’s home?”

“Two of her own. A cousin, I think. Somebody from before she set, but nobody remembers who.” Nit reeled it off. “Ask her, she’ll tell you for an hour.”

“How do you know?”

“How do I know?” Nit did a loop. “Everyone knows. It’s *said*. That’s the whole point of saying it.”

“What if somebody stayed in a home after bad water and nobody invited them to stay?”

“They wouldn’t.”

“But what if they did?”

“Then somebody kept them there,” Nit said, patiently, the way you explain a thing to someone slow. “People don’t just stay.”

I tried a different way.

“Is there anything written down?”

“Anything *what*?”

That took a while, and when I finally got the idea across Nit thought it was hilarious and made me explain it twice so it could laugh again.

“What for?” it said. “We remember.”

Then it told me, at length and without being asked, about a dispute two shells over concerning whether a particular patch of weed belonged to the shell it grew nearest or the shell whose arms could actually reach it. This had been going for eleven years. Both parties had stopped speaking to each other and now conducted the entire argument through whichever loose one was passing, which meant that Nit had personally carried roughly four hundred insults across a gap of two body-lengths.

“Why don’t they just talk?” I said.

“Because then it’d be over,” Nit said, shocked. “They’ve been doing it eleven years.”

---

I asked Marr the other question, sideways.

“What happens if somebody stays behind a door and nobody invited them to stay?”

Marr’s arms went still, which they almost never did.

“Why?”

“I want to know how it works.”

“That doesn’t happen.”

“But if it did.”

“It doesn’t.” She started working again, faster. “If somebody is still in your home after the water clears, somebody kept them there. If nobody kept them, they come back out. There isn’t a third thing.”

“But if there were.”

Marr put her arm down.

“Then somebody kept somebody there and hid it,” she said. “That’s what that would be. And there’s only one reason to hide a thing like that.” She looked out toward the edge, and then she looked away from the edge, deliberately, in a way I did not understand for another two weeks. “Don’t go asking that where people can hear you.”

“Why?”

“Because of what they’d think it meant.”

## Chapter 13: The Asking Is the Thing

It took me until evening to get all the way around that, and I got there by trying to do it.

I worked out what I would actually say. I would go to Vesh, and I would say: *Rell has never invited anyone to stay in her home.*

And then I said it to myself again and heard it.

Because Vesh would have to check. That is what a keeper is. And to check he would have to ask somebody — Marr, say, two shells along — whether she had ever heard anything about Rell welcoming somebody into her home.

And Marr would say no.

And then Marr would spend the rest of her life knowing that the keeper had once come and asked her that.

I tried it four or five different ways after that. I tried a version where nobody names Rell. I tried a version where you ask about every shell on the bank so that hers is only one of them. I tried a version where you ask the loose ones instead, because nobody listens to the loose ones.

Every single one of them ended with somebody who had never thought about Rell’s door in their life going home thinking about it.

There is nothing written down on that bank. There is no place you can go and look. The only way to establish that a thing did not happen is to get everybody to agree it did not — and the only way to start that is to say the thing out loud in front of them.

She had worked that out twenty years ago, alone, out at the cold edge where nobody came, and she had been the only one holding it ever since.

## Chapter 14: Bad Water

The storm came four days later.

It did not come like the small one. The light went out of the water in the middle of the day, and the long pull came through and kept coming, and the whole bank started shouting.

I do not know much about weather. What I know is what I saw: the silt came up in sheets, and it did not settle, and it began to move along the floor in the direction of the drop.

Toward the edge.

The loose ones went into doors all over the bank, and the doors shut, and the middle of the bank turned into a row of sealed shells and I could not see a living thing.

I found Nit already halfway through somebody’s door.

“Get in!” it shouted at me. “Coll’ll have you, he doesn’t care —”

“Rell’s out at the edge.”

“So?”

“The silt’s going that way.”

“So she’s low,” Nit said, “and she’ll be all right, and *get in* —”

“Will she?”

Nit did not answer.

That was an answer.

“Come with me.”

“I’m not going out there in this!” And then, in a completely different voice, young and frightened and honest: “I *can’t*. I’d get taken over the drop. I’m not big enough.”

“All right,” I said.

“Don’t —”

I went.

---

I will not pretend the storm was interesting. It was not. It was the same thing over and over for a very long time, and that is the whole of what it was.

Her shell sits low. That is what makes the edge a poor position: the floor tilts, and everything the water carries ends up banked against whatever is furthest out, and what is furthest out is Rell.

By the time I got there the silt was already up over her upstream rim and building against the side of her shell, and it was coming faster than she could clear it. She had both arms working. She had been working, I found out later, since the light went.

She did not say anything to me. She did not stop working.

So I started on the upstream side, where her arms could not reach around, and I took what I could move and put it downstream, and went back and took more.

That is all it was. There is nothing else to tell about it. I did that for the whole night. I do not know how many times. I stopped counting somewhere in the middle, which for me is unusual, and I noticed that I had stopped and did not start again.

Twice the silt came up faster than I could take it away and got as far as the rim of her door, and both times I got it off, and neither of us said anything about what it would have meant if I had not.

Near morning the pull went out of the water and the silt began to settle instead of move.

I kept going until her upstream side was clear.

Then I stopped, because there was nothing left to move, and floated there, and discovered that I did not seem to be able to go anywhere else.

Rell’s arms came down.

Neither of us spoke for a long time.

“You can stay,” she said, “until the water’s right.”

## Chapter 15: Seven

The water was right by midday and I did not go.

She did not tell me to.

She did tell me that I had put the silt in the wrong place. All of it. Downstream was correct, she said, but I had gone downstream and slightly *in*, which meant a third of it would come back around on the next bad water, and if I was going to do a job I might as well do it so it stayed done.

“You watched me do that all night,” I said.

“I was busy.”

“You watched me do it about four hundred times.”

“I lost count,” Rell said, which was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and she went back to her weed.

Around the middle of the afternoon she said, “Seven,” and I said, “It’s the same as the first day,” and she said, “I know,” and that was the whole conversation, and it was the best one we had had.

Then, much later, with no warning at all:

“There’s somebody in my home.”

I did not say anything. I have got better at that.

“I didn’t welcome them,” she said. “I never invited them to stay. I never said the words. There was no welcome. Nothing.” Her arms were still. “They’ve been there twenty years.”

“What is it?”

“No.”

“Do you know what it —”

“*No.*” The rim shivered. “I’ve told you what I’m going to tell you. It’s in there. I didn’t put it there. Don’t ask me anything else.”

“All right.”

“I mean it.”

“All right,” I said. “Can I ask something that isn’t that?”

A pause.

“Try.”

“Why haven’t you told the keeper?”

Rell made a sound I had not heard before. It was almost a laugh and it was not a good one.

“Because of what happens next,” she said.

“He’d help.”

“He’d *ask*.” She turned it over, patiently, as if she had done it ten thousand times, which she had. “Vesh is decent. He’d believe me, probably. But he can’t do anything quietly, because there isn’t a quiet. Everything he does, he does by saying it out loud where the bank can hear.”

“So he says it out loud. You didn’t do anything.”

“And what have they heard?” she said. “They’ve heard the keeper ask Rell at the edge whether there’s someone in her home who was never invited to stay. That’s what they’ve heard. That’s all they’ll ever have heard, from then until I die.” Her arms came in. “It doesn’t matter what he says after. There’s no *after*. The asking is the thing.”

I thought about that for a long time.

And then I made the worst mistake I have ever made.

## Chapter 16: The Worst Mistake

I was not being careless. I was being clever, and it was worse.

On the last world I went to, everybody obeyed a rule that turned out not to be part of the world at all. It was an agreement. They had been making it every day for so long that they had forgotten they were making it, and when one creature finally did the thing everyone said could not be done, nothing happened.

I had that. I was proud of it, in whatever way I am able to be proud of things.

So when Rell said the asking was the thing, I heard a rule made of people.

Then the people can make it differently, I thought, and went to find Vesh.

---

Vesh was in the middle of the bank, where everyone could reach him, which is why he was the keeper.

He was very old. His shell had more growing on it than shell.

His door opened twice while I was waiting — once to take a thing in, once to let a loose one out — and a third time, while I was actually talking to him, for a small one who put its head in, said something I did not catch, and got told to come back later.

“Sorry,” Vesh said. “It’s like this all day.”

I told him there was a question about a door.

“Whose?”

I did not answer.

He waited. He was extremely good at waiting; better than me, and I am the best waiter I know.

“There’s a home on this bank,” I said, “with somebody in it who was never invited to stay. And it isn’t the owner’s fault. And I want to know how you would find that out without ruining her.”

Vesh was quiet a long moment.

“You’ve told me it’s a her,” he said.

I had.

“And there are four set Kelt on this bank I’d worry about,” he said, “and only one of them lives where nobody has been for twenty years.”

“I didn’t say —”

“No,” he agreed. “You didn’t.”

He was not angry. That was the thing I could not get past afterward. He was careful and slow and he was trying, genuinely, to do the least harmful thing available to him, and I watched him work out that there wasn’t one.

“You want me to ask a question in a way that nobody hears,” he said. “I can’t. I haven’t got anything to do it with. I’ve got my voice and I’ve got where I’m sitting.” He moved an arm, taking in the shells all around him, close enough to touch, which was exactly why he was keeper and exactly the problem. “Everything I say, twelve people hear. That’s what I’m *for*.”

“Then don’t say anything.”

“You’ve said it,” Vesh said, gently. “That’s already happened.”

## Chapter 17: Talk Starting

He did it as well as anybody could have.

He said, in front of the bank, that there was talk starting and he wanted it stopped before it got going. He said Rell at the edge had done nothing and been accused of nothing. He said anyone repeating otherwise would answer to him.

He named her in order to defend her. He had no choice; you cannot defend somebody without saying who.

And I watched it go out across the shells the way Sennet’s welcome had gone out, shell to shell down the bank, and by the time it reached the far end it was not what Vesh had said any more.

I heard four versions before evening.

Marr’s arms were going the whole time and Marr was not being cruel. She was frightened. She said, over and over, that of course Rell hadn’t, of course she hadn’t, and every single time she said it she put the thing she was denying into somebody else’s head.

Frightened decent people did all of it. Nobody on that bank meant any harm at all.

---

I went out to the edge at dusk.

I do not know what I was going to say.

Her arms were in. Her door was shut flat. There was a shell on the floor at the cold edge of the world with nobody living in it as far as anyone passing could tell.

“Rell.”

Nothing.

“Rell, I —”

Nothing.

I stayed out there for a long time, and the light went, and the cold came up off the drop, and she did not make one sound, and I understood that this was not the sulking of somebody who would be finished with it in the morning.

I had done the exact thing. Not something like it. The precise thing she had spent twenty years preventing, I had done in an afternoon, because I was sure I knew how rules worked.

## Chapter 18: Nine Days

She did not open for nine days.

I counted. I stayed out at the edge and counted, because it was the only thing I had.

The bank talked. It talked itself out, the way talk does, and by the sixth day it had mostly moved on to a dispute about weed rights near the shallows, which everyone found more interesting because both parties were shouting.

But it did not go away entirely. It went into the ground. Twice I heard a loose one, repeating something it did not understand, in the specific singsong the young use for things they have overheard: *someone in Rell’s home, someone in Rell’s home.*

Nit came out to the edge to see me, which took nerve, and stayed a hundred body-lengths off, which was honest.

“Is it true?”

“Which part?”

“Any of it.”

“I don’t know what they’re saying.”

“That she’s been keeping somebody in her home for twenty years.” Nit’s limbs were going in a way I had not seen. “That’s — you can’t. You can’t do that.”

“Why not?”

“Because then how would anybody *know* anything?”

“Know what?”

“Anything!” Nit’s limbs went all at once. “Who’s part of whose home. Who welcomed who. All of it. If somebody can stay behind a door and nobody welcomed them —” It stopped, and started again somewhere else, which I had never seen Nit do. “Then it’s all just — nothing. It’s all just people talking.”

It went off very fast after that, and did not come back for two days.

Nit had got to the middle of it without meaning to, which is the only way anyone gets to the middle of anything.

## Chapter 19: Every Door

So I did the only thing left, which was look at doors.

I had nine days and nothing to do.

I went along the bank and I looked at every set Kelt on it, and I counted, because that is what I have instead of standing.

Here is what I found.

Coll had a wide door. Coll had four shells within arm’s reach and something going in or out of him most of the day.

Marr’s was wider. Marr talked to everyone and everyone talked to Marr, and her door opened perhaps thirty times between light and dark, mostly for nothing — a thing handed in, a thing handed out, a loose one going through on the way to somewhere else.

The two out past the weed beds — a pair who had settled a body-length apart when they were young and had spent forty years within reach of each other and could never, ever be guests in one another’s homes, because neither could move — both had wide, easy doors, and they used them constantly, for nothing, all day, back and forth, because it was the only thing they had.

I watched them for most of an afternoon. They passed the same shell of grit between them eleven times. There was no reason for it to go anywhere. Somewhere around the seventh time I worked out that the grit was not the point, and I stayed anyway.

One of them noticed me eventually and asked whether I was going to settle.

“No,” I said.

“Where would you put yourself if you did?”

I thought about it, because it was a serious question and she had asked it seriously.

“I don’t know.”

“Well,” she said, “that’s the whole business, isn’t it,” and passed the grit back over.

The old female up near the shallows: narrow. Nobody much goes up there. The weed is poor and there is nothing past her, so there is no reason to walk that way unless she is the reason, and she has not been the reason for a long time.

I stopped and talked to her for a while, because I was passing and because nobody else was. She told me that in her day a thing my size would have been given something to carry, and then she asked me to say it all again because she had not been listening the first time.

I stayed most of the morning.

Her door opened once while I was there, for nothing much, and it took her a while, and she made a small sound doing it that I did not think about until four days later.

The one past Coll who had outlived his brood was the same. Narrow, and slow with it, and nobody near him.

And Vesh. Vesh, who was older than any of them — old enough that his shell was more weed than shell — Vesh, who was reached by every creature on that bank every single day.

Vesh’s door still worked.

## Chapter 20: A Door Narrows

I floated over the middle of the bank and put it together.

They all told me that doors narrow with age. Every one of them had said it. It was one of those things everybody knows.

And it was not age.

Or it was not only age. Vesh was the oldest thing on that floor and his door opened twice while I was there. The narrow ones were not the old ones. The narrow ones were the ones nobody came to.

**A door narrows when it is not opened.**

They had never noticed, because on a bank where the shells sit close and everybody is reached every day, there is nothing to notice. You would need a Kelt who had gone twenty-six years almost without opening. You would need to put somebody out at the cold edge where nobody comes and leave them there for most of their life, and nobody had ever done that on purpose, and nobody had ever thought to look.

Rell was the one experiment nobody had run.

---

And then, because I had the shape of it, the rest arrived — and it did not arrive as new information. It arrived as something I had already been told.

Bad water, my second day on that world. The light going brown. The loose ones scattering off the open floor and going into the nearest doors, three into Marr, two into Coll, Nit into a shell it barely knew.

Nobody had welcomed any of them. Nobody had invited them to stay. There had been no witnesses, no words. They had just got out of the weather, the way you stand in a doorway out of the rain.

And Marr, not even watching them come back out, flicking an arm at them:

*They always come out.*

I went and found Nit.

“When you shelter. In bad water.”

“What about it?”

“Does everyone come out after?”

Nit stared at me.

“What?”

“Afterward. Everyone who went in. Does everyone come out?”

“Course they do.”

“How do you know?”

“Because —” Nit stopped. It actually stopped, which I had never seen. “Because they do. You go in, water goes off, you come out. That’s what it *is*.”

“Does anybody count?”

“Count what?”

“How many went in. How many came out.”

There was a very long pause, and Nit’s limbs slowed right down, and I watched a young creature arrive at the edge of a thought and decline to go over it.

“Why would anybody count that?” it said.

## Chapter 21: Nobody Counts

Twenty years ago there was a storm.

Rell had been at the edge six years by then, opening for almost nobody, and her door had been quietly getting smaller the entire time, and not one creature on that bank — including her — had any idea that was a thing that could happen.

A loose Kelt got caught in bad water out past the last of the shells, and did what every loose Kelt on Sull does, and went into the nearest open door.

And when the water settled, it could not get back out.

There would have been no fuss. Nobody counts. The whole bank was shut up in its own shells with the silt going over, and the loose scattered all over everywhere, and afterward everyone came out and shook themselves off and went back to what they were doing.

I stayed outside her shut door and understood the entire thing, and there was nothing I could do with it.

Because I am a small thing with no door, that everybody on that bank believed would settle down one day and grow up. Nit was the only creature there who thought of me as a person, and Nit was eleven.

If I swam into the middle of the bank and announced that doors narrow from being alone and that a child had been trapped in Rell’s home for twenty years by an accident of weather, they would have listened to it exactly as much as they listen to any loose one shouting something across open water.

There was one creature on Sull who could say it and be believed.

She had not spoken to me in nine days.

## Chapter 22: The Closed Door

On the tenth day I went out to the edge and said it to a closed door.

“I know what it is.”

Nothing.

“I know how it got in. I know why it can’t get out. I worked it out four days ago and I’ve been sitting out here since, because I can’t tell anybody, because nobody would believe me. Only you can say it.”

Nothing.

“I know I did the thing you told me not to do.”

The water moved over the drop and off the shallows and the light went down the way it does.

“I’ll come back tomorrow,” I said.

I came back for four days.

On the fourth day the rim moved.

It was not much. It came up off flat, a fraction, the way it did when she was listening.

“Say it, then,” Rell said. Her voice was terrible. “Since you know so much.”

So I said it. All of it, in order, the way I had worked it out — the doors along the bank and the wide ones and the narrow ones and Vesh being ancient and still working. The narrowing. Then the storm, and the sheltering, and Marr flicking her arm at the loose ones streaming back out into the water. *They always come out.*

And nobody counts.

I got to the end and stopped.

Rell did not say anything for a very long time.

When she did, her voice had changed completely. It was flat and it was small and it was twenty years old.

“I thought it was me,” she said.

“I know.”

“I thought I’d done something. All this time. I thought there was something wrong with me and that was why it —” Her arms moved and stopped. “You’re telling me it’s because nobody came.”

“Yes.”

“That’s worse.”

I had not thought of that.

She was right, and I did not have anything to say.

## Chapter 23: Trust

“I want to go in,” I said.

“No.”

“Rell —”

“*No.*”

“Why not?”

“Because —” and she stopped, and started again in a different place. “Because you’d have to come back out.”

There it was. The whole thing, in one sentence, from the only creature who had actually thought about it for twenty years.

“I know,” I said.

“Do you? It’s narrower now than it was then. It’s been narrowing the whole time. If it’s shut too far for what’s in there to get out —” Her arms came in. “You go in and I can’t get you out again. I can’t. There’s nothing I could do. You’d be in there with it.”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you asking?”

“Because somebody has to know who it is,” I said. “And it’s going to be me.”

“Any of them could fit through. Any of those little ones out there could fit through a gap this size, and half of them are smaller than you —”

“Would you open for them?”

Silence.

“No,” said Rell.

“That’s why.”

I waited.

“There’s one more reason,” I said. “If a loose one goes in there and comes out, the whole bank knows by dark. They can’t help it. It’s what they’re for.” I moved a little closer to the rim. “There’s nobody I’d tell. There’s nobody who’d believe me if I did. I’m the only creature on this bank that can go in there and have it still be yours afterward.”

The water went over us for a while.

“You’d be trusting me to open again,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You’ve got no reason to.”

“No.”

“Everybody I ever knew is somewhere else,” Rell said, “and I have been alone with this thing for twenty years, and you’re the one that made the whole bank say my name.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ll still go in.”

“Yes.”

Her door opened.

It hurt her. I could hear it. It came up off the rim and stopped, and she made a sound, and it came further, and she made the sound again, and it stopped at a gap I could get through and no more, and I did not wait, because waiting would have made her hold it.

I went in.

## Chapter 24: The Room

It was a room. That is all it was.

It was warm, and dim, and the air — it was not air, but it did what air does — was completely still, and it smelled the way a place smells when it has been the same temperature for a very long time.

The floor was worn in places. Not much. Two or three paths, going nowhere, the shine you get on stone from something crossing it over and over.

I could hear the outside. Muffled and constant, the way you hear weather through a wall: the water moving, the drag off the drop, the sound of the shallows a long way off. A creature in here would have heard everything on that bank for twenty years and been able to reach none of it.

And there were marks. All around, in the walls and the floor, changes in the surface that were not damage and not decoration — the places where somebody who had been welcomed into this home a long time ago had left the room different. I did not know how to read them. Rell would. She would know every one.

That is what they meant when they said somebody was still part of a home even after they had gone. It is a room, and the room is changed, and it cannot be changed back.

Then something moved in the corner.

## Chapter 25: Twenty Years

It had made a place there.

That is what I could not get past. Not that it was there — I knew it was there, I had come in specifically to find it there. What I had not prepared for was that the corner had been arranged. There were bits and pieces set out along the wall. Small things. A curl of shell. Something that had been a piece of weed a very long time ago. Objects that had come in with it, or come loose off the walls, laid in a line the way you lay out things that are yours.

Somebody had made a home out of a place that was never supposed to be one.

It came toward me across the worn floor and stopped a little way off.

It was small and pale and soft, and it had a great many limbs, and it was loose. Completely loose. No shell, no door, nothing. The same as Nit. The same as every child out on that bank.

Twenty years.

Its whole brood had settled and grown old and some of them had died, and this one had never grown a door at all, because you do not grow a door until you settle, and you cannot settle in a room.

“Hello,” I said.

It did not move.

“Can you understand me?”

Its limbs went, slowly, in a way I recognized from Nit and could not read.

“Are you all right?” I said, which was a stupid question, and I knew it while I was saying it.

It came a little closer.

And then it spoke, and its voice was perfectly ordinary and perfectly calm, and it asked me the only thing it had wanted to know for twenty years.

“Is the water gone?”

## Chapter 26: Not Yet

I did not know how to answer it.

I understood, standing on that worn floor in the dark, that it had no idea. Not roughly, not approximately — none. There is no light in there. No tide, no weather, nobody arriving, nothing changing at all. There is nothing to count. I count everything and I could not have told you how long I had been inside that room, and I had been inside it for less than the time it takes to say so.

It knew a long time had passed. It had stopped being able to say how long, somewhere in there, years ago, and it had let it go.

“Yes,” I said. “The water’s gone.”

“Then I can go out.”

“Not yet.”

Its limbs stopped.

“Why not?”

“The way out is too small,” I said.

There was a long pause in the warm dark.

“I know,” it said.

I do not know how long I stayed. Longer than I meant to.

I asked it questions and it answered most of them, and I will not put all of that down here, because some of it was not mine.

It asked me about the bank. It asked whether Coll was still there, and I said yes, and it seemed pleased, and I did not have the heart to say that Coll was very old now.

It asked whether the shells near the yellow weed were taken.

I said I thought somebody had picked that spot.

“That was going to be mine,” it said.

## Chapter 27: Sef

Getting out was worse than getting in.

She opened as far as she could and I could hear what it cost, and the gap was not as big as it had been, and I did not get through the first time.

The second time I did not think I was going to get through. There was a moment — and it was not long — where I was in the gap and not moving in either direction, with the rim against me on both sides, and I understood that this was how it happens. Not dramatically. You are just on the wrong side of something that is closing.

Then I was out in the cold water at the edge of the world, and her door came down flat behind me, and both of us made a noise.

Neither of us said anything for a long time.

“It’s your brood,” I said, when I could. “Isn’t it.”

“Sef,” said Rell.

That was all she said, and it was the first time in twenty years that anyone but her had known the name, and she said it out loud into open water.

“You’ve known the whole time.”

“Of course I’ve known the whole time.” Her arms were shaking. “She came in out of the water. I had my door open because I always had my door open, in case any of them ever —” She stopped. “It was six years and nobody had come out here. Nobody. And then there was bad water and something came in my door and it was *her*.”

“And then she couldn’t get out.”

“And then she couldn’t get out, and I couldn’t tell anybody what had happened, because what happened is not a thing that happens.” Her voice went. “So they’d have said the other thing. That I welcomed my brood-sister in secret. That I *kept* her.”

“That isn’t what happened.”

“It’s what there is,” Rell said. “There isn’t anything else to think.”

She was quiet for a long while after that. The light was going.

“You saw her,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You saw her and you came back out and you’re still here.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody else has ever seen her,” Rell said.

And that was it. That was the whole of what changed. I had not fixed anything and I had not got Sef out and I had no standing to say a word to anybody about any of it.

But there were two of us now who had seen it, and there had only ever been one, and it turns out that is not a small difference. It is most of the difference there is.

“I’ll say it,” Rell said.

“You don’t have to.”

“Yes I do.” Her arms came down. “I’ve been not saying it for twenty years and it hasn’t got her out.”

## Chapter 28: Say It Out Loud

She said it four days later, in front of the bank, which meant in front of everyone, because on Sull there is no other way to say anything.

Vesh arranged it as well as it could be arranged. He put it in the middle of the day and he asked the loose ones to carry word to the far shells so that nobody could say afterward that they had heard it secondhand, and he told them all to be quiet and let her talk, and mostly they were.

She could not go to them. That is the part I had not thought about until the morning of it. She is fixed at the edge and everyone else is fixed where they are, so what actually happened is that half the bank’s loose ones came out and hung in the water around her shell, and the rest of it was carried inward, shell to shell, all afternoon.

She had to say it to children and let them repeat it.

She said it plainly. She said there had been a storm twenty years ago and her brood-sister Sef had come through her door out of the bad water like any loose one does, and that when it cleared she could not get out again. Rell had never welcomed Sef into her home. Sef had sheltered there. She had never been invited to stay.

She said she had told nobody because she knew exactly what they would think.

Then she said the last part, which was mine, and which she had made me say twice more before she would take it: that a door narrows when it is not opened, and that hers had been narrowing for six years before the storm because nobody had come out to the edge, and that this was why the gap had closed.

It did not go well.

I want to be honest about it. Somebody said, not quietly, that this was a very convenient story. Somebody asked why she had never mentioned it before, which was the entire point and which she had just spent a long time explaining. Somebody wanted to know how anybody could check.

Nobody could check. That was true. There was nothing written down, because there is never anything written down.

Marr was there and Marr said nothing at all for a long time, and I watched her arms and I could see her deciding, and it took her longer than it should have.

Then Marr said, “I’ve known her twenty-six years,” which was not evidence of anything, and it was the thing that turned it.

---

Vesh did what a keeper does, which is say a thing out loud in a place where everyone can hear it.

He said that Rell of the edge had welcomed no one in secret and kept no hidden guest.

He said that a door can narrow from being left alone, and that this was new, and that he was old and had never heard it before and did not much like learning it at his age.

And then he said the part I did not expect, which was that he had known for years that something was wrong out at the edge and had said nothing, because he had believed that asking would be worse than waiting.

“I was wrong about that,” he said. “I waited six years. Then I waited fourteen more.”

Nobody said anything to that.

He is very old and he had just told an entire bank that the way they do things had cost somebody twenty years, and that he had helped by being careful.

## Chapter 29: They Came Back

Nothing was passed. No new rule. There was nobody to write anything down and nothing to write it on.

What happened instead was that the loose ones started coming out to the edge.

At first because Vesh asked, and a keeper’s asking is most of what a keeper has. Then because it was interesting, and children go where the talk is. Then, eventually, because that was where you went.

They brought her weed and grit and news, most of it wrong. Somebody’s mother sent out a thing Rell did not need and would not have been sent a year earlier, and Rell said she owed for it, and was told not to be ridiculous, and was annoyed about that for a week.

It was not all kindness. Part of it was fear, and the fear was reasonable: every set Kelt on that bank had just learned that a door can close on you if nobody comes, and every one of them knew somebody out toward the edges who nobody came to. Two shells up from Marr there was an old male who had outlived his brood and whose door was a slot. People started going to him too. Nobody decided to.

But the coming was not what did it.

They came out to the edge, and Rell could have stayed shut.

The habit was twenty-six years deep and it had kept her safe the entire time, and there is nothing in the world harder than opening to people who have just spent two weeks discussing you.

She opened.

Badly, the first times. Not far, and not for long, and not for everyone. There was one afternoon early on when three loose ones came out together and she could not do it and told them to go away, and they went, and she did not speak for the rest of the day.

They came back the next day. That is the whole of what the bank had to offer and it turned out to be enough, because it was the one thing she had never had.

She opened again. And again.

And because she opened it, it widened.

Slowly. Over weeks. There was nothing to see from one day to the next and I would not have believed it at all except that I count things and the numbers went the right way.

I stayed for all of it, which surprised me. There was nothing left to find out.

I think I wanted to see it.

## Chapter 30: The Water Is Gone

Sef came out on a gray afternoon with nothing else happening.

There was no crowd. Rell would not have it; she said the whole business had been public enough for one lifetime, and she was not going to have her sister come out of a door in front of an audience after twenty years in the dark.

So there was Rell, and there was me, and there was Nit at a distance, because Nit had appointed itself to this and could not be got rid of.

The door came up. It went further than I had ever seen it go, and it kept going, and it did not stop where it had stopped before.

Sef came out into the water.

She stayed very close to the shell for a moment, which I understood. There is a great deal of everything out there.

Then she went up a little, into the open, and turned slowly all the way around and looked at the bank — at the shells going away into the distance, at the light, at all of it, twenty years of it, and at her sister, who had been young when she went in.

“Is the water gone?” she said.

I did not stay for the rest.

I left two days after that.

I went round the bank first, which took most of a morning. Coll wanted to know whether I had settled yet. Marr asked me three questions about myself and then talked for twenty minutes. Vesh said something I did not understand at the time and have thought about since, which was: “You did more harm than anybody here, and more good. I hope you know both of those.”

“I know both of those,” I said.

“Good,” said Vesh. “Some don’t.”

## Chapter 31: The Other Thing

Nit came with me to the outer edge, which is as far as a loose one will go.

“You’re going, then.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“You never know anything,” Nit said. It did a loop, and stopped, and did not do another one. “I’m settling. Two weeks. By the yellow weed.”

“I know. You told me.”

“I told you about eight times.”

“Eleven,” I said.

Nit made the clicking laugh, and then stopped making it.

“You’re really not going to,” it said. “Are you. Settle.”

“No.”

“Ever?”

“I don’t think so.”

Nit thought about that for a while. It is the longest I ever saw it think about anything.

“That’s the saddest thing I’ve ever heard,” it said.

I have carried that a long way. At the time I thought Nit had misunderstood.

---

Rell was at the edge, where she is.

“I’m going.”

“I know. Marr’s told everyone.”

“Marr would.”

Her arms were working at the weed. Her door was cracked, which by then was ordinary and which four months earlier would not have been possible.

“You’ll be all right,” I said, and immediately understood that it was the wrong kind of thing to say, and that I had been on this world for months and still could not do that part.

“I’ve been all right,” Rell said. “That was the trouble.”

I did not have an answer.

“Come here,” she said.

I went closer.

“Nearer than that.”

I went right up to the rim of her shell, close enough to touch, which is a thing you do not do to a set Kelt without being asked, and which I had never once done in all those months.

“I’m not going to welcome you into my home,” Rell said. “You’d hate it. You’d be part of this place forever, and you’d never shut up about it.”

“That’s true.”

“So this is the other thing.” She put one arm along the top of her shell, next to me, not touching. “There. That’s all there is. Somebody sat next to somebody.”

We stayed like that for a while.

“Sef says the yellow weed is taken,” she said.

“It is.”

“She’ll find somewhere.”

“Yes.”

“She’s got time,” Rell said, and that was the first thing anybody on that world had said that I could not measure, and I did not try.

## Chapter 32: The Near Side

I went up.

The shells went small below me, spread out over the pale floor, the wide-doored ones close together in the middle and the narrow-doored ones further out with the water going over them. From high enough they were just stones in a field.

I could see the loose ones. Threads of them, going everywhere, carrying things.

I could see one thread going out toward the edge, where a year ago there had been nothing going at all.

Then the water went white above me and the bank was gone, and I was in the place I go between, where the sky is black and there is a white line and a shape that opens.

I stopped there, the way I do now.

They were right about one thing: **be careful who you welcome into your home.** Once you do, they stay part of it. Everyone on that bank had that part exactly right, and it had never once failed them.

What they had put on top of it was the part nobody looked at. *So anyone who stayed behind your door must have been invited to stay.*

Nobody said that one out loud. You do not say the part everybody already knows. It just sat there underneath everything, being obvious, for as long as anyone could remember, and it cost Sef twenty years.

What I had at the time was smaller than that.

I had wanted her to open. Not so I could find anything out — I had found it all out already, and it had not been worth anything. I wanted her to open because it was me.

I still don’t know what to do with that.

I went forward toward the shape.

And in the dark, at the near side of it, so faint I would have gone straight past it a year ago, something moved the way a thing moves when it has been waiting.

It did not come closer.

It knew me.
