SEER WORLDS: OPEN ROAD

# Part I: Lines Everyone Knows

## Chapter 1: An Edge Without a Wall

I arrived above a road the color of yellow fruit. That was the first fact I had. The second was that the road had been cut into the side of something much larger than a mountain.

Look up from where I was, and there were pale shelves one above another until distance pressed them into a single line. Look down, and the same shelves continued into a dim place where I could no longer tell stone from shadow. Homes opened from the white cliff. Gardens hung from frames below the road, their vines dangling over a fall so deep that I could not find its end. Slow gray bands moved behind the silver sky.

I was glad to be there. I usually am, at first.

I moved along the road without touching it. Blue leaves turned on a tree ahead of me, and somewhere beyond the bend, little bells rang among many voices. I wanted to see the tree from underneath and find the source of the bells. Those were not large wants, but I did not have any others.

“Stop.” The voice came from close behind me. “Back up.”

I stopped, then turned. A four-legged creature stood with a fruit basket between her front feet. Brown fur covered her except for a cream stripe along the underside of her long neck. Two flat fins lay against the fur at the back of her head, and both had turned toward me. When I asked how far I should back up, her mouth stayed open after the answer should have come.

“Just come here.”

I moved until I floated beside her basket. One round yellow fruit had escaped and rested near the edge of the road. I could have reached it sooner than she could, if reaching had been something I did. I had no hands or feet. I was a dark round shape, small enough to fit inside her basket, and movement was one of the few things about myself I understood.

She hooked the fruit toward her with one foot. “You nearly crossed Venn's stretch.”

The yellow road continued past the blue tree. Wind moved its leaves, but nothing marked a stretch belonging to anyone. I examined the shallow wear from feet and cart runners, the dark soil spilled near the tree, and the cut of the rock. I found no gate, paint, rope, crack, or change of surface.

“Show it to me.”

“You're looking at it.” She made a low sound when I asked which part. Later I learned that sound meant she was trying to keep an unfriendly answer inside. She set the basket down, walked three measured steps, and placed one pale-tipped foot on the stone. “My side.” She slid that foot forward by its own length. “Venn's side.”

The same piece of road lay under both positions. I asked whether Venn had cut it or lived beside it. She said he had done neither and lived three shelves below us. I looked over the cliff, though there was no reason to expect him directly beneath our feet.

“Then how does he stop anyone?”

Her fins lifted. She studied my entire curved surface, which did not take long. “He owns the distance. He doesn't have to stand on it.”

Near her back foot stood a black post I had taken for a weathered stone. It reached almost to her knee and had a gray metal band fitted around its middle. Grass had been cleared from the base. The road looked old. The cliff looked older. The post looked chosen.

I circled it once and asked if it belonged to Venn. She explained that it marked the start, then became annoyed when I asked what started there. “You gave me a name for it,” I said. “I still don't know what it is.”

She sat and folded her neck until her face was level with me. We stayed that way for a moment. I had reached the part of a question where people sometimes stopped answering and began deciding what kind of person had asked it.

“Can you feel the edge?” she asked.

When I said no, her fins aimed toward the road. She did not touch the post or close her eyes, yet her whole body settled around a fact I could not find. “It's there. The ground changes under you, except it isn't the ground. Everyone knows when they reach one.”

“Everyone except me.”

She looked toward the invisible line, then back at me. When she asked what I was, I told her I did not know. That answer bothered her less than I expected. My failure to feel the road bothered her much more.

She rose, took the basket in her mouth, and started toward the bells. At the post she avoided the stretch she had shown me, using a narrow strip beside the cliff wall. Whoever owned that strip was apparently willing to let her pass, though I could see no more of it than I could see of Venn's.

I followed. Six steps later, she put the basket down and turned her long neck all the way back. “Why are you coming with me?”

“You know where the road is.”

She pointed out that I could see it as well as she could. I had already learned that this was not the same thing. “I know where the stone is,” I told her. “You know where the lines are.”

Her tail moved once against the ground. “My name is Mara.”

I did not know yet that this was an invitation. I only knew it was another useful fact, so I gave one back. “I am Dot.”

“Is that your kind or your name?”

“Both, I think.”

Mara lifted her basket again. This time, when she continued toward the market, she checked that I came with her.

## Chapter 2: Twenty-Three

Mara did not take the open middle of the road. She kept beside the wall until the black post was behind us, then crossed toward the cliff edge at a place with no post at all. Her path made a wide hook around a section I could not distinguish from the rest.

I copied the hook. This pleased her for about four steps. Then she asked where I had come from, and I gave her the answer I had: I did not know. I sometimes found myself in new places without noticing the instant of arrival. I remembered other worlds but no home before them. I could move, see, hear, speak, and learn. I did not sleep or eat. That was most of my useful information about me.

Mara stopped beside the blue tree. “You don't eat? Everything eats something.” She lowered the basket and looked at its contents as if my answer had made them less reliable.

I told her again that I did not. When she asked how I stayed alive, I had to give her another unhelpful truth. “I don't know that I am alive.”

Her fins rose so fast that I moved back. I thought I had frightened her, but a bright sound came from her instead. It began in her throat, broke in the middle, and made her tail tap the road. She was laughing. I had heard laughter on other worlds, though never from a Vey, and I liked hers immediately.

“You say awful things very calmly,” she told me.

“Would they improve if I shouted?” I asked. That made the sound happen again. When she picked up the basket, she walked more slowly so I could stay beside her instead of behind. My information about Mara had increased, though I did not yet understand which part had changed her mind.

We passed under the tree. Its leaves were blue on top and gray below, and each grew from a jointed black stem. I went up among them to see whether the color came from the light. It did not. Mara waited beneath me, trying to look as if waiting for a floating stranger inside a tree was an ordinary use of her morning.

When I came down, she asked how far the broken branch stood from her. I chose the center of her front feet as the start. “Twenty-three.”

“Twenty-three what?”

The answer had arrived whole, as it always did. I could divide it or compare it, but I did not have another name attached. “Twenty-three of the amount between you and the branch.”

Mara sat down again. “That is the least useful answer I have ever heard.”

“It is exact.”

“Exactly useless.” She considered the branch without narrowing her eyes or moving closer. To her it was eleven walking steps, a narrow reach, and too small for anyone to mark unless it blocked a door.

I moved from her feet to the branch at the height where her head would pass. She had given several descriptions, and none was the thing I measured. Her fins did not count steps, she said. They let her know the size of a distance in something like the way she knew the position of her tail. When I pointed out that her tail was attached and the branch was not, she said that was why the explanation was difficult.

I asked her to turn away. She did, though she complained while doing it. I moved the loose fruit from beside her basket to the base of the tree and asked which place was farther from her. She answered without looking. Then I moved to three positions in the road and asked when I had gone beyond the beginning of Venn's holding. She answered each time, and her voice sharpened as I approached the edge.

“You're measuring me now,” she said.

I admitted I was. Her fins lowered. “Ask first.”

I came back to her side. Until then I had treated her ability as I treated the tree: a fact available for examination. Trees did not mind. Mara did, and that made her a different kind of fact. I asked whether I could learn more about her fins.

She lifted the basket. “Later.” It was neither permission nor refusal. I would learn that Mara used *later* for questions she had not answered, work she had not done, and choices she wanted to keep where she could see them.

We continued toward the bells. This time she asked before testing me. She chose two doorways, a hanging rope, and a crack high in the wall. My answers came as numbers. Hers came as walking steps, easy reaches, owned runs, and one place she called too broad to cross before rain.

We were describing the same spaces. We were not finding the same things inside them.

## Chapter 3: The Crossing

The bells grew louder, but Venn's distance still lay between us and the bend. Mara used the thin strip at the wall. I stayed beside her until the black post had nearly passed from sight, then stopped and asked what using the middle would cost.

Mara stopped too. She could have kept walking. I mention that because I did not understand, then, how often she would pause for one of my questions when continuing would have made her day easier.

She set down the basket and explained the crossing fee. Venn did not collect it at the road. A watcher recorded who used the stretch, and the charge reached the traveler's home later. Refusing to pay caused a larger charge. If the traveler still did not pay, the office could take a distance the traveler owned.

I asked what happened when the traveler owned no distance to surrender. Mara's fins flattened. “Then people learn that.” The person's name stayed beside the unpaid charge, she explained. Other owners might refuse passage. Work became harder to reach, and market sellers might stop trusting the person to return payment. No wall appeared, but roads could close one by one.

I looked back at the post. “So Venn does not stop a body. Everyone else stops helping the body afterward.” Mara said that was not how she would put it. When I asked if it was wrong, she pulled the basket closer and told me it was incomplete.

I should have asked what part I had missed. Instead I moved toward the center of the road. Curiosity is useful when a fact can be tested without putting anything else at risk. I was still learning to look for the anything else.

Mara stepped in front of me. “Do not cross.”

“I have no home for a charge to reach.”

“That does not make it permitted.” Her light-tipped foot pressed into the stone at the point she had shown me before. The muscles in her leg had tightened. “This side is open. Past my foot is Venn's.”

I went around her.

I moved no farther than the length of her neck. Nothing touched me. The road did not bend, grip, or push. No sound came from the marker. The gray bands behind the sky continued their slow motion. From where I floated, the bells sounded neither closer nor farther than they should.

I waited. Waiting is part of a test, and I am good at it.

Mara was not. Her tail had gone straight along the ground, and every part of her had drawn away from the invisible edge except the foot that still marked it. She said my name once. It did not sound like an invitation now.

I asked whether she wanted me back. Her yes came before I finished, and I returned at once. That did not repair what I had done. A person may obey after ignoring a warning, and the second action does not travel backward into the first.

Mara checked the road behind me. Then she checked the black post. Her fins moved forward and back in quick, shallow motions. She walked a circle around me without entering the held stretch, as though my passage might have changed something she could locate.

“Did you feel pressure?” she asked. “Heat? A pull in your middle?”

I had no middle that I could identify, and I had felt none of those things. She asked about dizziness, wrongness, and the sense that I had stepped somewhere before permission reached me. I knew what *before* meant. I knew what permission meant. I did not know how one would feel ahead of the other.

“It was road,” I told her. Mara sat beside her basket. The market was close enough now that Vey passed the bend and noticed us. None came near Venn's post. Each adjusted direction before arriving at it, with no pause and no need to look.

“Every Vey can feel a held line,” Mara said. “Little ones feel it before they know the owner's name. Animals turn away. Even a dropped roller curves if it has enough distance to slow.”

I reminded her that I was neither a Vey nor one of Bell's animals. “I can see that.” She rubbed one fin against her shoulder, then made herself stop. “But things belong to someone whether the thing crossing is from Bell or not.”

I returned to the marker and stayed on her side. Up close, the metal band had tiny marks stamped around it. Wear had softened them, but they were deliberate. I could study the record, the watcher, and the way other travelers turned. The one thing I could not study was the edge Mara felt. I had passed through the place where it should be and found no trace.

“I am sorry I crossed after you told me not to,” I said.

Mara's fins lifted halfway. “Are you?”

I considered the question. “I am sorry I made you afraid. I still wanted the answer.”

“Those can both be true,” she said. “It doesn't make the first one disappear.”

Another Vey approached with two bundles tied across his back. He began moving toward the open strip while he was still twenty steps away. Mara watched him, then watched me.

“You need to come to my house,” she said. I asked whether this was punishment. “No. I need someone else to see you do it.” She stood, took the basket, and added around its handle, “You are not doing it again until I say.”

I followed her toward the market. This time she did not check whether I came. She kept one fin aimed backward and knew exactly where I was.

## Chapter 4: Bell

Bell was the name of the shelf, not the sound ahead of us. Mara had to explain this because I spent several minutes trying to understand why anyone would name a home after a noise that came from somewhere else.

The shelf widened as we walked. On our left, doors and windows had been cut into the cliff. Woven shades softened the white light, and shallow steps led up to most homes. On our right, the ground ended. Wooden frames reached out over the drop, held by ropes and iron pins. Vines hung beneath them with pale fruit growing downward.

I went to the edge for a better view. Mara asked me not to, so I stopped above the last strip of stone. Far below, another shelf carried its own road, houses, and moving Vey. More levels continued until the light could no longer separate them.

When I asked what I should avoid, she turned both fins toward me. “Looking into other shelves. Down belongs to people. They live there.”

The drop was open sky and stone. I asked how looking could use it up or damage anyone. She said damage was not the point. Families below had windows, gardens, arguments, and meals. Being higher did not give Bell permission to watch them.

That answer I understood. I moved nearer the wall and kept my attention on our own shelf. A few doorways later, a Vey stood straight up the cliff beside a window, replacing a rope pin. All four feet held the vertical stone while her tail stretched behind her. She talked through the open window to someone inside and never looked down.

I watched until Mara stepped between us. “You moved away from the edge so you could stare at somebody working.”

I pointed out that the worker was standing on a wall. Mara pointed out that she was replacing a pin. “Those are both true,” I said.

Mara admitted they were, but only one concerned us. She called up to the worker and asked whether the lower garden rope had begun to fray. The worker tested it with a back foot, said it would last through the next wet season, and returned to the pin. That was how Vey looked at walls: not as surprising places to stand, but as places where useful work waited.

I tried to imagine Mara's sense of distance working in every direction. To me the window was above the road. To the worker it seemed to be beside her. The cliff did not change. Bodies changed what parts of it could be used.

The road narrowed around a house with a deep stone basin outside. A line of Vey waited with jars. Mara crossed early toward the open side, then stopped because I had stayed at the wall.

I asked who held the distance around the line. “The house does. They lend the basin reach each morning.”

I could have gone straight through the waiting line, since I took up little room and touched no one. Mara led me around. A borrowed distance, I learned, still had manners inside it.

The bells resolved into separate sounds near the broad eastern end of Bell. High notes came from small metal cups tied over stalls. Deeper ones hung at the entrance to the lower plankway. Vey feet clicked on stone. Sellers called prices, neighbors called names, and ropes knocked against the frames below. The air carried warm fruit and wet rock.

Mara paused before the crowd and told me to stay near. I moved beside her shoulder. When she asked for nearer, I touched her fur. She jumped sideways and almost lost the basket.

“That is nearer,” I said.

“It is also touching.” She settled the basket, checked that no fruit had escaped, and gave me a distance with her front feet. “Stay about this far. If someone walks between us, come back to me. Do not test any lines. Do not follow anything down a wall.”

I asked if the last order applied to interesting things. “Especially those.”

We entered together. The market filled the widest place on the shelf and continued below it on platforms I could see through gaps in the planks. Cloth roofs overlapped above tables. Ropes carried baskets between levels. Every open route appeared to bend around some object, stall, or person, yet the middle of the stone road stayed nearly empty.

I had found the bells. I had also found hundreds of invisible edges. Mara looked back at me and found me at the distance she had ordered, close enough to follow and far enough not to touch.

“Good,” she said. It was the first rule of hers I had followed correctly on the first attempt. I decided not to tell her that I had counted twelve others I did not understand.

## Chapter 5: Blue Shell

The empty middle of the market road remained empty even when the edges became crowded. Vey carrying baskets squeezed between tables, waited behind one another at corners, and sometimes climbed a few steps up the wall to let someone pass. A straight route lay open through the center. Nobody took it.

I began counting what they avoided. Some edges had black posts. Others used smaller stones set into the road, each with a dull band around it. At one fruit table, a silver strip had been fixed directly into the wall because there was no room for a post. The markers did not form squares. They made long runs, narrow approaches, and shapes that bent around stalls and vanished down stairs.

I told Mara I could see thirty-two. She had to ask what I meant. “Markers, without counting anything below us.”

She asked me not to count aloud. Owners might think I was checking their holdings, and checking a holding without business there could be taken as a challenge. I did not know how a number became a challenge, but I remembered the way Mara had reacted when I measured her. I kept the rest inside.

At the lower edge, a plankway left Bell and sloped toward a shelf beneath us. It had rails made of rope and boards darkened by many feet. The first section crossed open air before it reached a frame braced against the cliff. A small bell hung at its entrance, though travelers rang it only when the Vey seated there had turned away.

He was thin and gray, with a low table, a metal cash box, and a row of flat blue shells. Each traveler stopped at his marker. He took a coin, passed over one shell, and made a short mark on a slate. The traveler carried the shell down the planks.

“Does the shell keep the boards from breaking?” I asked.

Mara glanced at me to see whether I was trying to be funny. I was not. “It proves Anlo let you cross.”

Anlo heard his name and lifted one fin. Mara returned the greeting. He looked at me for only a moment, then served a Vey whose back basket held long roots. That surprised me after the attention I had received on the road, until I noticed that five travelers had arrived behind her. Anlo was working. My unknown nature did not shorten his line.

A Vey came up from below with an empty basket and a shell between her teeth. Her fur had thinned along her legs, and she stopped on the last plank to breathe before stepping onto Bell. Anlo waited without calling to her. When she reached his table, he accepted the shell and asked whether her daughter's cough had eased.

She said it had. She counted two coins onto his table, one at a time, then asked after his shoulder. He told her it was none of her business and moved the cash box closer so she would not have to reach. Both of them sounded pleased.

The older Vey turned around and bought another crossing down. After she had gone, Mara confirmed that she paid once in each direction. The lower settlement was called Bell Under, and about sixty families lived there. Most growing frames hung below the market shelf while most stalls stood above. Anyone who grew beneath and sold above crossed Anlo's run in the morning and again after trading.

I asked how long Anlo owned the plankway. Mara corrected me: Bell maintained the boards. Anlo held only the crossing from the upper marker to the lower one.

I looked through the gaps. The wood had nails, rope, repairs, and weight on it. Those things could be owned, used up, or broken. The air between the markers showed no wear at all.

When I repeated the question, Mara understood I meant the holding. “Until he sells it or gives it to an heir. His family has held it longer than my grandmother has been alive.”

The old Vey was halfway down. Her empty basket knocked lightly against one rail while a full basket passed her coming up. Both travelers turned their bodies, tested their footing, and made room. Anlo watched until they cleared each other, then returned to his slate.

He did not look cruel. She did not look angry. The coins still left her mouth twice every market day.

I had expected unfair things to announce themselves. On Bell, a costly thing could sound like two neighbors asking about a cough.

## Chapter 6: The Game in the Dust

Four young Vey had claimed a patch of road between a grain stall and the wall. I knew they had claimed it because one dragged her front foot through the dust and shouted the word while the other three scattered.

No adult objected. Shoppers went around the square, and one seller shifted a basket before a child ran into it. The game had become part of the market route as quickly as a dropped box would have.

The child inside the line lowered her body. Another crept toward her from the grain stall. His tail lay flat. His fins had turned forward so far that their tips almost touched the sides of his head. The two children behind him whispered directions that contradicted each other.

I asked what they were doing. Mara had begun walking again, but she stopped where the children could not hear. “Playing Claim.”

The creeping child placed one foot over the scratch. The child inside the square shouted that it was hers. He pulled back, and the two behind him complained that she had called too soon. An argument followed about whether half a foot counted. None of them inspected the line. They knew exactly where it ran.

Mara explained the game while keeping one eye on her basket. One player marked a distance. The others tried to cross before the holder declared ownership. If a challenger reached the far side in time, the claim ended. If the holder spoke first, the challenger went back.

I asked what stopped a challenger who had already begun to cross. Mara said it was the same thing that stopped anyone. I guessed a later charge. “No. Losing.”

That answer made more sense. Games needed rules because the rule made the game possible. A child could ignore it, but then the other children would no longer play with that child. I had seen similar arrangements elsewhere.

The holder noticed me and asked what I was. “Dot,” Mara said. “Dot is with me.”

Being with Mara seemed to answer enough. The child asked whether I could play. Mara said no at the same moment I said I did not know how. The children accepted my answer and rejected hers.

They gave me the outside position. The smallest child drew a fresh line with two quick strokes and took the middle. I floated toward the mark at the speed the earlier challenger had crept. Her fins tracked me. When I crossed, she made no claim. She only stared.

“You were supposed to stop,” she said. I pointed out that she had not made the claim. “I didn't think you could cross.”

The other children crowded close. One passed a foot under me to see whether I was hiding legs. Another asked whether I had jumped over the claim. The smallest one redrew the line directly beneath me and declared the space hers.

“Now move,” she ordered. I moved.

All four children shouted at once. Two thought the claim followed me. One thought I had broken it. The holder sat in the dust with both fins pressed back, looking less upset than interested.

Mara came between us. She did not scold the children. She told them Dot games required rules nobody had written yet, and their families were waiting. That last statement had more force than the first. They returned to the old square, though every one of them looked back.

Mara reminded me that I had promised not to test another line. I reminded her that she had named held lines, while the children were playing a game. She opened her mouth, closed it, and looked back at them. “It belonged to her while she held it.”

When I asked if it truly had, Mara's tail struck the stone. “You cannot take every word apart.”

I wanted to know why. “Because we have to reach my mother before the fruit warms.” She picked up the basket so firmly that its woven sides bent inward. I understood the practical limit, even if the other one remained unclear.

We moved on. Behind us, the children had already changed the game. The smallest stood outside while another drew a much narrower square around herself. They discussed whether floating counted as crossing, jumping, or cheating. No adult came to settle it.

Then one child dashed at the new line. The holder called the claim, and the runner stopped so quickly that dust rose around all four feet.

She could have continued. Her body was already moving. The scratch could not hold her, and no office would charge her later. Still, she stopped because another child had named the space and because the game disappeared if she did not.

The line had been on the ground for less than a minute. Every child could already feel where it was.

# Part II: The Closed Run

## Chapter 7: Fruit in Rows

Mara's mother arranged fruit by size. The largest blue pieces sat at the front of the stall. Smaller ones filled the back trays, and damaged ones waited in a shallow bowl beneath the table. She moved each fruit with her mouth, set it down, then turned it until the pale seam faced the road.

She saw me in the reflection on a metal price card. The fruit between her teeth stopped halfway to its place. She said Mara's name, and Mara answered that she knew.

Her mother put the fruit down before asking what I was. Mara explained that my name was Dot, that she did not know my kind, and that I had crossed one of Venn's held stretches without sensing it. She said the last part quietly, though the market around us was loud.

“Show me,” her mother said.

Mara stared at her. “You told me not to let Dot do it again.” Her mother did not remember giving that order. “You would have,” Mara said.

The older Vey looked at me. Her fur was the same dark brown as Mara's, but the stripe on her neck was gray rather than cream. “Did my daughter order me before I met you?” I said yes. One corner of her mouth moved. “That sounds like Mara.”

She did not ask me to cross anything. Instead she gave me a place beside the damaged-fruit bowl where I would not block customers. When a buyer came, she returned to work. Mara unloaded the basket into the back trays, checking soft spots and turning the seams outward without being told.

I watched the stall work because it gave me more information than another list of questions would have. Buyers did not merely choose fruit. They asked which shelf had grown it, how long it would keep, and whether the wet season had made the skin bitter. Mara's mother answered quickly while adding amounts in her head. If a buyer hesitated, she cut the price on the smallest pieces rather than praise them.

“Your row is crooked,” she told Mara. Mara corrected three fruits. The row still looked crooked to me, but her mother accepted it. Their measure was not mine.

Between buyers, the older Vey introduced herself as Eli. She asked whether Dot was a title, a description, or a name. I told her it was all three because I had needed all three and had only one word.

“That is efficient,” Eli said. Mara called it confusing. “Those can happen together.” Eli slid the damage bowl farther beneath the table as a group approached. Her answer sounded as if she had said similar things to Mara before.

A customer with a rope harness bought six large fruits and asked who held me. Mara's head came up. Eli continued counting coins as she replied that no one held a guest. The customer corrected himself, saying he had meant who was responsible if I damaged a distance.

“Dot is,” Mara said before Eli could answer. The customer looked at my lack of rings, tag, harness, or visible pocket for money and asked what happened if I could not pay.

Mara's fins went flat. Eli placed his change on the table one coin at a time and waited until he picked it up. “Your fruit is ready.”

He left without repeating the question.

I asked whether I had embarrassed them. Eli said the customer had managed that alone. Mara said nothing and began checking a row that did not need checking.

The market thinned when the light turned from white toward gold. Eli packed the remaining large fruit into two carrying baskets. She put the small ones under damp cloth and counted the damaged bowl.

Eli counted seven damaged pieces. Mara corrected her to six. When Eli looked beneath the table, I moved aside and revealed one fruit hidden by my shape. She retrieved it. “Seven. The visitor comes to supper.”

Mara lifted both fins and asked why.

“Because we need to know what you brought through the market before every neighbor tells us a different version.” Eli tied the cloth over the trays. “Also because leaving a person beside a damage bowl after closing would be rude.”

I told Eli I did not eat. She paused with the knot between her teeth. “Then supper will be inexpensive.”

Mara laughed. The work around her face loosened, and for a moment she looked like the Vey under the blue tree instead of the one guarding me through a crowd. Eli assigned Mara one basket and balanced the other across her own back. She gave me the empty space between them as though that were also part of the load.

I went home with the fruit sellers.

## Chapter 8: The Run Home

Going home required less turning than reaching the market had. Eli left the stall from its north side, passed one black post, and entered a long strip of road that curved with the cliff. Mara followed without asking whose holding it was.

“Ours,” she said when I asked. The answer changed her voice. Pride was part of it, but so was caution. She carried the word as carefully as the fruit basket on her back.

I measured from the market post. The run stayed near the wall while smaller holdings crossed it from doorways, steps, and hanging gardens. At each meeting point, Eli knew which route had the right to continue and which traveler should wait. Others greeted her and stepped aside without seeing a pass or paying a coin.

I asked whether everyone knew the run belonged to them. “Everyone on Bell knows,” Eli said. “The record matters when someone doesn't.”

Mara told the history while we walked. Her grandmother's mother, Sennet, had claimed the route four generations earlier. The run began at an anchor stone above their home and ended at the second post by the north stalls. It had passed through the family without sale or break.

The road did not feel different to me, but Mara's body did. She took the open middle instead of searching for borrowed edges. She let the basket ride lower. When a neighbor entered from a side path, Mara kept moving and the neighbor adjusted course.

I told Mara she liked being on it. “I like not asking.”

At a narrow section, Eli stopped beside a Vey pulling a handcart. The cart held folded stall cloth and was too wide for both loads to pass. Eli could have required the cart to back out. Instead she shifted her basket to Mara, climbed three steps up the wall, and waited while the wheels bumped through.

The cart driver thanked her and promised a small payment on market day. Eli told him to repair the left wheel before he paid anyone. It struck the wall on every turn and would soon damage the axle.

After he left, I called the crossing a gift. Eli corrected me. She had rented it, and the driver would bring a coin if he remembered. When I asked what happened if he forgot, she said, “Then I will remember for both of us.”

Mara laughed under the two baskets. Eli took hers back, and we continued. The exchange was friendly. It was also part of their income.

The market run did more than carry their feet to work. On busy days, sellers reaching the north stalls needed pieces of it. Eli rented brief crossings, kept track of who owed what, and sometimes accepted fruit, rope, or repair work instead of coins. Stall sales rose and fell with weather and harvest. The road paid most reliably.

I asked how many distances the family held. Mara's tail lowered. “One.”

She pointed out a pale Vey ahead of us. Metal rings ran along the upper half of his tail, though he turned into a doorway before I could count them. Vey with several holdings wore one ring for each. Families with a single route often wore none because the first ring told everyone exactly how little they had.

I called the rings useful information. “Only if you are the person with nine.”

I remembered the customer asking who held me and what happened if I could not pay. A ring could answer those questions before anyone spoke. No ring answered them too.

At the western bend, the run left the market road and climbed a gentle slope toward the homes. Eli slowed. The fruit baskets were not large, but the straps pressed flat paths in her fur. Mara offered to carry both. Eli gave her the smaller one and kept the heavier load.

I asked why Eli had not given Mara the heavy basket. “Because she has already offered twice,” Eli said.

Mara made the low sound that meant an unfriendly reply was staying inside. I was beginning to learn the family's measurements.

The old anchor stone stood near their doorway. Unlike the market marker, it was broad and pale, worn smooth where generations of Vey had rubbed against it while turning home. I measured the full route behind us: four hundred and twelve walking steps by Mara's count.

“One real run,” Mara said, touching the stone with her light-tipped foot. It gave them a way to work, a small payment from neighbors, and a place in Bell's record. It also told anyone counting rings that there was nothing else behind it.

Eli carried the last basket through the doorway. Mara waited beside the anchor until I reached her, even though I did not need the permission her foot seemed to offer. Then we crossed into the family's home together.

## Chapter 9: Nine Rings

The next day I learned that a busy market could become quiet without becoming silent. Bells still moved. Feet still struck stone. A seller below us kept calling a price because she had not yet noticed. Around Eli's stall, voices ended one after another as a pale gray Vey approached.

I counted the rings on his tail before Mara named him. There were nine.

Venn walked with two Vey who carried flat document cases across their backs. He wore no other sign of office, and the market road made room before he reached it. Some sellers greeted him. He answered each by name.

Eli was weighing fruit against small metal pieces in a balance tray. She finished the measure, wrapped the fruit, and gave the buyer his change. Only then did she face Venn.

“Eli,” he said. “Mara.” Then his attention came to me. The small fins behind his head lifted, but his tail remained still. “And I have not met you.”

I gave him my name. He asked whether I was made or born, whether I was animal, machine, or some kind of traveling tool. My answer to each question was the same. I did not know.

When Venn asked whose property I was, I corrected him. “I am not property.” He lowered his head. “Then I asked badly. I am sorry.”

The apology arrived without an excuse attached. I accepted it. Mara looked from him to me as if one of us had failed to behave according to the useful arrangement she had prepared.

Mara told him that held lines did not act on me. “I heard.” Venn did not ask for a display. He glanced toward the center road, then back to Mara. “Bell has had three versions since yesterday. Dot flew above the run, broke its marker, or made the distance disappear.”

I corrected the last version. “Good,” Venn said. “Replacing a recorded run is difficult.”

I could not tell whether he had made a joke. Mara could. One side of her mouth almost moved, and then she remembered she did not want it to.

Venn turned to Eli. The two document carriers stepped closer but did not open their cases. “I filed a competing record for the north market run eleven days ago. The office should deliver notice today. I wanted to tell you myself first.”

Eli kept all four feet still. Only her fins changed, drawing back against her fur. She asked which run. “The route from your anchor to the north-stall post.”

“That is ours,” Mara said.

Venn looked at her, not at Eli. “Your family uses it. You have a recognized chain. I know both things.”

Mara asked what Venn had filed. One carrier removed a bound book from her case and opened it to a marked page. Venn did not touch it. He explained that his grandfather had bought a collection of routes from the Ruhl family sixty-one years earlier. The sale had been witnessed, paid, and entered. The market run appeared among them.

Eli moved one fruit from the front row to the back. It was not soft. I had watched her test it that morning. “We have Sennet's record.”

“I know.” Venn's voice remained careful. “That is why I filed instead of placing my marker. The office has to decide which chain controls.”

Mara came around the stall table. “You own nine. We own this one.”

Neither statement was an argument under Bell's rules, but the market heard them as something else. Two nearby sellers stopped pretending to arrange their tables.

Venn lowered his tail until the rings touched stone. “I did not create the entry, Mara. I found it while repairing my grandfather's book after a water leak.”

Mara said he could have left it damaged. “Yes.” He looked at the open record. “I could have.”

I studied his face and found no pleasure. I found no lie either. His claim could harm Mara's family without requiring him to enjoy it. That was harder to arrange in my understanding than a cruel owner would have been.

A clerk in a gray sash entered the market carrying a rolled notice. She saw Venn, then Eli, and slowed. Eli cleared a place on the stall table. “Put it here.” The clerk obeyed, but Eli did not open the paper while Venn stood there.

He dipped his head to her, to Mara, and then to me. “The office hearing begins as soon as you answer. I will bring the original book.”

After he left, the market's noise returned in uneven pieces. A bell rang too loudly. Someone below resumed a price at the wrong number. Eli kept looking at the rolled paper while Mara placed her light-tipped foot on it before the wind could take it.

Mara did not ask whether the distance was theirs. She asked what happened to the stall if the office gave it to Venn. Eli had no answer ready.

## Chapter 10: Opposite Directions

Bell's distance office occupied one room behind the market. Paper covered more of its walls than stone did. Bundles filled shelves from floor to ceiling, and labeled boxes stood in the spaces between. Retired markers leaned near the door with their metal bands clouded by age.

I wanted to inspect all of it. Mara told me to inspect nothing unless invited.

The clerk behind the main table was named Orra. Gray fur circled her muzzle, and one shoulder sat lower than the other. She opened Eli's notice, checked three lines, then made a mark in a book without asking why we had come.

“North market run,” she said. “I expected you yesterday.” Eli reminded her when the notice had arrived. “Then you are quick.” Orra removed two records from different shelves. One was a folded sheet whose edges had softened from handling. The other remained bound in Venn's family book. His carrier placed it on a wooden support so its spine would not be strained.

Venn stood at the far end of the table. He had brought the original as promised and no companions except the carrier. The nine rings on his tail rested against one another without sound.

Orra began with Sennet's sheet. She read the name, the house anchor, the north-stall marker, the witnesses, and four transfers within the family. Each new holder had been entered beneath the last. No sale appeared. No challenge appeared either.

“Recognized and continuous,” Orra said. Eli's fins eased forward by a small amount. Then Orra read the purchased record. Venn's grandfather had paid the Ruhl family for several holdings sixty-one years earlier. The amount, witnesses, and office seal remained visible. The same house anchor and market post were named.

Mara leaned over the table. “Sennet's is older.” Orra agreed while running one blunt claw beside the faded entry. “Age establishes that it existed. Age does not always establish control.”

She explained the rule through the marks in front of us. A paid transfer created a second trail: the old holder's entry, the buyer's entry, the amount, and the office witness. An inherited run could pass without new payment, so later records sometimes contained only a family name. When two complete chains collided, the recorded sale carried more proof than repeated inheritance.

Mara said this meant Venn would win. “If both chains describe one run and neither contains an error, his filing is stronger.” Orra did not look pleased by her own answer. She also did not soften it.

I moved above the records. Mara began to object, but Orra waved one foot. “Let Dot look. Half the market says Dot can erase a distance. Reading ink should be safe.”

The handwriting on Sennet's sheet leaned left and joined letters I had first learned separately. I worked through it twice. Venn's book used darker ink and straighter forms. The marker descriptions agreed. The number agreed: four hundred and twelve walking steps.

One phrase did not. I asked Orra to read Sennet's endpoint line aloud. She did. The claim traveled from the house anchor toward the market post. Then I asked for the purchased entry. That line began at the market and ended at the house.

“They face opposite ways,” I said. Mara looked at the papers again. Venn came one step closer. Orra did not move.

“A run between two points is the same length in either direction,” she said.

“This table is the same length from you to me and from me to you,” I agreed. “But if direction never matters, why did both clerks write one?”

Orra answered that clerks copied the speaker's words. Sennet had probably stood at her home when she made the claim. The Ruhl seller had probably begun at the market. I asked whether either fact appeared in the record. It did not.

Venn studied his grandfather's page and asked if the Ruhls might have held only the return. “There has never been a one-way holding on Bell,” Orra said.

“Never recorded,” I corrected.

She gave me the same look she had given the notice. “I have kept this office for thirty-one years.”

I told her that proved what the books had said during her service. “The ink is older.”

Mara made the low sound again, this time on my behalf. Orra's tail moved once beneath the table. Then she pulled both records closer and placed two blank cards beside them. On one she copied Sennet's line. On the other she copied the Ruhl line. She turned the cards until both starts faced the same edge.

Nothing about the words changed. Something about the room did.

Eli asked whether direction decided the claim. Orra said she did not know. Mara asked whether the run stayed open while Orra learned. Orra said it could not. Use by either claimant would become evidence of control while control itself remained disputed.

Eli asked how long the closure would last. “The shelf authority has sixty days to hear it. They may extend to ninety.”

Orra closed Venn's book and folded Sennet's sheet. She did not return either to its shelf. “I was prepared to rule from the sale marks. I cannot do that with an unexplained difference in the route description.”

She placed both records in one gray case and tied the cord herself. “This goes up today.”

## Chapter 11: The Gray Knot

Orra closed the run before we left the office. She carried a strip of gray cord to the old anchor stone, wrapped it beneath the worn top, and tied one square knot where anyone approaching from the house could see it.

The cord did not cross the road. It did not reach my whole width. A traveler could pass without touching it or even changing stride.

Eli watched Orra pull the knot tight. “We open the stall every morning.”

“I know.” Orra checked the ends of the cord. “Until the authority rules, neither chain may gain new use. If I leave the run active, every crossing becomes an argument about control.”

Mara asked whether Orra could record both families using it. Orra said the record had no way to divide one disputed holding without deciding that such a division existed. She sounded angry at the book, but she still left the knot in place.

Eli repeated sixty days. Orra corrected it to a possible ninety. Eli turned toward the house. “I heard you the first time.”

Orra's apology followed us through the doorway. It did not untie anything.

Before the light had fully changed, Eli packed the smallest fruit basket. She needed to take tomorrow's soft pieces back to the stall so they would sell first. Mara tightened the carrying straps. Neither spoke about leaving them until morning.

“We need a different route,” Mara said. The cliff did not contain many. From their door, every short approach joined the held run before the market. A stair descended west toward Bell Under, but reaching the market from there required going below, crossing Anlo's plankway, and climbing the far stair to the south end. Mara stood still while her fins measured what her feet had not yet walked that day.

“Two thousand and forty.” The closed route was four hundred and twelve. I checked the comparison twice and told her the detour was nearly five times as long. “I know how numbers work, Dot.” She tightened one strap that did not need tightening.

We took the western stair. It dropped along the cliff in steep turns, each landing claimed by a different household or garden. Eli knew the borrowed strips, but the fruit basket made every wall crossing slower. At one bend she waited for two children carrying water because the open reach could not hold both loads.

Bell Under received less light. Homes opened beneath the upper shelf, and the market's frames made a roof of boards, roots, and rope. Voices from above came through the gaps without faces attached. We passed the old woman I had watched buy two shells. She was sorting roots outside her door.

At Anlo's lower marker, Eli put down a coin. He did not ask why she had come from home by this route. The gray knot had reached him faster than we had.

Anlo offered to mark the crossings for payment after the hearing. Eli picked the coin back up, looked at it, then returned it to his table. “If I owe today, I pay today.”

Anlo accepted that boundary. He passed her a blue shell and gave Mara another after she paid her own coin. I did not require one. I stayed close enough to Mara that Anlo could watch me, but he did not decide whether I counted as a separate crossing.

The plankway swayed under the Vey and not under me. At the upper end, Eli returned her shell. Mara returned hers. The fruit had traveled farther and now cost two coins to reach the same stall.

While we climbed toward the north end, I asked Mara whether she could still sense the old run. She stopped on the stair. Her fins turned toward home, though stone and hundreds of steps blocked the view. “Yes. The edges have not moved.”

“Then the knot changed the rule, not the distance.”

Mara came up one step until her face was level with me. “The rule decides whether our use becomes evidence against us. It decides whether the office calls us owners or thieves. You keep asking what happened to the road as if the road is the only thing that can happen to.”

She continued upward before I answered. Eli had heard, but she did not look back. We reached the market after the gold light had begun to fade. Eli stored the soft fruit, checked the stall cloth, and started the same long route home with an empty basket.

I measured every step beside them. The number did not become smaller because I understood it.

## Chapter 12: The Place by the Door

The family's home had been cut farther into the cliff than its doorway suggested. A woven screen formed the front wall, with two openings left for light. Sleeping nests lined the back. A water jar stood beneath one window, and a lamp hung from a chain where no tail could knock it down.

Tavi had met me at supper the night before, when Eli kept the questions mostly to the road. On the second evening she waited at the threshold with all the questions that had survived a day. She was smaller than Mara and had a pale patch over one eye. Before Mara could set down the empty basket, Tavi asked whether I slept, bit, grew, broke, hatched, or had ever been inside a wall.

Mara told Tavi that Dot did not know. “I did not ask you.”

I answered the questions I could. I did not sleep or bite. I had never noticed myself growing. I had not broken yet. I did not remember hatching, and I had been inside many walls when doorways led there.

Tavi asked to touch me, and Mara refused for me. I reminded her that she had already ordered her mother once before the answer came. Mara looked at me, then at Tavi. “Ask Dot and accept the answer.”

I allowed one foot. Tavi pressed it gently against my side, then pulled back and called me cold. I told her she was warm.

This satisfied her enough to repeat the experiment with the other foot. Mara ended it there.

Nema watched from beside the open window. She was older than Eli, with pale fur around her muzzle and one fin that lifted only halfway. She did not ask me anything. While Tavi brought bowls and Mara heated roots over a covered stone, Nema followed the work with her eyes and moved one bowl when it blocked the water jar.

There were places for four Vey around the low eating cloth. Eli's remained empty. Mara served Nema first, then Tavi, then herself. She placed a bowl in front of me before remembering I did not eat.

Tavi suggested the bowl could hold my questions. “Then we need a larger one,” Mara replied.

They ate without waiting for Eli because the long route had kept us late and she had stayed to close the stall. Tavi described three new versions of my market crossing between bites. In one I had swallowed Venn's marker. In another I had bought the whole road with a coin that had no sides. Mara corrected only the version in which I had carried her home.

Nema listened. Once, when Tavi said the held distance had vanished under me, Nema's working fin rose as far as it could. She still said nothing.

The lamp had burned low by the time Eli entered. The basket on her back was empty, but she stood in the doorway as if it still held weight. Mara moved her own bowl aside and brought water.

Nema asked how much the stall had taken. Eli drank before answering: about half an ordinary day's money. Buyers arriving from Bell Under had stopped at the south end rather than climb to the north stalls. Several regular sellers had done the same, which gave customers even less reason to continue.

Tavi counted on her feet and asked if the loss would continue for sixty days. “We do not know.” Eli set down the water cup carefully. “The authority may rule sooner. It may take ninety.”

Mara began listing changes. They could carry only the highest-price fruit, open later, close earlier, or rent a small space near the south steps. Eli answered each with a cost. Less fruit meant leaving good produce unsold. Shorter hours missed the buyers who came around work. Every southern space already belonged to someone, and rent there would sit on top of Anlo's crossings.

Mara finally suggested using their run. Eli's head lifted. “No.” Mara did not argue; the speed of the refusal left no space for it.

Tavi looked from one to the other and pointed out that the run was theirs. “The hearing decides what the office recognizes,” Eli said. “Until then, the knot stays.”

She served herself the roots that had gone cool. Mara offered to warm them, but Eli began eating. Nobody mentioned that she would walk the same 2,040 steps again before the next market opened.

I stayed near the door because the room belonged to the family and I did not know which inside spaces were mine to use. Nema noticed. She shifted her tail away from a folded mat, making an open place beside the window.

“You are not lost,” she said when I moved there. I told her I knew where I was. Nema's half-lifted fin tilted toward me. “That is not what I said.”

I waited for the rest. She turned back to the window instead.

Below us, the last market bells stopped. The route home had closed hours earlier, but Mara's family was still paying for it in cold food, tired bodies, and the empty place Eli had left by the door.

# Part III: What Holds

## Chapter 13: A Road Above the Road

Mara began searching Bell's west end the next morning. Eli had not asked her to. She said she was only checking whether a garden stair connected with the lower route before Anlo's marker. The measuring cord over her shoulder suggested a larger hope.

The homes thinned as we moved west. Fruit gardens gave way to storage sheds, then to bare frames whose ropes had been removed. The shelf narrowed until the main road ended at a wall of uncut cliff. From there, the western stair dropped toward Bell Under.

Mara tested every side path. One ended at a locked garden. Another joined a family stair whose owner would rent passage, but the rent plus Anlo's crossing cost more than the stall had made the day before. A third path had lost two steps to a rockfall and would not hold a loaded basket.

I watched the wall while she measured the drop.

A shallow line crossed the cliff above us. It began behind a storage shed, climbed to the height of a house, then continued around the curve of the rock. The ledge was narrow but deliberate. Square holes marked places where posts had once stood.

I told Mara there was another road above us. She did not look up. “There is old cutting everywhere.”

I went to the first square hole. Tool marks remained beneath the weathering. The ledge held level for thirty of my units before the curve hid it. “This cutting has a route.”

That made her look. Her fins rose toward it and flattened at once.

“No,” she said. I had asked no question, so I waited for the one she had answered. When she began rolling her measuring cord, I moved closer to the ledge.

“It goes to Corran,” she said. “It used to.” Corran lay beyond the west curve on another broad shelf. The low road took several days with family loads because it descended, traveled below the cliff bends, and climbed again. The old cutting reached an upper stair near Corran by a much shorter line.

I asked why she was measuring ruined steps when this shorter route existed. Mara tied the cord more tightly than necessary. “Nobody walks the Corran ledge.”

I floated up until I could see its surface. Loose grit covered the first section, but the stone beneath remained broad enough for Mara's four feet. I found no deep cracks. The old post holes weakened only the outer edge, where a careful traveler would not step.

I told her the rock still held. “That isn't the danger.”

Mara's answer brought both fins flat against her head. She told me the warning her grandmother had used whenever they came west: never enter a route that no one owns. She had heard it before she knew what Corran was. Nema had heard it before her. The old ledge belonged to no recognized family on Bell.

I pointed out that no owner meant no fee. “It also means no one keeps it stable.” Mara pointed up with her nose but kept her feet on the lower road. “An open run can stretch so its end never comes, fold back to its start, or arrive somewhere else.”

I looked at the cut stone. Its length continued around the bend. “Have you watched it do those things?”

“No.”

She said this without hesitation. I asked whether she knew someone who had. She named no one. Everyone knew the risk, which meant no one tested it, which meant no one she knew had seen the failure. To Mara, that chain supported the warning. To me, it left the center empty.

I moved ten units along the ledge and came back. Mara's tail went rigid, but she did not order me down. She knew by then that the danger she sensed did not act on me in the ordinary way.

I reported that those ten units remained in place. Mara corrected the size of my evidence. “For ten.” I agreed.

That answer surprised her. She had expected me to turn ten into the whole road. I was trying to learn.

The post holes continued into the distance, and one broken iron pin still held a thread of old rope. This had been maintained work once. People had carried loads there, replaced rails, cleared grit, and trusted an owner whose name I did not know.

I asked who had held it last. Mara told me to ask Orra. She sounded certain the book would close the question, which was useful. A question that could be closed should be taken to the place with the answer.

She left the measuring cord over her shoulder. We walked back toward the office together, with the road above us appearing and disappearing as the cliff curved.

## Chapter 14: The Blank Page

Orra was copying the direction lines onto a larger sheet when we returned. She listened to Mara's question without lifting her head, then stopped with the pen touching paper.

“The Corran ledge?” Mara confirmed it. Orra put down the pen and asked what had made us look there. Mara mentioned the stall, the detour, and the cut stone. She did not mention my ten-unit crossing until Orra asked why grit covered my lower surface.

“Dot went out,” Mara said. “Only a little.” Orra looked at the dust, then at me. “Of course.”

She disappeared between the shelves at the back. Boxes moved. A bundle fell and made her say a word Mara had not used in front of Tavi. When Orra returned, she carried a wooden-covered book against her good shoulder and used the table to support its weight.

The Corran record began near the middle. Its first pages listed repairs, marker changes, payments, and transfers for the ledge. Many names belonged to the Ise family. The entries grew shorter as the ink changed color across the years.

Orra followed them with one claw. The last recognized holder was Ise Marrow. A later clerk had entered Marrow's death. No sale followed. No child, partner, named successor, or office transfer appeared beneath it.

She turned the page. Someone had written *Corran* at the top. The rest was blank. So was the page after it.

Mara suggested a missing sheet. Orra showed her the binding thread. It passed through every fold without a break. Page numbers continued in order. If a clerk had removed a record, the cut or gap should remain. There was neither.

Mara suggested another book. “Possibly.” Orra made the word sound like a door she was willing to check but did not expect to open. “I have indexed every distance volume in this office. No later Corran holding appears.”

I asked for the last length. Orra read eight hundred and thirty walking steps from Bell's west post to Corran's upper stair. The same number appeared in four earlier measurements. The final repair note replaced two rail pins near the bend. Nothing said the ledge had failed.

I asked who owned it now. Orra left one foot on the blank page. “Under this record, no recognized person does.”

Mara paced the narrow open strip between the table and shelves. She said the owner might have existed without an entry. Orra agreed. The owner might also have moved to another shelf, changed names, or died with an heir no clerk found. Each possibility required a person who had not claimed a crossing, rent, inheritance, or repair for more than a century.

“A distance cannot belong to nobody,” Mara said. Orra replied that this was what she had been taught. The answer changed Mara's pacing. “You were taught?”

Orra lowered herself onto a cushion. Her bad shoulder had begun to shake from holding the book open. She told us that unowned distances were believed to lose stability. A held run stayed fixed between its markers because an owner maintained its form. Without that holding, length and destination might change.

I asked what an owner did to maintain form. Orra named records, markers, repairs, and recognition. Those actions maintained the route's use. I asked what action kept the space from lengthening. She could not name one beyond ownership itself.

I asked whether Orra had measured a failed distance. Her working fin turned toward the door while the market noise continued outside, ordinary and close. “No.” Mara stopped pacing.

Orra added that failures were rare. She had seen reports from other shelves: a stair that returned travelers to the same landing, a garden reach that grew longer after its holder died. When I asked whether she had inspected those reports, she said they had been repeated in training, not kept in Bell's office.

Mara said Orra trusted the clerks who trained her. “Yes.” Orra looked down at the wooden book. “And they trusted theirs.”

The blank page remained under her foot. It proved no owner had been recognized after Ise Marrow. It did not prove the ledge was safe. It also did not contain the failure everyone had learned to fear.

Orra closed the cover carefully. “If you are asking permission to use it, I cannot give that.” Mara said she was not. Her measuring cord still hung over her shoulder when we left, but she kept one foot on it so the loose end would not trail toward the west road.

## Chapter 15: The Mark at Corran

We returned west before the sky bands turned violet. Mara said she wanted to put away the measuring cord. The storage shed stood below the ledge, but she carried the cord past its hook and stopped at the first cut step.

I told Mara she intended to measure it. “No.” She looked up at the old road. “I am going to stand here while you do.”

I asked whether she wanted me to. Mara said wanting was not the useful word. If the ledge remained eight hundred and thirty steps, the record was accurate. If it did not, she wanted to know before anyone in her family became desperate enough to try it.

I asked whether she would come. “I cannot test the same thing you test.” She set the cord on the stone but kept one foot through its loop. “If it folds under you, you float. If it changes what a Vey feels, you may not notice. I can watch from here until the curve.”

That was a limit, not an argument. I went up alone.

The ledge began two feet wide and kept that width beyond the first broken post. Grit shifted in the shallow depressions left by old feet. Wind traveled along the wall in steady pushes, but I had no weight for it to move. On my right, the cliff rose beyond sight. On my left, Bell fell away into shelves and pale air.

I measured from the west post. Every old rail hole stood the same interval from the next except where rock had broken one outer corner. The walking surface remained intact there, wide enough for Mara if she kept near the wall. I checked the crack beneath it and found solid stone.

At one hundred steps I turned. Mara stood below and behind me, small beside the storage shed. Her working fin aimed at the ledge. I signaled by moving up and down twice, the answer we had agreed meant the road remained stable.

The curve took Bell out of sight.

Beyond it, the ledge crossed a long face of bare cliff. No homes, gardens, doors, or side paths broke the wall. Some rail pins remained as rust-dark stumps. Others had left only square holes. The wind made a low sound in each empty place.

I stopped at every hundred steps and measured the width. It varied by less than one of my smallest useful divisions. Distance from the wall did not change. The far end approached at the proper rate.

That sentence sounds more certain than the crossing felt. I could measure what the ledge was doing while I occupied it. I could not measure what it might do after my attention moved on. Mara's question had followed me around the curve.

At seven hundred steps, the stone widened. Broken stairs climbed toward the lip of another shelf. Corran lay above, with roof edges and garden ropes visible against the sky.

Beside the first stair, someone had cut a small sign into the cliff.

It was a circle with one dot at its center.

I knew it.

No memory followed the knowing. I could not place the mark on another wall, object, page, or world. I had no image of seeing it before. Familiarity arrived alone, as exact and useless as my answer of twenty-three beneath the blue tree.

I examined the cut. Weather had softened the circle more on its western edge. Dark growth filled the deepest part of the dot. The tool had entered the stone many years before the rail pins rusted, though I could not give it an age.

The final measurement from Bell's west post to the foot of Corran's upper stair was eight hundred and thirty walking steps.

I repeated the last thirty. The number did not change. I measured the wider stone, the first stair, and the mark's position. Then I started back, because a test without a return would leave Mara watching an empty curve.

Bell reappeared after four hundred steps. Mara had climbed to the ledge mouth but kept all four feet below the first post hole. When she saw me, she sat down without taking her foot from the cord.

I signaled stable twice, then descended. Mara asked for the result, and I gave her the route facts first: solid walking surface, one broken outer corner, steady width, working stair, correct destination, and eight hundred and thirty steps in each direction.

She heard the word *first*. “And?”

The mark did not belong to the route question. It belonged to me, though I did not know why. Telling Mara that I recognized it would give her fear another object without giving either of us an answer.

“There is an old cut sign near the Corran stair,” I said. “A circle around a dot. I do not know what it means.”

That was true. It was not all I knew.

## Chapter 16: Evidence for One Body

Mara did not celebrate the number. She unwound the measuring cord and compared its knots with the spacing between the first two post holes. The old record had described that spacing too. My result fit the road that had been built.

Mara repeated the measurement at last. I added that it held in both directions. “I heard you.” She began winding the cord again, slower this time. “And you arrived at Corran.”

I listed the upper stair, roof edges, garden ropes, and wider stone. She knew each feature from descriptions of Corran. None proved the ledge would treat her as it had treated me.

I reminded her that accuracy was what she had wanted. “I wanted an answer to a smaller question because I was afraid of the larger one.” Mara sat beneath the first broken post. “You gave me the smaller answer.”

I went back to the route mouth. The first steps remained where I had left them. “The ledge has had no recorded holder since Ise Marrow. It still matches four old measurements.” Mara corrected the claim: it had matched while I crossed.

I said I had crossed twice. Mara looked up. “Do you think saying it twice makes twice into a hundred and forty years?”

I did not. I had almost said it as if I did.

We tested what could be tested without her stepping onto the ledge. Mara set one end of the cord at the west post while I carried the other to each remaining rail hole in sight. The spaces agreed with the record. She placed a loose stone on the first step and pulled it back with the cord. Its weight and path did not change.

Then she rolled a fruit pit along the cut. It traveled farther than she expected and stopped against the wall. It did not curve away from the unowned route as held lines made moving objects curve. Mara retrieved it with the cord rather than walking out.

I called the pit's straight motion different and evidence supporting my crossing. Mara agreed to both descriptions. She still would not enter the ledge.

She was not refusing evidence. She was placing it beside the cost of being wrong. I had no family waiting for me, no body that fell, and no market that would lose its last worker if I vanished. A safe result for me was information for Mara, not permission.

I asked what could be enough. Mara touched the light patch on her foot to the lower step, where the road had not yet begun. “Someone would have to walk it.”

“Then the first walker cannot have enough beforehand.” Mara agreed. Her foot stayed where it was. “That is the larger answer.”

The violet light reached the western wall. Below us, Eli and Tavi appeared on the lower stair with the day's empty baskets. Eli had made the long journey home. She looked up, saw Mara beside the ledge, and stopped.

Mara removed her foot from the step before Eli called her name.

We did not tell Eli about the full crossing that evening. Mara said the result belonged to her until she understood what she meant to do with it. I had wanted another witness when I crossed Venn's run. Mara wanted fewer.

I almost argued that facts did not belong to anyone. Then I remembered the customer asking who held me, the way Mara had refused Tavi's question for me, and my own decision not to tell Mara what the mark felt like.

I promised not to tell Eli. Mara studied me and said I thought she was wrong. “I think you may be. I also think it is your family and your risk.”

She picked up the cord. “Those can happen together.”

It was Eli's sentence from the stall. Mara carried it down the stair with the measurement she had not yet chosen to share.

## Chapter 17: The Missing Holder

For three days Mara tried to find an owner the book had missed. She did this while working. Each morning we carried fruit down to Bell Under, paid Anlo, crossed the planks, and climbed to the stall. Each evening the baskets were lighter and Eli's coin pouch lighter with them. Between customers, Mara asked older sellers whether an Ise family still lived on another shelf.

One remembered an Ise rope maker at Corran. Another remembered a family with a similar name below Bell. Neither could connect them to Marrow. Orra sent a question to the Corran office and received no answer during those three days.

On the second morning, Mara suggested the holder might not know. We were climbing the far stair with damp fruit, and I asked what holding meant if that person had never heard of the route, collected from it, repaired it, or crossed it.

“It means the run passed to them.” I asked what had passed.

Mara gave me the answer Bell used: ownership. I asked what physical thing had traveled from Ise Marrow to the unknown heir. She said nothing had to travel. The relationship continued.

When she said the relationship continued, I asked what two things shared it. She nearly dropped her end of the basket. “Dot, I am on a stair.” I waited until we reached the landing. She did not continue the answer.

On the third day, Orra allowed Mara to inspect the death register. It named no surviving child. Mara checked neighboring family records anyway, following marriages and moves until the lines ended outside Bell's books. The possible holder moved farther away with every page and became more important at the same time.

I asked Orra whether the ledge knew about an unregistered inheritance. She rubbed her bad shoulder. “That question does not belong in a record office.” When I asked where it did belong, she looked toward the market and did not answer.

We left by the north door. The four children from the Claim game had drawn a new figure in the dust. It had three joining squares and one narrow path between them. The smallest child guarded the path while two others tried to distract her.

One challenger asked a seller a loud question. When the holder turned her head, the second child ran. Dust rose under his feet. The holder spun back and called the claim before his last foot crossed. He stopped with his body leaning forward and his tail thrown behind him for balance.

No marker stood there. No office held their rules. The child who had drawn the line could erase it with the same foot. Still, all four argued about the exact position of the runner's last toe rather than whether the line existed.

Mara stopped beside me. The smallest child lost the argument, wiped away her line, and drew another six steps farther down the road. The others changed direction at once. Shoppers changed with them.

I asked who was holding the new shape. Mara named the child. I asked what the child was using.

Mara's fins moved forward. The holder had already left her own line to chase a challenger, yet the other children still turned at its corners. A Vey carrying folded cloth walked around the square without asking whose game it was.

Mara watched one body after another preserve the shape. Her mouth opened, but her first answer did not come out. When she finally spoke, her voice was low enough that only I heard. “All of us are.”

The children began another round. The holder scratched one extra branch into the path, and everyone learned it before the dust settled.

Mara walked toward the stall more slowly. She no longer asked me to explain what she had seen. For once, the question belonged to her before it belonged to me.

## Chapter 18: Too Much Answer

Mara did not go to the stall. She climbed to the low wall above the market and sat where she could watch the children without standing in anyone's route. I stayed beside her.

Below us, Anlo passed blue shells across his table. Eli turned fruit seams toward the road. The children erased one branch of their game and added another. Thirty-two markers remained visible from where we sat, and every body moved among them without needing to read the bands.

Mara told me to say what I thought. When I asked which part, she named the whole pattern: why I crossed and she stopped, why the fruit pit rolled straight, and why children could create an edge with one foot.

I began with the part I could measure. Stone occupied space whether a Vey claimed it or not. The Corran ledge kept its length, width, direction, and destination after Ise Marrow's record ended. The child's dust line did not alter the stone beneath it. Venn's stretch had not pushed me back.

“That is the road,” Mara said. “What is the holding?” I pointed toward the game. The holder had moved outside her square, but three children and six shoppers preserved its shape. No single body held the whole edge. Their shared response did.

Mara's fins aimed at the market. “Then the markers are reminders.” I added records and warnings, all telling Vey which response to repeat. “Everyone except you,” she said.

I had arrived after Bell's learning. No parent had guided my feet around a post. No game had trained my body to stop. No seller had denied me a route because I failed to pay. I recognized the rules only as information, so the felt edge never formed for me.

Mara followed the thought farther than I did and asked whether an untaught Tavi could cross too. I did not know. Tavi might have learned from watching before anyone spoke. Vey fins might sense other bodies preparing to turn. The system could be learned, physical, and shared at once. One stable ledge did not separate those parts.

Mara said the Vey themselves were doing some of the holding. I agreed.

She looked down at Eli's stall. A customer came from the south steps, bought two fruit, and returned the way he had come. He never looked up the north road.

Mara named Anlo's crossing, Venn's nine, and her family's run. I said all remained in use through what everyone did.

I meant this as a description of force. Mara heard what it might take away.

She got off the wall. “Then Sennet did not give us anything.” I said Sennet had given them the record.

“A reminder, you said.” Mara's voice remained quiet. “My mother walks before light to sell fruit between two stones because people agree she may. Anlo takes her money because people agree he may. Venn finds ink in a wet book and people agree it matters more than hers.”

I tried to correct the size of the conclusion. Agreement was real behavior with real cost. A route could belong to a family in the system even if stone itself did not recognize the family. The market run supported their work because Bell acted on the record.

Mara reduced my correction to the part that hurt: the run belonged to them because everyone said it did. I agreed. “And if everyone says it belongs to Venn?”

I looked at the stall, the anchor road beyond it, and the gray case in Orra's office. The useful answer would have protected something Mara needed. The true answer was smaller.

“I do not know what belongs means without what people do.”

Mara stepped away from me. I saw the movement and still did not understand the wound inside it.

“You came here less than a week ago,” she said. “You crossed one line and measured one dead road. Now you're telling me the only thing my family owns is a thing other people let us call ours.”

I said this was not what I had intended. “I know.” She looked down at Eli carrying a tray to the front row. “That does not help.”

Mara descended toward the market. I could have followed at her pace. I did not. She had not asked for distance, but I had finally learned enough to see when my presence would make a question harder.

The children started another round. Their new line worked at once.

I stayed by the wall with an answer that might be true and had been given too large, too early, and to the person paying the most for it.

# Part IV: The First Walker

## Chapter 19: One Result

Mara did not speak to me through the rest of the market day. I helped Eli move fruit, carried the waste bowl to the garden bin, and stayed outside the stall whenever Mara entered it. Near dusk, the family started down toward Anlo's crossing without asking me to join them.

I followed at a distance of twenty steps. This seemed respectful until Eli turned and asked why I was behaving like a loose moon. Mara kept walking. Eli waited until I reached her, then said there were better ways to give someone room than making their mother count backward every few breaths.

I had not known Eli was counting. I moved beside her.

At the crossing, Anlo took another blue shell. The remaining family purse made a smaller sound when Eli closed it. No one discussed the sound. They climbed the last stair home, fed Tavi, and divided the damaged fruit between supper and tomorrow's tray. Eli set aside only twelve unbruised pieces for market, fewer than would have covered the front half of the display a week earlier.

Mara left after dark with the household lamp hooked over one shoulder. Nema watched through the open doorway but did not call her back. When I asked whether I could go, Mara said, “If you listen before explaining.”

I agreed and followed her to the west storage shed.

The Corran ledge was a darker shape above the lower road. Mara set the lamp on a flat stone below its first step. Its frame had four thin sides around a shallow cup of oil. One side was blackened where a wick had burned too high.

Mara lit the wick. Warm color reached the shed wall, her forefeet, and the first old post hole. She held one narrow twig above the flame until its tip darkened. Then she moved the twig to the other side of the lamp. That end remained pale.

“What happened?” she asked.

The first end had burned. I said so.

“And the whole stick?”

It had not burned. She turned it slowly between two claws. One result belonged to one place and one moment. It could support a larger idea, but it did not become every result merely because it fit.

I understood her demonstration before she said my name. I had measured one ledge for one body on one day. I had then used the children's game, the market paths, Anlo's crossing, Venn's run, and her family's record as if one explanation could finish them all.

“Some of those things may share a cause,” I said.

“May.”

I repeated the smaller word. Mara waited. The lamp flame bent west in the wind and straightened behind its frame.

I told her I had turned a possible pattern into an answer and placed it on her loss. I had spoken as if explaining how Bell worked also explained what her family deserved. It did not. Even if every marker depended on shared behavior, choosing that behavior still affected food, work, and who had to walk in the dark.

Mara's fins remained lowered, but she did not leave. “You said agreement was real.”

“I said it after making it sound unreal.”

She looked at the charred twig. “Yes.”

I wanted to tell her I had meant respect. Intent had already failed as evidence. Instead I said I was sorry for giving a large claim where I had only one safe crossing and several observations. I was also sorry for speaking about her family's run as a system before speaking about what its loss was doing to them.

Mara adjusted the wick. She did not say the apology repaired the harm. She told me what had happened while I sat above the market. Eli had asked two southern sellers whether they needed a worker. One could offer three mornings each week after the harvest ended. The other needed no help. Nema had begun sorting household tools into those they used daily and those they could sell.

The gray cord had been tied for five days.

I had counted those days as inquiry. Mara's family had lived them as shrinking choices.

She extinguished the lamp and waited while my vision adjusted. “I still need to know what the ledge does.”

I said I could describe what it had done for me. I could measure again. I could not turn either fact into a promise for her.

“Good.” Mara picked up the lamp. “Keep the answer that size.”

We started home together. At the blue-shell crossing, the planks were empty and Anlo's table was closed, but the marker still directed our feet toward the long stair. Mara stopped before it. For one breath I thought she meant to test the boundary.

She only looked west, toward the road above the road. Then she continued down.

Trust had not returned to its old shape. Mara had given me one task inside it, narrow enough to perform and important enough to fail: keep one result from pretending to be the whole.

## Chapter 20: A Shorter Line

The next morning Mara asked me to measure the full alternative without calling it an escape. We began at her door before Eli lifted the first basket. I marked the point in memory and followed the family road west instead of taking the stair toward Bell Under.

The route passed seven homes, two gardens, and the empty storage frames. At the Corran mouth, Mara stopped below the ledge. I continued alone, measured the eight hundred and thirty steps again, and reached the upper stair with the same number.

From Corran, I found a descending public path that curved back toward Bell's north market. Its markers were faded but held routes. Four travelers used them while I watched. The last stair joined the market road beyond Anlo's plankway, twenty-six steps north of Eli's stall.

The entire line from Mara's door to the stall measured one thousand four hundred and eighty walking steps. It charged no blue shell. The detour below Bell took two thousand and forty steps and crossed Anlo's table in each direction.

I returned quickly because the comparison appeared useful. Mara was still beneath the ledge with the first fruit basket between her feet. Eli and Tavi waited farther back. Eli had refused to leave for market until she knew whether Mara intended to follow me.

I gave them the three numbers. Tavi announced that one thousand four hundred and eighty was smaller. Eli said this was why the household kept Tavi available for difficult office work.

Mara asked for the route itself. I described the Corran stair, the public markers, and the market entrance. When I called both connecting stairs safe, she interrupted. “For feet?”

I reviewed my crossing. On the Corran side, one stair had lost half its outer tread. I had floated from the intact center to the landing. On the market side, a gap separated the final step from the road by a distance shorter than my width. I had crossed it without changing height.

Mara asked why those details had not been part of my first report. I had measured the places where a body could pass, and I had passed. My use of *safe* had contained my body without saying so.

I went back with Mara as far as she would travel. She remained below the unowned ledge, so I described each later stair against the old rail spacing and drew their shapes in the dust. The Corran break left room for three Vey feet if a traveler turned sideways. The market gap required a jump. A loaded Vey could make both movements. I had watched similar jumps on held routes.

“Similar,” Mara said.

I removed the word *safe* from my description. The route was physically passable according to the stone I had inspected and the movements I had seen elsewhere. It was also six hundred steps shorter than the current detour.

Eli set down her basket. She asked whether I believed Mara could cross the unowned section. I said the ledge had held for me and had remained stable through repeated measurements. I could not measure what Mara would feel or promise the same result for her.

Tavi looked from me to the road mouth. “Could it eat her?”

Nema had followed us without my noticing. She answered before anyone else did. The old stories did not speak of eating. They spoke of turning, stretching, and wrong arrivals. Mara told Tavi that none of them had watched those things happen and that not watching was different from proving they could not.

Tavi considered this. “That is a bad kind of different.”

No one corrected her.

I tried to compare the risks as quantities. Six hundred fewer steps reduced time, damaged fruit, and fatigue. Avoiding Anlo preserved two shells each market day. The ledge surface had one broken corner and no measured shift. The two later gaps could be crossed with care.

Mara asked what number I assigned to her not coming home.

I stopped. A probability would have helped, but I had too few events to make one. I had one body, one day, and no Vey crossings. Even a very small chance would be carried by Mara, Eli, Tavi, and Nema rather than by the line on my map.

“Then you don't have a comparison,” Mara said. “You have one side counted.”

She was right. Shorter did not mean chosen. Passable did not mean safe. My map could show distance and visible damage; it could not price absence.

Eli lifted the basket again. The family turned toward the long stair. They would spend six hundred extra steps and another shell to reach a stall that might not earn either cost.

Mara paused beside me before following. She thanked me for the line and refused the conclusion I had almost attached to it. Both actions belonged to her. I kept them separate.

## Chapter 21: What Waiting Costs

Eli closed the stall before midday. She had sold nine fruit and traded one damaged piece for a cup of hot water. Three nearby sellers were already gone. The cloth worker had folded her canopy; the seed seller had left an empty stone where his bins had stood.

Closing the family run had changed more than one route. Southern buyers still reached the market, but the north row no longer lay on anyone's natural path. Fewer buyers made sellers leave. Empty stalls gave the remaining buyers less reason to climb there. The loss repeated itself without a clerk tying another cord.

At home, Eli cleared the supper table and placed four objects on it: the household purse, a Corran market notice, a list of tools, and the family stall key. A seller in Corran would rent half a table for twelve days. Eli could carry the best fruit there by the low roads, sleep under the table cover, and learn whether enough buyers came.

If it worked, Mara and Tavi would follow. Nema could choose between the Corran rooms and a cousin two shelves below Bell. The family home would be rented if anyone wanted it. The north stall key would return to the market office.

Tavi asked whether all their bowls had to move. Eli said only the ones they could carry. Tavi put both forefeet around her favorite bowl.

Mara read the notice twice. Corran was close across the old ledge and days away with a loaded cart below. The half table would be gone if Eli waited for Bell's authority. The gray cord could remain for more than fifty days.

“We could take the upper road,” Mara said.

The room changed. Eli's tail struck the floor. Nema made a small sound and gripped the table so hard one claw scraped the wood. Tavi pulled the bowl against her chest.

Mara did not withdraw the sentence. She said the ledge had kept its recorded length for Dot, its stone was intact, and the upper Corran stair still connected with a held market path. She also said none of this proved what a Vey would experience.

Eli asked whether Mara intended to make herself the proof. Mara answered that she had not decided.

They argued without raising their voices. This made the fear easier to hear. Eli named every person who depended on Mara returning. Mara named every choice that would disappear while they paid for the lower route and waited. Eli said a smaller life still contained Mara. Mara said losing the stall, house, and work was not a harmless way to stay safe.

Neither claimed the other was wrong.

Nema carried Tavi and the bowl into the next room. When she returned, she placed the bowl back on the table and asked us to stop using old stories as if their age supplied a witness.

Her mother had warned her about ownerless roads. Her mother's father had warned her mother. The story said a traveler could enter a straight path and come home from the wrong direction, older than the family waiting. Sometimes the traveler was Ise Marrow. Sometimes Ise was the waiting clerk. In one version Corran lay at the far end; in another, the place had no name.

Nema had never known which telling came first. She had never met anyone who claimed to see it happen.

Mara asked why Nema had repeated the warning to her. Nema looked toward the door Tavi had gone through. “Because my mother was afraid for me, and I was afraid for you.”

Fear could be inherited without being invented. Nema's uncertainty did not make the ledge safe. It did show that the warning had changed while everyone treated it as fixed.

Eli sat with one forefoot over the stall key. The Corran notice offered three days to answer. She would accept the half table, take one trial load by the lower road, and ask the market office to hold the place. If the trial earned enough, the family would decide what followed.

That plan avoided the ledge and began breaking their life apart anyway.

Mara asked how much of the household purse the journey would use. Eli showed her: travel fees, cart rent, two nights under cover, and the table payment would take more than half. Another failed trial would leave too little for the Bell detour.

Nema's good front leg trembled as she returned the tools list to the center of the table. She could pack and watch Tavi, but she could not manage repeated lower climbs. If the family divided between two shelves, someone else would have to carry her.

I had thought waiting preserved the present while action changed it. Nothing on the table supported that thought. The office delay had already begun choosing where they worked, what they kept, and who would carry whom.

Mara turned the Corran notice over. On the blank side, she drew the old ledge as one straight line. She added no promise beside it.

“I need to ask a better question,” she said.

## Chapter 22: The Question I Could Give

Mara brought the blank side of the Corran notice to the ledge after supper. She had added the route numbers around her straight line: eight hundred and thirty for the unowned stone, one thousand four hundred and eighty from door to market, and two thousand and forty for the current detour.

She sat below the first step and asked me for a question.

I began to list the ones we had not answered. Did held distance act through learned response, Vey sensing, or both? Could the presence of a recognized owner change what a traveler perceived? Did age affect an unused route? Mara touched one claw to the paper, and I stopped.

She did not need another question about the road. She needed one she could answer.

I searched through the facts I had collected about her. Most were observations, not permissions. Mara protected her family, corrected my claims, tested evidence in pieces, and disliked decisions made for someone who would carry the result. She had crossed thousands of held distances without asking whether stone could be trusted. Here, certainty was impossible before the act that required it.

“If you step onto the ledge,” I asked, “what choice must still belong to you?”

Mara looked up. The route mouth held its ordinary shape. Wind moved grit over the first cut stone and did not move it back.

She said the choice to stop. Everyone spoke of a first walker as if beginning required finishing. She wanted to place one foot past the old post and still be allowed to return. If she crossed half the route and became afraid, she wanted that return treated as information rather than failure. If she reached Corran, she wanted the crossing to remain hers before it became Bell's example.

I asked who had been taking those choices away. Mara named no person. The story did it. Need did it. Even our measurements pressed toward an answer because everyone had begun arranging the result before she moved.

I had helped create that pressure. By mapping the shorter line, I had made refusal look like choosing six hundred extra steps. By measuring stable stone, I had made fear look like a mistake waiting for correction.

Mara said the numbers still mattered. “They just don't get feet.”

I understood. Numbers could describe the road and the cost around it. They could not walk her body into either choice.

She asked what I would do if she entered. I could travel beside her near the wall, watch the stone, count distance, and report any change. I could return with her at any point. I could carry messages, though I could not carry her if she fell. I would not tell her the route was safe merely because it remained safe for me.

Mara added one condition. If she froze, I was to ask whether she wanted to go on or go back. I was not to recite evidence unless she requested it.

I repeated the condition exactly. She asked whether I could promise to follow it when the answer seemed obvious.

“The answer has seemed obvious to me several times,” I said. “That has not made it yours.”

The corner of her mouth moved. It was not the easy smile from the blue tree, but it was the first movement toward one since the market wall.

Mara folded the notice along the line she had drawn. She would come before the morning sky brightened, when the west road was empty. Eli would be loading fruit. Tavi and Nema would still be inside. She planned to test one step beyond the first post, then decide again.

I asked whether Eli knew.

“No.” Mara watched my reaction. I had promised that the earlier crossing result belonged to her until she chose to share it. This plan involved more than information; if Mara failed to return, silence would become part of the harm.

I said I would accompany her, but I could not help conceal a crossing once it began. Someone should know where she was.

Mara disliked the limit. She also accepted it. She would leave Eli a note naming the ledge and time, sealed under the household lamp. Eli would find it only after Mara had gone unless she woke early.

That did not remove the family's risk or consent to it. It gave them the truth soon enough to act.

We walked home. Mara kept the folded notice. I kept no conclusion about what morning would prove.

## Chapter 23: Before the First Step

Mara arrived before the light reached the west wall. She had left the folded notice under the lamp as promised. She carried no fruit, cord, or market bag. The absence of those objects made the attempt harder to mistake for work.

We climbed to the ledge mouth. Mara placed all four feet on the broad starting stone below the first old post hole. The route ahead was pale enough to see and dark enough that its far curve disappeared early.

I moved beside the wall and waited.

Mara lifted one front foot. Her other three feet shifted to support it. The raised foot traveled forward until its light patch crossed the line of the missing post. Then it stopped above the ledge.

Nothing in the stone changed. Grit continued moving in the wind. I did not report either fact.

Her foot shook. She lowered it behind the line, breathed, and tried again. The second attempt stopped in the same place. On the third, her tail struck the wall hard enough to scatter dust from an old rail hole.

I asked the promised question. Did she want to go on or go back?

“Back.”

We descended together. Mara sat below the cut step with her fins tight against her head. She had not entered the ownerless distance. She had also done exactly what she claimed the right to do: stopped, answered, and returned.

That did not keep disappointment from reaching her. She said the ledge had beaten her without moving. I reminded her that she had told me not to turn evidence into a larger answer. One attempt could not prove the whole of her courage any more than my crossing proved the whole road.

Mara gave me a tired look. “You waited until after I stopped to explain.”

I had. She said that part was correct.

Footsteps sounded on the lower road. Eli came around the storage shed carrying Mara's folded notice. The household lamp cord was still looped around one wrist. She must have found the paper almost as soon as we left.

Eli looked at Mara's feet, the clean ledge beyond them, and me. She asked whether Mara had gone out. Mara said no.

Relief moved through Eli's body so visibly that her front legs lowered. She did not say she had been right. She sat beside Mara and leaned one shoulder against hers.

After a long time, Mara asked why Eli had not called from the road. Eli said she had wanted to. She had imagined ordering Mara home, blocking the first step, and sending for Orra. Each action might have stopped the crossing. Each might also have made the next attempt something Mara did in secret.

“I was afraid an argument would choose for you,” Eli said. “I was also afraid it wouldn't.”

Both fears had brought her to the same place: close enough to know, far enough not to command.

Mara said she could not cross when the whole road seemed to be waiting for one hidden moment. If she succeeded before dawn, people could say Dot had carried her, that the ledge had changed only in darkness, or that she had never gone. If she failed, only three bodies would hold the failure, and she would keep trying to erase it.

She wanted the next attempt at midday, when Bell's ordinary traffic could witness the beginning without turning the road into a private trial.

Eli's shoulder left hers. “With people watching?”

Mara said yes. Orra should see the start and record only what she observed. Venn should be invited because any open route would affect the pending run. Eli, Nema, and Tavi could decide for themselves whether to attend. No one would be permitted onto the ledge behind her.

I asked why witnesses would make the step easier rather than harder. Mara looked toward Bell, where windows were beginning to show morning light. “Because then it isn't a dare I make against myself. It is a thing I choose where everyone can see what choosing costs.”

Eli did not approve. She said those exact words so Mara would not later turn silence into consent. Then she agreed to carry the invitations.

The distinction mattered to both of them.

Mara stood. Her legs still trembled. She went home to help load fruit for another long detour, because deciding on a public attempt did not remove the market day before it.

At the storage shed, she stopped once and looked back. The line she had failed to cross remained visible only because every Vey who knew the warning could place it.

At midday, Mara intended to place herself there too, then ask again whether to go on or go back.

## Chapter 24: Where the Ground Stops

At midday, eleven Vey waited below the Corran ledge. Eli had invited four. Orra came with a narrow writing board strapped across her good shoulder. Venn arrived alone. Anlo followed after closing his table for the midday rest, and three sellers came because they had seen the others turn west.

Nema stayed home with Tavi. She had chosen that limit in the morning and asked Eli not to describe it as weakness. Tavi sent her favorite bowl with Mara because she believed a person returning from somewhere should bring an object back. Mara placed the bowl beside the first step.

Orra stated what she could witness: Mara's entry, return if visible, and any measured time. She could not certify the route, create a holder, or declare a crossing legal under a category Bell did not have. Venn asked whether his pending claim would be affected. Orra said the office would decide that later.

“Then this isn't part of my claim,” Venn said.

Mara answered before Orra could. “It is part of my day.” Venn lowered his fins and moved back.

The three uninvited sellers whispered near the storage wall. Eli told them they could watch if they stayed off the cut steps and did not advise Mara. Anlo objected to being given the same rule as a market child, then obeyed it.

Mara climbed. I took my place between her and the cliff wall, leaving the open edge clear. Orra called the start onto her board but did not begin the time.

The missing first marker had left a square hole at the outer edge. Every Vey below knew the unowned road began there. No rope, paint, cord, or change in stone marked the boundary. It still stopped Mara's raised foot in the same place as before dawn.

I asked whether she wanted to go on or go back.

“Wait with me.”

Waiting was one of the choices she had named. I held my position. Below us, even the whispers stopped.

Mara asked me to describe the stone beneath her lifted foot. The surface lay one hand lower than her other front foot because the ledge sloped slightly toward the wall. It was solid, dry, and covered by a thin layer of grit. No crack crossed the landing point. The nearest edge was farther than the length of her lower leg. She said that description did not match what her body reported.

On a held road, Mara could sense the next placement before her foot landed. She did not see a line or hear a command. The route's shape arrived through her fins and legs as a quiet continuation, a certainty about where her weight could go. At the missing marker, that continuation ended. The stone remained visible, but her body received no place from it.

“It feels like putting a foot into a word no one finished,” she said.

I had mistaken the problem when I called the boundary invisible. Mara perceived an absence I did not. To me, the cut stone offered every usual measurement. To her, those measurements ended before they became footing.

I asked whether my report helped. She said yes, but helping did not make the missing sense appear.

Her lifted leg began to shake. Eli moved one foot below and stopped herself. Orra's pen remained above the board. Venn looked at the empty marker hole rather than Mara.

I could repeat eight hundred and thirty, solid stone, and two completed crossings. Mara had not asked. The useful thing I knew now was not another fact about the ledge.

“You can put your foot back,” I said. “I will go home with you.”

Mara closed her eyes. Her raised foot moved backward a fraction. Then she opened them and looked down at Eli.

Eli's body leaned toward her. She did not speak.

Mara asked me what I would do if the stone changed. I would tell her, return beside her, and seek the nearest stable surface. I could not promise to hold her body. I could promise not to continue alone and call that accompaniment.

She asked whether the route continued for me now. I measured the first ten steps. They had the same width and interval as before. “For me, now, yes.”

Mara breathed out. “Keep the answer that size.”

Then she lowered her foot beyond the missing marker.

The light patch touched grit. Her claws spread. Weight moved through the leg slowly, then all at once. The ledge did not lengthen, fold, or disappear. Mara's other three feet remained behind the line.

Orra's pen touched the board.

Mara stood with one foot on an ownerless road. Fear remained in her body. So did choice.

# Part V: The Open Distance

## Chapter 25: Around the Curve

Mara kept her first foot weighted for twelve breaths. She lifted it once, set it in the same print, then shifted a second foot beyond the marker. When the other two followed, Orra called the start time.

No one below cheered. Eli had forbidden it, and the silence suited the work better.

Mara walked five steps and stopped. The ledge remained wide enough for her stance, but she kept her body close to the wall. I moved beside her at the height of her working fin.

“Go on or go back?” I asked.

She chose ten more steps. We repeated the question there. Her answer carried us to the first intact rail pin, then the next empty hole. Each choice stayed small enough to return from.

At fifty steps, Mara looked back. The witnesses had begun to seem like one clustered body below the route mouth. Eli stood ahead of them with the empty bowl at her feet. Orra's pale board reflected the midday light.

Mara asked me to count aloud. I gave each step number without adding what it meant. At one hundred, she paused. At two hundred, the wall curved and hid all but the upper edge of the storage shed.

The last sight of Eli disappeared at two hundred and eighteen.

Mara stopped so suddenly that her rear foot struck the front one. Her body lowered. The route's missing continuation had not changed, but the visible way home was gone too. Stone extended ahead and behind while her sense supplied neither direction as a place to put weight.

I asked our question.

“Wait.”

We waited. Wind moved through the rail holes. Far below, pale air filled the space where Bell's lower shelves should have been visible. Mara pressed one shoulder against the cliff and asked for the measurement behind us.

Two hundred and eighteen steps to the west post. The width at her feet differed from the starting width by less than one claw. The route behind remained continuous to the curve. Those were my current results.

Mara asked whether I could still locate Eli. I could locate the route mouth from our counted turns, but I could not see through stone. “Then don't say she's there,” Mara said.

I corrected myself. Eli had been there when the curve hid her. I expected her to remain, but I did not know.

Mara's breathing slowed. She chose five more steps.

After the curve, our progress changed. She no longer asked at every post hole. She asked at three hundred, then four hundred. I reported width and counted distance when requested. At a loose patch, she tested each foot before transferring weight. At the broken outer corner, she turned sideways as we had planned and kept three feet on the inner stone while one crossed.

Fear did not leave. It became work her body performed while moving.

At six hundred steps, Mara asked what I thought would happen at the Corran end. I said the held upper stair had supplied direction to the four travelers I watched. I did not know how the change would feel to her.

“I hope it feels rude,” she said. “I would like to be annoyed instead.”

The stone widened at seven hundred. Corran roofs appeared above the shelf. Mara saw them and laughed once, a sound with too much breath in it.

Near the first stair, I showed her the cut sign. The circle surrounded its single central point, weathered but complete. Mara moved close enough to touch the outer groove.

“It looks like you,” she said.

I had noticed that. I told her the part I had kept private: the sign felt familiar even though no memory came with it. I did not know whether it represented me, another being shaped like me, a place, or nothing related.

Mara lowered her foot from the mark. She could have objected to my silence. Instead she said one unknown was enough for the crossing and we would return to mine later.

The Corran stair began eight hundred and thirty steps from Bell's west post. When Mara placed her front foot on its held surface, her entire body jerked. Both fins rose. She took another step, then backed onto the ledge and stepped forward again.

The sense of footing had returned. She described it as a hand she had not known was touching her until it touched her again. The stair did not feel stronger than the ledge. It felt finished.

Mara climbed to the first landing and lay down with all four legs spread. I did not count the time until she asked. She rested for seventy-four breaths.

A loose dark pebble lay near the stair wall. Mara picked it up for Tavi and carried it in her mouth to the ledge edge. She had reached Corran. The public attempt could end there if she waited for someone to come by the lower route.

Instead she looked back at the unowned road. A one-way result would leave Eli on the other side and place the family choice exactly where it had been, except with more fear added.

“We go back,” she said.

The held sensation vanished again when her first foot left the Corran stair. Mara flinched, but the foot landed. We counted down from eight hundred and thirty because she wanted the number home to become smaller.

At the blind curve, she stopped for nine breaths. I asked our question. Mara chose to continue.

Bell returned one piece at a time: roof edge, storage frame, Orra's board, the empty bowl, and Eli. The waiting Vey saw Mara before she saw their full bodies. A sound rose below, quickly silenced by Eli, then rose again because Mara had begun laughing.

She still had more than two hundred steps to cross. I kept counting, because visible did not yet mean returned.

## Chapter 26: What the Witnesses Saw

At one hundred steps, Mara's laugh ended. The old marker hole came into view, and with it the place where her body expected the road to stop. Moving from the ownerless side did not erase the boundary. Her pace shortened as we approached.

The witnesses spread along the lower road so everyone could see. No one climbed to meet her. Eli had kept that rule even after Mara appeared.

At ten steps, Mara halted. The west post stood behind the waiting crowd rather than in front of it, and the route beyond the post carried the sense of held footing again. She told me the return felt stranger: the finished place pulled at her while the unfinished stone still supported all four feet.

I gave her the same choice: go on or go back.

Mara looked over her shoulder toward the blind curve. “Go on.”

She crossed the missing marker one foot at a time. When the last rear foot reached held stone, her legs folded. She did not fall; she chose the floor before her body chose it for her.

Eli climbed only after Mara called her name. She stopped close enough to touch and asked permission anyway. Mara leaned against her before finishing the answer.

For several breaths, neither spoke. Mara's body shook against Eli's. Eli's tail wrapped across Mara's back and pressed her toward the stone.

Then Mara opened her mouth and dropped the Corran pebble into Tavi's empty bowl.

The sound was small. Every witness heard it.

Orra recorded the return time. The round trip had taken much longer than an ordinary sixteen-hundred-sixty-step walk, because the board included pauses, tests, and the rest at Corran. She wrote those facts beside the times rather than smoothing the attempt into a faster story.

The three sellers began asking at once. Had the road tried to turn? Had Corran appeared in the correct place? Did the ledge now feel held? Could they carry goods over it before evening?

Mara remained against Eli while she answered. Corran had appeared where the record placed it. The route had stayed eight hundred and thirty steps in each direction. Its surface had supported her. It never began to feel held. She had been frightened before the first step, beyond the curve, at the Corran return, and at the final marker.

One seller said that meant it was safe. Mara raised both fins. “It means I crossed.”

Orra repeated the distinction. The office had witnessed one Vey travel out and back on one date, accompanied by me. That observation belonged in the file. It was not a holding record, a maintenance inspection, or permission for public traffic.

Anlo asked what more anyone required from a road that went where it should. Orra named rails, surface inspection, entry rules, responsibility for injuries, and a legal category. Anlo said stone did not wait for categories. Orra replied that injured Vey still needed someone to answer the door.

Venn had not moved from his place near the shed. He asked Mara whether the ledge felt different after she knew it had carried her once. She considered the question rather than treating it as a challenge. The return was not easier because the road felt safer. It was easier because she knew fear could travel with her and did not have to decide the direction.

Venn wrote that sentence on the back of one nine-ring band. He did not explain why.

The first seller stepped toward the cut stair. Eli blocked him with her tail. Mara had crossed for herself, not opened a race. Until Orra inspected the route, anyone following would use the same uncertainty with less preparation and no agreement about who would accompany them.

The seller objected that Mara had no right to close an unowned distance. Mara agreed. She also had no power to make him careful. She asked him to wait one day because he had watched the cost instead of hearing only the ending.

He waited.

That was the first change created by the crossing. It did not make the ledge safe. It made one Vey's request stronger than the old warning for long enough to allow another inspection.

At home, Nema met Mara in the doorway. She touched Mara's face, front legs, sides, and tail as if checking a delivery against a list. Mara submitted until Nema reached the second rear foot, then asked whether any part remained unchecked.

“The part that makes decisions,” Nema said.

“That part is tired.”

Tavi carried the bowl to the table. The pebble was ordinary dark stone, flatter on one side where weather had rested it against the stair. She asked if it had been afraid too. Mara said she had no evidence of that.

Tavi put the pebble beside her spoon so it would not eat alone.

Mara slept before supper. When she woke, she told Eli she felt three things and did not want any of them corrected: safe at home, still scared of the road, and relieved she had crossed.

Eli accepted all three. The next morning, the gray cord would still close their family run. The purse would still be light. Orra would still need to decide what one witnessed crossing allowed her to do.

But the blank Corran page was no longer the only local evidence. Bell had watched a person enter the unfinished feeling and return with a stone.

## Chapter 27: Orra's Measure

Orra returned the next morning with a measuring cord, a stone hammer, three red ties, and no writing board. She had copied Mara's witnessed crossing into the office file before sleeping. Now she meant to inspect the ledge as a clerk responsible for distances rather than as a witness responsible for one event.

Her shoulder made the supply strap hang unevenly. Mara offered to carry it. Orra accepted the help as far as the first step, then took the strap back.

“If you carry my tools, this becomes your crossing again,” she said.

Mara moved aside.

Orra wanted no one on the ledge with her. She would use the old rail holes as measurement points, test the repaired places named in Ise Marrow's record, and stop if the surface failed under the hammer. I could observe from the route mouth until the curve, but my measurements would not enter hers unless she requested a comparison afterward.

That condition removed the work I was best able to do. I agreed because independence was the purpose.

Venn arrived while Orra tied the cord around her middle. No invitation had been sent. He stood below the storage wall with all nine ring bands visible and asked no questions.

Orra stepped beyond the missing post.

Her first response resembled Mara's: legs lowered, fins flattened, and one front foot searched twice before accepting stone. After the second rail hole, she established a pattern. She drove a red tie into each hundred-step interval, stretched the cord along the wall side, and struck the ledge ahead of her before transferring weight.

The hammer's sound traveled back after Orra disappeared around the curve. A clear strike, a pause, then another. The interval changed whenever she measured. None of us could see whether the route still led to Corran.

Mara stood where Eli had stood the day before. She did not ask me to locate Orra through the rock.

The hammer stopped for thirty-seven breaths. Mara's tail lifted. Venn left the wall and moved closer to the first stair, but he remained below it. The next strike came lower and duller, followed by two clear ones.

I could have crossed to learn what happened. Orra had required an independent inspection, and she had not called. I stayed.

Venn asked Mara whether waiting felt worse after knowing the road could work. Mara said it felt more specific. Yesterday she had feared what the ledge might do. Today she feared a loose stone at one known corner, Orra's shaking shoulder, and each silence between hammer blows.

He asked if that was better. “Ask me when she comes back.”

At the time an ordinary walker should have reached Corran, the hammer remained silent. Orra had warned us not to treat her inspection like ordinary travel. She would test every rail base, measure the wide places, and rest her shoulder before returning.

Venn counted the nine bands on his neck with one claw. I did not know whether this motion meant worry, calculation, habit, or nothing. Mara saw it too and did not assign a reason.

The first hammer strike on the return sounded after twice the ordinary crossing time. The intervals arrived in reverse. When the sound stopped at the blind curve, Mara closed her eyes but kept her feet still.

Orra appeared carrying one red tie instead of three. Dust covered her legs. A dark patch marked the strap where her shoulder had rubbed it. She crossed the last hundred steps without looking down at us.

At the post, she stopped with all four feet still on the ledge. She unwound the measuring cord and compared its final knot with the square hole. Only then did she step onto held stone.

Mara asked whether she was hurt. Orra said her shoulder hurt exactly as much as it had expected to, which was different from no.

Her inspection found the route eight hundred and thirty steps long. The two missing red ties remained at the broken outer corner and a hollow-sounding patch beneath an old rail base. The corner still left sufficient inner width. The hollow patch required a surface repair before loaded traffic. Neither defect altered the destination or length.

Orra had also inspected the Corran stair and the circle-and-dot cut. She had no record of the sign. Its placement appeared older than the last repair entries, but stone wear could not give her a reliable date.

Venn asked what she would write. Orra answered that she would petition the shelf authority to create a public category for a distance with no private holder. The route could not honestly be transferred from Ise Marrow, because there was no proven person transferring it. It could not honestly be claimed as newly discovered, because the road and record were old.

“And the pending run?” Venn asked.

Orra said the two decisions were separate. His record could prevail over Sennet's and the public ledge could still exist. The family might lose the 412-step holding while Bell gained a free 1,480-step route. One answer would not be used to disguise the other.

Venn touched the lowest ring band. His fins showed nothing I could name with confidence.

Orra asked Mara to carry the tool strap back to the office. The independent crossing was finished. Mara lifted the weight onto her shoulder without making the inspection hers.

## Chapter 28: A New Blank

The shelf authority answered nineteen days after Orra sent the petition. Those days did not pass as a pause.

Eli took the Corran half table by the lower road. The trial sold enough fruit to justify a second load and too little to cover moving the household. Cart rent, sleeping cover, and travel fees consumed most of the difference. She returned with a bruise beneath the supply strap and three customers who promised to buy again if she stayed.

The Bell stall opened only four mornings during that time. Mara and Eli could not keep walking the long detour and still harvest, sort, carry, and sell. Nema traded two stored tools for meal grain. Tavi stopped asking when the gray cord would come off and began asking which shelf they would live on.

The Corran ledge also changed. Orra hired two stone workers with emergency office funds. They packed the hollow patch beneath the old rail base and cut the broken outer corner into a wider inner tread. The red ties stayed in place because repair did not create a legal route.

Mara crossed three more times while the work was underway, each time by choice and with another adult waiting at both ends. The first seller who had agreed to wait accompanied her on the third. He returned frightened, embarrassed by his fear, and carrying a list of places where he wanted a rail.

Mara told him embarrassment was not part of the inspection.

Orra included his list anyway.

On the nineteenth morning, an authority courier delivered a flat packet closed by two green knots. Orra sent for everyone named in the open files: Eli, Mara, Venn, and the Corran clerk. The Corran clerk joined by speaking through a paired tube set into the office wall. His voice arrived thin but understandable.

The authority had not named an owner for the ledge. It had created a new kind of record.

An *open distance* connected two public points without granting a private holding between them. Its markers would list measured length, inspected hazards, and the office responsible for upkeep. No person could charge for the distance itself. Travelers could use it without claiming that its missing held sensation had changed.

The decision required a current inspection, two witnessed crossings by Vey, notice at both ends, and public maintenance. Orra's report supplied the inspection. Mara and the seller supplied the witnessed crossings. The repair costs would be divided between Bell and Corran because both market roads gained the connection.

Orra read the final limit twice. Opening the route did not certify that every ownerless distance was stable. It established a method for testing one and recording the result.

Mara looked at me when the sentence ended. One result remained one result, even when an authority built a category around it.

The Corran clerk agreed to place the far notice. Orra opened a fresh volume rather than writing beneath Ise Marrow's name. On the first page, she entered the endpoints, eight-hundred-thirty-step measure, repair sites, inspection date, and witness names.

She left the holder field blank.

Then she drew a box around the blank and labeled it *not required*.

The blank no longer meant a missing person who had to be found. It meant the route belonged to no private record by design.

Venn asked whether anyone could later claim it. Orra read the restriction: an open distance could be closed for damage, but it could not become a private holding without a public review at both endpoints. Use alone would create no ownership.

Eli asked when the route could open. Orra answered that notices and rails would take two days. The family could not recover the nineteen days, the sold tools, the lost buyers, or the lower-road fees. The decision affected what came next; it did not repair what had already happened.

Before closing the office, Orra brought out nine narrow files. Each ended the way Corran's old volume had ended: a named holder, a death or disappearance, and no later transfer. Two were garden stairs now blocked by growth. One had collapsed. Six had not been inspected within living memory.

The new category did not open them. It made their blanks into questions an office could ask without inventing an heir.

Orra tied no cord around the files. She placed them in a separate tray marked *review*.

Two days later, Bell and Corran set matching notices at the ledge. Each showed the distance, the repaired tread, the missing rail sections, and the name of the two offices responsible for maintenance. A low rail guarded the hollow patch. The first marker carried a green band rather than a family color.

Orra invited Mara to uncover it. Mara declined. She had supplied a crossing, not gained ownership of the road or its opening. Orra removed the cover herself.

Vey gathered below the steps, but most did not enter. The new marker told their minds the distance was public. It did not immediately teach their bodies what footing should feel like without a holder.

Mara went first because she needed to reach the market. Eli followed with a half basket. At the missing post, both stopped. Mara waited until Eli named her choice, then they stepped onto the open distance separately.

The green band did not hold them. Their feet did.

## Chapter 29: The Road They Lost

The authority decided the 412-step claim six days after the open distance began carrying market traffic. Orra posted the hearing notice at both ends of the closed run and placed copies on Eli's stall and Venn's nearest marker.

This time the office was full before we arrived. The new route had not erased interest in the old one. Four hundred twelve steps remained far shorter than one thousand four hundred and eighty, and a recognized holding could be rented, inherited, repaired, and used as security for trade.

Orra read the ruling without making us wait through the whole record again. Venn's document named the same endpoints, stated a valid transfer, and placed the holding direction in the form current law required. The Sennet sheet described the reverse direction and supplied no later correction. Age alone did not make its wording prevail.

The run belonged to Venn.

Mara's front feet stayed still. Eli lowered her head once. Neither argued. They had already given the office every fact they possessed, and the new road had not turned those facts into a stronger claim.

Venn accepted the record. He did not look pleased, guilty, or triumphant in a way I could identify. He asked Orra whether the gray cord could come down immediately.

It could. Orra removed the closure knot and returned the old anchor to use. Then she fastened Venn's band to the marker. The road became passable again, but passage now required his terms rather than the family's.

Eli watched the new color settle in the wind. Her family had not regained a road. They had gained another way to reach their work.

That distinction stayed painful even when the new way helped.

On the first full market morning after the ruling, Mara and Eli left home with two baskets and entered the Corran ledge. The green marker did not remove the unfinished feeling. Eli stopped at the first post and nearly returned. Mara asked the question I had once asked her, and Eli chose five steps.

They reached the stall by the 1,480-step line without paying Anlo or Venn. Three Corran buyers arrived behind them. Two sellers who had left the north row reopened their canopies because traffic now entered from that side. By midday, Eli's front tray was empty.

The change repeated over the next week. Corran growers carried goods into Bell. Bell sellers tried the route in groups. Some turned back at the first marker and used the lower roads. Others crossed slowly, resting at the new rail. No one charged them for the distance.

Anlo lost part of his plankway trade. He responded by lowering the blue-shell fee for early sellers and adding a covered rest bench beside his crossing. He did not call this generosity. He called it keeping customers.

Venn's 412-step run attracted travelers who valued speed or disliked the ownerless sensation. He set a price below Anlo's old fee and hired one worker to check the markers each morning. The award had value. The open road had limited how much value he could extract from need.

Eli declined Venn's offer of free family passage for the first twelve days. She said she would not use a gift while she was deciding whether it was kindness, apology, or an attempt to make the hearing easier to remember. Venn accepted the refusal.

The family kept the Bell home and stall. They did not recover the two tools Nema had traded. The Corran table owner retained part of Eli's payment under the cancellation terms. One southern buyer never returned. Loss survived beside recovery.

I found Venn at the old anchor after market. He had removed one neck band for cleaning and held it between two claws. Nine lighter circles marked the places where the bands usually rested.

He asked what I thought the rings meant.

I first answered with what I could verify. Each band corresponded to a recorded holding. Together they told Vey that offices recognized nine distances under his name. They affected where people walked, what they paid, and what Venn had to maintain.

“That is what they do,” he said. “What do they mean?”

The question tried to make me judge the person from the sign. I had done something similar when I turned Mara's run into an example of agreement. I kept this answer smaller.

“They mean nine records are attached to you,” I said. “What you do with that attachment means more, and I have not seen all of it.”

Venn rotated the loose band. He asked whether I believed he should have refused the award. I said the record supported him under Bell's rules. Refusal would have changed the result for Mara's family, but I could not call it required while pretending the holding had no value to him.

He looked toward the green marker on the higher road. “You make fairness sound unfinished.”

It was. One correct record had harmed a family. One brave crossing had not restored their ownership. One useful public route had reduced Venn's advantage without making his claim false.

Venn put the ninth band back around his neck. I still did not know whether he wore the rings as pride, duty, defense, or all three. The question had made that uncertainty more precise, which was the honest size of what I knew.

## Chapter 30: The Mark Ahead

Nema asked to see the sign on the last morning I spent at Bell. She could not manage the Corran ledge, so Mara drew it on a scrap from the old market notice: one circle, one point at the center.

Nema rotated the scrap between her claws. Tavi rotated her body to match it until Eli told her the drawing would remain visible without help.

The sign reminded Nema of a story she could not place. She might have heard it as a child, invented it after seeing me, or joined two older stories because they now appeared to fit. She made us agree to those possibilities before she continued.

In the story, a traveler came from outside the known shelves. It had no feet and used no held roads. Some tellings made it a light. Others made it a hole through which distant places could be seen. It left a circle with a point wherever a journey began, or wherever one ended; Nema could not remember which.

Mara asked whether Ise Marrow had seen the traveler. Nema did not know. The name Ise belonged to so many versions of the ownerless-road warning that it might have attached itself later. She could not remember whether the circle had been in the story before she saw Mara's drawing.

Tavi solved the difficulty by declaring that the mark looked like me and that I came from outside. Nema told her resemblance was not a record.

“It can still be a clue,” Tavi said.

No one corrected that.

I examined Nema's account for what it gave me. A possible traveler. A possible sign. No date, witness, direction, or stable wording. It did not tell me where I began. It did tell me the Corran carving might belong to a question older than Bell's empty ledge.

I had stayed because Mara's family faced an unfinished result that my crossing had helped expose. Their stall now opened each market day. The home no longer sat under a moving plan. Orra had nine review files and a method instead of a rumor. The remaining questions at Bell belonged to people who could carry them forward.

My question pointed outward again.

When I said I would leave, Eli placed one fruit in front of me. She remembered before I had to explain and removed it. Then she put it back. “It is a farewell, not a meal.”

I accepted the purpose even though I could not carry the fruit for long. Tavi ate it on my behalf and gave me the stem. The stem had no use either, which made the exchange consistent.

Nema told me to keep uncertainty in its proper shape. She had spent much of her life treating a repeated warning as a witnessed fact. She did not want her new story to become another road that stopped someone merely because no one remembered building it.

Eli thanked me for helping her family and blamed me for several terrible days. I thanked her for saying both. She touched the place where a shoulder would have been if I possessed one.

Mara walked with me toward Corran. At the green marker, two Vey carrying cloth waited behind her. The public notice, rail, and maintenance names were all in place. Mara still stopped.

Her front foot rose and searched above the first ownerless stone. The missing continuation had not vanished after repeated crossings. She asked the cloth carriers to leave room rather than crowd her. Then she counted three breaths and stepped on.

They followed at their own pace.

Mara no longer needed me to count every step, but she asked for the hundreds. At the blind curve, she stopped and looked back even though Bell was already hidden. She chose to continue before I asked.

At Corran, the cut circle and point waited beside the stair. Mara laid Nema's drawing over it. The shapes matched in design but not in proportion; the stone circle was wider, and its center sat slightly low.

“One result,” Mara said.

I agreed. One resemblance, one uncertain story, and one familiarity without a memory.

She asked whether I was leaving because of the sign. It was part of the direction, but not the whole. I had arrived at Bell with no name for myself beyond my shape and no history I could reach. Bell had not supplied those answers. It had shown me that an unknown could be entered without pretending the result was guaranteed.

Mara said that sounded like something I had learned from her. I said yes.

Beyond Corran's upper homes, the shelf narrowed to a pale lip over open space. This edge had no Venn post, blue-shell table, or green band. Wind crossed it without turning. Farther out, I could feel the thin pull that had carried me to Bell, a direction through the space between settled places.

Mara could travel no farther. Her world offered no stone there, unfinished or otherwise.

She asked our question one last time. Did I want to go on or go back?

I looked toward Bell. From Corran, its shelves overlapped into one dark shape cut with paths I could no longer separate. Somewhere inside that shape, Eli was arranging fruit, Tavi was guarding a pebble from loneliness, Nema was keeping a story uncertain, Orra was opening a review file, and Venn's nine rings were changing what people did without telling me everything they meant.

I wanted to go on.

Mara did not tell me the space was safe. She told me she would remain until I disappeared from sight.

I gave her Tavi's fruit stem because I had reached the limit of how long I could pretend to carry it usefully. Mara laughed and tucked it behind one fin. Then she said my name.

The invitation inside it remained.

I moved past the shelf edge. Corran fell behind me, though I could still see Mara's small shape against the stone. The between-place had no road I could measure against steps. I traveled by the pull, checking my direction whenever the light of the shelves shifted.

Mara stayed until distance reduced her to a point. Then the curve of pale space hid Corran.

There was no sound, marker, or record around me. I did not feel lost in the same way I had before Bell. Being unable to name the destination had stopped meaning that every direction was equal.

Near the far end of the between-place, another world gathered out of brightness. Dark ground separated from pale air. A vertical face turned toward me as the distance closed.

A circle had been cut into it.

One point waited at the center.

Familiarity reached me again, exact and incomplete. I did not make it an answer. I measured the remaining distance and went closer.
