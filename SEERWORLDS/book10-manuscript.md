## Chapter 1: Arriving in an Argument

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to nine worlds. On the last one I found out something enormous about all of them, and I came here still turning it over.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I came out into air that was *thick*.

---

That was the first thing and I could not do anything with it.

There was nothing in the air. I checked. No dust, no damp, no smoke, nothing floating in it, and the light came through as clean as light comes through anywhere.

But every sound in that place arrived *late*. Not much. Enough that a door shutting forty paces off came to me a beat after the door had finished shutting, soft and close and slightly behind itself. For about a minute I thought something had gone wrong with me.

*Thick* was the word that turned up. I want to be clear that nothing about me was touching anything. It was the only word that fit what I was hearing.

Then I looked at where I was.

I was over a dish of low ground at the bottom of a slope, and there was a town on the slope above it, going up and away, and the low part was crowded. Houses jammed against houses. Roofs so flat and so wide that from above they looked like a floor with cracks in it. Everything down there was low and everything up the hill was tall, and I have been on enough worlds to know that this usually means something.

The light was going orange at the edges. It was late in a day.

And it was quiet, except that quiet is not the right word for it, and I did not have the right one.

---

Two people were arguing in the street directly below me, and they had been arguing before I arrived, and they went on arguing while I came down.

They were flat.

That is the thing to say first. Broad and low and wider than they were tall, with no neck at all. The body just carried on and became the front end. A rim of short legs underneath. Two blunt grasping limbs at the front. And the whole upper surface a wide pale membrane, faintly ridged, like the top of a leaf.

No faces. Nothing anywhere on them that was for hearing with.

They were shouting anyway, and I could hear it perfectly.

“It’s the *draw*,” said the first one. “It has been the draw since the year they put it in and you have said it was the weather every year since and every year you say it a bit louder.”

“I say it a bit louder,” said the second one, “because you keep not listening to it.”

“Four summers.”

“Four bad summers. Still-less summers, all four, and you know they were, because you complained about them at the time.”

“Because of the draw.”

“Because they were still-less.”

I came down between them to listen, because I could hear both of them equally well from there, and it seemed the efficient place to be.

They both stopped and told me to move.

Not angrily. They said it as you would to somebody who had sat down on your coat. The first one said *move*, and the second one said *you’re in it*, and neither of them appeared surprised to see a small floating thing they had never met, which was a novelty for me.

I moved. They looked at me for a moment longer than the situation required.

“What is it?” said the second one.

“How would I know what it is.”

“You know everything else.”

“I know about the *draw*,” said the first one, with enormous dignity, and went back to it.

---

Their names were Sab and Ren.

What I had, standing there, was that I had been rude, and that the rudeness had nothing to do with sight lines. They had not been unable to see past me.

I went along the street to test it.

Six or seven of them were working on the low side, spread out. One at the far end said something short to one in the middle, and the one in the middle answered without turning, and neither was facing the other.

Then somebody up the slope dropped a load of something metal.

The whole row flinched.

Not at the noise. I heard the noise. It was an ordinary clatter, the kind of noise you would look up at and then stop caring about. They flinched the way you would flinch if somebody shoved the table you were leaning on. Two of them actually moved a step, away from the direction it had come from, and one of them said something extremely rude about the people up the hill, and they all settled back down.

I thought: *they felt that.*

Then I went and looked at a wall for a while. I had come out of a crossing carrying the largest fact I have ever carried, and within an hour I had put it down somewhere and gone to watch a stranger flinch.

I am not going to pretend I noticed that at the time.

---

The child found me at the wall.

She was a third the size of the adults and twice as loud, and she came down the street at a speed none of them appeared to have, and stopped in front of me in a way that suggested she had been sent to do something else.

“You’re the floating thing.”

“Yes.”

“Sab said there was a floating thing and Ren said there wasn’t.”

“There is.”

“I know,” she said. “I’m looking at it.” She went around me once, close. “What are you for?”

Nobody has ever asked me that and got an answer, and I have stopped being embarrassed about it.

“I don’t know.”

She thought about this for slightly less than a second.

“All right,” she said.

And that was the whole of it. On nine worlds I have watched people take that answer badly: laugh at it, be sorry for me, decide I was lying. This one took it the way you take the weather.

Her name was Ip. She was nine. She informed me of both of those facts immediately and then asked whether I wanted to know about the rim, and did not wait.

---

I got enough from Ip by dark to stop being completely lost. It came in the wrong order, and a good deal of it came wrong.

The low place is the Hollow. The town above it is Cairn Down and does not think about the Hollow much. Two hundred people in the dish, and it is the wet end, the crowded end, the end everything runs down into.

“It’s thick here,” she said. “That’s why.”

“Why what?”

“Why we’re here and they’re up there.” She said it as though explaining why a river is wet. “It runs down. Everything runs down. We get it.”

*It* was silence. And they did not mean the absence of anything. They meant the thing itself, a substance with weight, that settles and pools and finds the bottom of the bowl, and the bottom of the bowl was them.

Sound pushes it out. I had been shown that twice already and had not put it together.

“You’re bad at it,” Ip told me, with approval. “You just go where you like.”

“Is that bad?”

“It’s *rude*,” she said, delighted. “Nobody does it. You went right in the middle of Sab and Ren and they had to stop.” She thought about it. “Do it again tomorrow.”

---

That night I went up the slope to see how far the thickness went.

It stopped about twenty paces above the rim, and it did not stop gradually. Heavy and full on one side of a line; four body-lengths on, not.

Standing on the line I could feel it. No. I could not feel it. I want to be accurate about this.

I could *hear* the difference. Below, the town came to me soft and close and slightly late. Above, the same sounds arrived thin and immediate.

I could hear that a thing was different. I could not feel what the thing was.

Nobody down there would have put it that way. They would have said thick here, thin up there, and every one of them would have known exactly what every other one meant.

I thought I had understood the world in an evening.

It was a beautiful evening. The light went out of it slowly and the thickness came up out of the ground as the day cooled, the way mist comes up off a field, except that there was nothing to see. Below me two hundred people were shutting shutters and hanging cloth over doorways and going quiet for the night, not because they were tired but because a room you have shouted in all day is a room you have emptied, and it wants the dark to fill up again.

Somewhere down there Sab and Ren were still going.

By then I was already fond of the place, which I have learned to be careful about.

---

## Chapter 2: The Hour

I met Orrel the next morning, in a ditch.

She was down in it to the middle of her body, doing something to a stone channel with a scraper, and what I could see of her upper membrane had wet grit on it. There was a smell of standing water. She had been at it a while.

“You’re what everybody’s been talking about.”

“Probably.”

“You’re in the light.”

I moved. She went on scraping.

I asked what the channel was for and she told me, at length, in more detail than I have ever been given about anything on any world. It is a water line. It runs from the top of the Hollow to the bottom of the Hollow. It has to be kept clear or the whole dish backs up. The north drain has been wrong for two years. She has told people about the north drain. The people she has told have agreed with her about the north drain. Nothing has happened about the north drain.

“Two years,” she said.

“That’s a long time.”

“It’s a *drain*,” said Orrel. “It’s not a difficult thing. It’s the easiest thing there is. You go and you look at it and you say yes, that’s wrong, and then you fix it.” She scraped. “I’ve done the looking and the saying.”

Somebody went past on the street above and said something I did not catch, and Orrel said, without turning her front toward them and without stopping work: “Thursday. The Kessets have got the new one.”

“Right,” said the person, and went on.

That was the whole of it, and I let it go past me.

---

The hour is on Thursday, except when it isn’t.

Once a week, everything in the Hollow stops. There is no announcement. Nobody rings anything. Nobody is in charge of it. But somebody has to know which hour it is going to be, because it moves. A new baby moves it, a death moves it, and weather moves it if the weather is going to be bad enough to strip the dish.

For eleven years the somebody has been Orrel.

She is not a leader. Nobody voted. If you had asked anyone in that dish who was in charge of the hour they would have looked at you the way you would look at somebody asking who is in charge of Tuesday.

She is the person who remembers. That is all of it, and it turns out to be a great deal.

---

They go low for it.

That is the whole practice and it took me the entire hour to work out why it isn’t just sitting down. Everybody gets as much of themselves as close to the ground as they can. They spread. A body that is broad and flat to begin with goes broader and flatter, and the membrane comes down until it is nearly touching, and two hundred people do this at once across four streets, and then nothing happens for an hour.

Some do it in their own doorways. Some come out and do it together in the street.

These two groups have disagreed about which is correct since before anybody now living was born, and the disagreement is conducted entirely in remarks made on the way past.

“Doorways,” Ip told me, upside down on a step with her legs going. “We’re doorways. Ren’s street. Ren’s *always* been street.”

“Which is better?”

“Doorways,” said Ip, in the tone of somebody asked whether water is wet, and then immediately: “Sab says street is showing off. Ren says doorways is hiding. My mother says they’re both being ridiculous and then she says doorways.”

---

Here is what the hour was, from where I was.

Quiet. Extremely quiet, in the way I mean that word: nothing making noise, nobody moving, no drumming anywhere in four streets. Sound came down off the hill and arrived soft and late the way everything arrived in that dish. It was pleasant. I hung over the middle of the street with two hundred flat people spread out beneath me not doing anything at all, and I thought: *this is nice.*

The hour was thin. Every single person down there knew it was thin. I could not tell.

---

Nobody said so, which is the part I would have missed even if I had known to look.

Nobody got up afterward and announced it was a poor one. What happened was that the hour ended. It ends when it ends; nobody calls it. Everybody got up and went to the eating ground, and the eating was a little flat.

They eat immediately afterward, all of them. It is not a meal so much as two hundred people arriving in the same yard inside four minutes, carrying things, and beginning to talk at exactly the same moment. After an hour of nothing the noise of it went up like a wall going over.

The food was small and hot and there was a great deal of arguing about whose was better. Somebody had done a thing to a root that somebody else considered a mistake.

Underneath it there was a flatness. I heard it perfectly well and read it as nothing. People finished sooner than I expected. Some didn’t come.

Sab said, to nobody: “Well.”

And Ren said: “Weather.”

And it began again, and went on for some time, and I listened to the whole of it and understood about a third.

---

“Was it a bad one?” I asked Ip.

“Was what a bad one?”

“The hour.”

Ip thought about it with her whole body, which is what they do. The membrane goes very still for a moment.

“It was an hour,” she said.

“They’re saying it was thin.”

“They always say that.” She was already looking at something else. “They said it last time and the time before. Ren says the summers. Sab says the draw. Sab always says the draw.” She flipped over. “It’s the same.”

“The same as what?”

“The same as it *is*,” said Ip, with the patience of a nine-year-old explaining an obvious thing to an adult, and went off to do something at the end of the street.

She was five when the draw went in. She has never sat in anything else.

---

Ovick found me in the afternoon, which was not an accident. He had heard there was a floating thing asking questions, and he had something he wanted looked at.

He is old and precise and he keeps the accounts. Within a minute of meeting me he had established that I could count. Within two he was explaining the ledger.

Then he took me to the pump.

It is at the top end of the dish and it is not beautiful: a squat stone housing with a wheel on it and a channel going away downhill. It lifts water out of the low ground and puts it somewhere useful, and before it existed the Hollow flooded twice a year in a way everybody there can still describe in detail.

“Four years,” said Ovick. “That’s four years of not doing that.”

“The draw paid for it.”

“The draw paid for it,” he agreed. “I signed it. I got a fair price. I’ve never once said otherwise and I’ll tell you now that I’d sign it again.”

He said it a shade too readily, the way you say a thing you have said a great many times to people who did not ask.

I asked what the draw actually was, and he showed me: a covered stone run going up out of the dish toward the workshops, low and sealed, disappearing into the slope. It is not much to look at. It is a channel with a lid.

“They still need it,” he said. “For the setting. Can’t make the stuff anywhere loud.”

“And it takes some.”

“It takes some,” said Ovick. “It’s *paid for*. It isn’t taken, it’s bought, and there’s a difference, and I’d be obliged if you’d remember which when you’re talking to Sab.”

---

I went up to the rim in the evening and looked at the whole dish.

Two hundred people, four streets, one pump, one covered channel going up the hill, and a thickness lying in the bottom of it that I could hear and could not feel.

There was a problem. That much was plain. The people who remembered the old hour agreed it was worse. Sab had the draw. Ren had four still-less summers. Neither of them said the other’s thing was imaginary. That is the shape of it. The draw takes silence and the summers have been wrong, and what nobody could show was how much of it belonged to which.

They had been at it for four years and neither had ever made the other produce a number, because there is no number. Nothing on that world measures it. You feel it, and you say what you felt, and the person beside you felt something slightly different and says that.

I could hear thickness as delay, but that did not give me a clean instrument. A sound sharp enough to time precisely would push some of the silence out while I measured it. The ordinary sounds I could listen to without disturbing anything changed in distance, force, and direction. I could compare obviously thick with obviously thin. I could not give either one a stable amount.

I have been to nine worlds and I have never been anywhere that could not settle an argument like that one.

I thought: *these people need better information.*

It was the most obvious thought anybody had ever had.

---

## Chapter 3: A Thing You Can Make

The workshop is three streets up from the rim and it is the least interesting building I have ever gone out of my way to look at.

It is a long low shed with a stone floor, and inside there are trays, and in the trays there is a gray paste, and the paste sits there. That is the work. Somebody mixes it in the morning, everybody is careful around it all day, and by evening it has become the thing it becomes and gets scraped into pots.

The foreman is called Ulth and he was already leaving when I got there.

He was leaving the entire time I was there. He answered every question I asked him fully and accurately while facing the door.

“Settling-work,” he said. “Goes in your roof. Goes in everybody’s roof. You’ve got it in yours down there, all of you, that’s the same batch as the pots outside.” He moved a tray two fingers to the left and looked at the door. “It has to set quiet. That’s the whole of it. Bang about near it while it’s going and it comes up wrong and then your roof lets water in a year from now and you blame the roof.”

“How quiet?”

“Quiet,” said Ulth, in the tone of a man who has never had to put a number on it because nobody has ever had a number to put. “Quiet like down there. That’s why we’re taking it. We’re up here where it’s thin and the work wants thick, so we bought thick.” He straightened up. “Four years and nobody’s said a word to me about it, and now there’s a floating thing in my shed.”

“Somebody down there thinks it’s why the hour’s gone off.”

Ulth stopped, which was the only time he did.

“Do they,” he said.

It was not a defense and it was not an apology. It was a man being told, for the first time, a thing about a place three streets away that he had never once had occasion to think about. Then he looked at the door again.

“They get paid,” he said.

“They do.”

“Then that’s the arrangement,” said Ulth, and went out of it, and I let him.

---

I want to set out what I had, because every piece of it was correct.

Silence is a substance. It has weight and it pools and it runs downhill, and the Hollow is the bottom of the local bowl, which is why the poorest quarter of Cairn Down has the best of the only thing it has.

Sound pushes it out of a place. A room you have shouted in is a room you have partly emptied, and it fills again over the next several hours, low and slow, from the bottom up.

You can *contain* it. I established that in an afternoon, mostly by watching a woman fail to.

She had it in a covered pail with a lid strapped on, carrying it up from her cellar to her mother’s house at the other end of the street, walking the way you walk with something full to the brim. Twenty paces along she stopped, said something unrepeatable, went back, and did it again.

“It leaks,” she told me, when I asked. “It always leaks. Everyone knows it leaks. It goes out the bottom, it goes out the seams, you get about half of it there if you go slow and you’re a fool if you go fast.” She looked at the pail with active dislike. “My mother’s is thin. She’s up the end. It’s not enough to bother about and she bothers about it.”

So: contained, badly. Moved, badly. Lost through any seam that is not sealed. Runs out and downward, always downward.

I have been on nine worlds and I have never had a substance behave so obligingly. Everything about it was consistent. Nothing about it surprised me twice.

---

The other thing I had was the cellars.

Every house in the Hollow has one, because everything in the Hollow is built to catch what runs down, and every cellar in that dish is thick. Thicker than the street. Considerably thicker than the hour.

I went into nine of them. People let me, on the grounds that I was small and strange and had asked, and in every single one it was the same: the deeper the cellar, the fuller it was, and a cellar with a door left open all day was noticeably poorer than the one next to it that had been shut.

And nobody used them for anything except turnips.

Here was the Hollow, in a bowl, at the bottom of everything, sitting once a week in an hour that was thinning. Underneath every single house in it there was a room full of the exact thing they were short of, and they were keeping vegetables in it.

---

The model, then. It took about a day and it went like this.

They are short of a substance.

A substance that can be contained can be gathered. A substance that runs downhill can be led. A substance that leaks out of an unsealed thing will stay in a sealed one.

The cellars fill because nothing happens in them. Silence does not have to be poured through the door. Quiet lets it settle in the space that is already there, and a low enclosed room keeps what settles instead of spending it on movement and sound.

Therefore: take a space, empty it of noise, seal it, and *leave it alone*. Let it fill the way a cellar fills. Then open it, on the day, into the street where the hour is going to happen.

It is a bath. You fill it in the morning and you get in it in the evening. There is nothing clever in it anywhere, which is what I liked about it. I have been on worlds where the answer needed me to be clever, and being clever is the part I trust least about myself.

I checked it against everything I knew. It did not contradict one thing anybody had told me.

And I ran every test I had. I am the only creature on that world that hears. I went down into cellars and listened to them fill. I listened to a sealed room and an open one, at the rim and in the bottom of the dish, on a still day and on a windy one.

Every measurement I could take said the same thing.

---

The old drain shed is at the low end, past the last houses, and it has not been used since the pump went in.

It is stone, it is dug down, it has one door and no windows, and it had four years of nothing happening in it. When I got somebody to open it the air inside was so thick that the woman who had opened it stood in the doorway for a moment and did not say anything at all.

Then she said: “Well, that’s the shed.”

They did it in a day and a half, and I did not do any of it, because I cannot do any of it.

That is worth saying plainly. I have no hands. I have never picked anything up. What I can do is look at a thing until I understand it and then say what I have understood, and on Ebb that meant I hung near a doorway for a day and a half saying *that gap*, and *the bottom of that gap*, and *no, that will let it out along the floor*, and a dozen people who had every reason to tell me to go away did the work.

They sealed the door frame with settling-work. Somebody made a joke about buying their own silence back and everybody laughed, including Ovick, who had come to watch and had opinions and expressed them.

And by the time they were finished they had emptied it. A dozen people working and arguing in a stone room for a day and a half had driven four years of it out through the open door, and when I went in at the end the shed sounded like any other shed in Cairn Down. Somebody remarked on it, in the tone you use for an obvious thing. You cannot work in a room and keep it.

That was the point. We were not going to give them the old silence out of a shed. We were going to fill an empty one and see whether it filled.

Then we shut it and left it alone for four days, and the difficult part of the whole business turned out to be persuading a neighborhood of extremely curious people not to open a door.

---

On the fourth evening we opened it.

Nothing visible happened at all. The door came off and there was a shed with nothing in it, and the street outside the shed was a street.

What I could hear was the town going away.

The noise off the hill, which arrives everywhere in that dish soft and late, went softer and later. Not gone, not muffled, just further off, the way a thing sounds when it is on the other side of a field. The scrape of somebody’s legs on stone twenty paces down came to me like it was coming from the end of a long room.

And the Ebbin standing in that street stopped.

All of them, at once, the way the row had flinched on my first evening, except the other way. Nobody said anything for long enough that I began to wonder whether I had done something dangerous.

Then somebody near the back said, “*Oh*,” and that was the end of the quiet, because two hundred people cannot be given a thing like that and stay quiet about it, and the noise that came up out of that street was the loudest thing I heard on Ebb.

Ip got to me first, going flat out, shouting a question with no verb in it.

Sab arrived and told everybody within reach that this proved it had been the draw all along. Ren arrived immediately behind and said it proved nothing of the kind. They conducted the entire argument standing in the thickest air any of them had been in for four years, at a volume that was actively spending it, and neither of them noticed, and I did notice and did not say anything, because I was pleased.

That is the honest word. I was pleased with myself.

Orrel came down at the end, from wherever she had been, and put herself low in the doorway of the shed for a while without saying anything to anybody.

Then she said: “Thursday.”

---

## Chapter 4: The Good Week

It held for two weeks.

That is the flat version and I am going to give it flat, because what matters is not what those two weeks were like but what they did to everybody, and what they did to everybody was let them plan.

They did it again the next Thursday and it worked again. Then the Thursday after that.

By the second one it had a routine. Somebody had to shut the shed on the Sunday, and somebody had to keep the children off it, and both of those turned into jobs that people volunteered for and then became territorial about. Two households on the low side moved the days they do their loud work, which they had not done for anybody in living memory. The Kessets came. They had stopped coming years ago, over a grievance nobody would explain to me, and Orrel had gone on moving the hour around their household the entire time as though they were expected. I had heard her do it on my second morning and had not understood. They came to the second one and stood at the back and did not say anything, and came again to the third.

And people started talking about the summer.

Not the past summers. The next one. Somebody said that if this went on they could stop worrying about the still-less weather altogether, and somebody else said not to say that out loud, and everybody laughed, and it was the kind of laugh that means the first person had said the thing everybody was thinking.

That is what two weeks buys you. Not happiness exactly. **Plans.**

---

I was there for the third one and it was the best hour anybody in that dish had sat in for four years.

I know that because they told me so, at length, with the specificity of people who have finally got a thing back and want to itemize it. And I could hear it: the town going away, the hill going quiet and distant, the whole world arriving late and soft, more of it than the first night I came.

Two hundred flat people went low in four streets. Nobody moved for an hour.

Afterward the eating was not flat. It was extremely loud, it went on a long time, and somebody argued about a root and enjoyed it.

---

Orrel said it that evening, at the eating, standing where she was.

She did not go to the middle and she did not wait for quiet, which meant about a third of the yard was still talking when she started. She said it in the voice she uses to move the hour to Thursday.

She said the hour had been thin for four years and everybody knew it and nobody had been able to do anything about it.

She said the floating thing had worked out how to make it and had been right.

She said she had been coming to this hour for eleven years and had watched it go off, and that she had not expected to sit in one like today’s again, and that she had been wrong about that, and she was glad to have been wrong about it.

She said: we should keep doing it.

Then she went back to what she had been doing, and the yard made a noise, and Sab said something about the draw that nobody attended to.

That is all of it. It took less than a minute.

---

We knew from the first Thursday that it did not keep.

The morning after we opened the shed, the street was an ordinary street again. Everything was back where it had been, the hill loud and near, the whole gift gone overnight like water off a stone.

Nobody minded. I went round expecting to have to explain a failure and found a neighborhood entirely satisfied, because they do not want silence in the street on a Wednesday. They want it for an hour on a Thursday, and a thing that lasts an evening lasts long enough.

“It’s a *pot*,” somebody said to me, meaning that you fill a pot when you want the thing in it.

So we filled the pot. Four days sealed, opened on the Thursday, gone by the Friday, start again. Three weeks of that.

---

Here is what nobody looked for, and I include myself and I include everybody.

We all checked whether it lasted. Nobody checked what the street was like *afterward*.

Why would you. A thing goes; you have had it; the world is as it was. And the difference between a street with all of its silence in it and a street with slightly less than all of its silence in it is not a difference any Ebbin can put a number on, because there are no numbers, and it is smaller than the difference between a still day and a windy one.

Made silence does not stay. Everybody knew that inside two weeks.

**It does not go alone**, and that took me five weeks and a great deal of stubbornness, because you cannot see it in one filling. You cannot see it in three. What one filling takes is less than the weather takes, and the weather was doing what it has been doing for four summers, and it is all the same street on the same hill and there is only ever one of it to look at.

---

What I did in the end was cheat the problem. If you cannot see a small thing once, you look at a great many small things at once.

We used eleven cellars, all offered. By then the Hollow was extremely interested and half of it wanted a job. Four we sealed and opened on the same schedule as the shed. Four we sealed and left sealed after they had filled. Three we left alone. I went round all eleven every morning for a month and listened to each cellar and to the street outside it.

While the sealed cellars were filling, the streets outside both sealed groups tracked together. The difference appeared after the first four were opened. Those streets went off a little faster. The streets outside the cellars that stayed shut did not. The next week we changed which filled cellars we opened, and the difference followed the opening.

Not much. Consistently.

Whatever we were making went out when we released it, and it took a share of what was already there with it, every single time, in an amount too small for one person in one street to notice in one week.

---

I want to say clearly what I did wrong.

I did not fail to test whether it kept. We knew that in two weeks and it did not matter.

What I failed to find was the leaving. There are only two instruments on that world: an Ebbin’s membrane, and me. Made silence and settled silence are the same thing to both of us. They sound identical. They feel identical, I am told, to anybody who can feel them. And what one filling costs a street is smaller than what a windy afternoon costs it, so a single test cannot show you, and two cannot, and by the time enough of them have run to show you anything at all, you have already done the thing a dozen times and told everybody it works.

---

The hour after that was the worst one anybody there could remember.

They went low in four streets, in the doorways and in the street, all of them, on a Thursday, in a dish that had less in it than it had held on the day I arrived.

I hung over the middle of it and listened to a town that was not going away at all. The hill was loud and close, everything arrived fast and hard and immediate, and two hundred people lay flat in it for an hour without moving, because it was the hour and you sit in the hour.

Nobody said anything at the eating.

I mean that literally: there was an eating, and it was quiet, and for the first time since I arrived the loudest thing in that yard was somebody scraping a pot.

Ip came and sat near me at the end of it and asked whether we were going to do the shed again.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it makes it worse.”

Ip thought about that for a while, upside down, legs going.

“Then don’t,” she said, and went off, and that was the only conversation about it I had that was easy.

---

Orrel did not come and find me and I did not go and find her.

She was at the eating. She had been at the hour. Somebody asked her a question about next week and she answered it, and the answer was fine, and I watched two people at the other end of the yard look at each other while she was answering it.

That was all that happened. Nobody said anything to her. Nobody said anything about her.

I have been on ten worlds and watched a considerable amount of harm done, most of it loudly.

---

## Chapter 5: What It Took with It

It rained for four days after that, which on Ebb is complicated.

Rain disturbs the surface of everything. It is two hundred thousand small shoves an hour, and the membrane reads every one of them, so a wet day is a loud day and nobody sits out in it. But rain also pins the medium down. It puts a lid on the dish. Whatever is in the Hollow when the rain starts is still in the Hollow when it stops, instead of going off up the hill in the wind.

So the Hollow’s position on rain is that it is horrible and they want more of it, and this position is held with total consistency by people who will argue about anything else.

“Four days,” said Ren, with enormous satisfaction, on the fourth day. “That’s four days of nothing going anywhere.”

“It doesn’t put any *back*,” said Sab.

“It doesn’t take any off.”

“It doesn’t put any back,” said Sab, “and there’s less in here than there was in the spring, and the pump channel’s been running the whole time, and you’re standing in the street telling me about weather.”

“Because it *is* weather. It has been weather for four summers. You’ve had four summers of me saying weather and you have not once...”

“I’m not saying it isn’t weather.”

That stopped Ren, briefly.

“You’ve said it’s the draw since they put the draw in.”

“I’ve said it’s *mostly* the draw,” said Sab. “I’ve never once said the summers were good. I’m the one who told you the summers were bad.”

“You said that to say it was the draw.”

“I said it because it was true and it was *also* the draw.”

And Ren said something to that, and Sab said something back, and I want to record that at no point in four years of this argument had either of them said the other was imagining things. Sab has never denied the summers. Ren has never denied the channel. They were not arguing about what was happening. They were arguing about how much of it belonged to which, and there is nothing on that world that could have told them, and they knew that, and they went on anyway because what else do you do.

---

The Thursday after the rain was no better than the one before it.

It was not dramatic. Two hundred people went low in four streets in the ordinary way and lay there for an hour and got up again.

What was different was afterward, and it was small, and I saw it because by then I was watching for it.

Two households did not come.

Not the Kessets. The Kessets came, which nobody remarked on and which I thought was the single most decent thing I saw in that dish. Two others, from the low end, who had been at every one since I arrived.

Nobody went and asked them why. That is not a thing you ask.

---

The north drain was still wrong.

Orrel was in it again that week, and I went down and hung near the top of the ditch, which is where I had been the first morning.

“You’ll be in the light,” she said, before I was.

I moved.

“I’m sorry about the shed,” I said.

Orrel scraped twice.

“I know.”

That was all. She did not make me explain it, and I did not ask her to make me feel better about it.

What we talked about instead was the north drain: two years of it, the shape of the fault, the fact that the run above it takes the fall wrong, and what it would take to put right, which is four people and two days and a quantity of settling-work.

“Four people and two days,” she said.

“Has anybody said no?”

“Nobody has ever said no.” She scraped. “That’s not the difficulty. Everybody says yes. Everybody has said yes for two years.”

I asked her whether she was going to ask again.

“I’ll mention it,” said Orrel.

---

Then somebody asked Kellow about the hour.

Kellow is perfectly ordinary. Kellow has a house on the middle street and two children and does something with cloth, and Kellow has never in the whole of this account been the person anybody asked about anything.

The question was small: whether the hour was going to move because of a thing that was happening at the top of the dish the following week. It is exactly the question you would have asked Orrel a month before. The person who asked it was not making a point. They wanted to know a fact and they asked somebody they thought would have it.

And Kellow said: “I’ll find out.”

Not *ask Orrel*. **I’ll find out.**

Then they both went on with what they were doing, and neither of them had done anything, and nothing had happened at all.

---

I understood what I was looking at about two days later and it arrived the way a smell arrives.

Nobody in the Hollow had decided anything. There had been no conversation. Nobody had said *Orrel got it wrong*; I asked, in a roundabout way, and the answers I got were genuinely puzzled.

What had happened was that a thing everybody used to do without thinking, they now did slightly differently without thinking.

You do not get eleven years back after that. There is nothing to appeal, because there is no decision. There is nobody to be angry with, because nobody did it.

---

Ip found me on the rim, where she is not allowed, and where she goes.

“Are they going to fix the shed?”

“There’s nothing wrong with the shed.”

“Then are they going to *do* it again?”

“No.”

“Because it makes it worse.”

“Because it makes it worse.”

Ip lay on the rim with her legs going and her membrane full of wind, which is a thing she likes and every adult in the Hollow finds unpleasant, and thought about it.

“Everyone was excited,” she said.

“They were.”

“Sab was excited and he *told* everyone the draw was wrong and then it stopped and now he’s saying the draw is wrong again.” She flipped over. “He says the same thing when it’s good and when it’s bad.”

That is the most acute thing anybody said to me on Ebb and she was not trying to say it.

Then she said: “Was it better before? Really before. Before-before.”

“They say it was.”

“How much better?”

And I could not tell her, because nobody can, because there is no number, and because I never sat in it.

“They can’t say,” I said.

“Then how do they *know*,” said Ip, not as a challenge but as an actual question from somebody who wanted the answer, and went down off the rim ahead of me, because the wind had got up and even Ip has limits.

---

The pump broke on the Sunday.

It is four years old and it broke the way things break. A fitting went, and the water backed up in the low channel, and a dozen people were out in the dark with lamps and a great deal of shouting getting it apart before the bottom street went under.

I was no use. I said so, twice, and was told to move twice, and eventually went and hung over the top of the ditch where I could see the whole of it.

Sab and Ren worked next to each other for three hours and did not argue once.

Ovick stood in the wet with the lamp and told everybody what the fitting had cost, and when it had been bought, and who had said at the time that it was the wrong fitting. Nobody listened. He said it anyway. It is what he has instead of hands in the water.

Orrel was in the channel to the middle of her body, doing the thing she does, and when it was over and the water was going down the right way somebody said *right* and everybody went home.

And at no point in three hours did the hour come up. Not once. Not as a grievance, not as a joke.

They had a thing to do and they did it, and the thing they had lost was not in the room, and I hung over a wet street at two in the morning above a dozen people going home, and none of it had anything to do with me.

---

## Chapter 6: Nobody Is Cruel About It

The new one is a Mell and she is called Ossa and she is the size of a folded cloth and she has no idea what any of this is about.

She was four days old when Ip took me to look at her, on the grounds that I had not seen a small one and that this was a deficiency in me. The Mells have the house at the turn of the middle street, and there were nine people in it, which is three more than the floor will take.

That is a real problem and not a figure of speech. A room is full when the floor is full. Two hundred flat people cannot use a house the way you would use a house, and every doorway in the Hollow has an argument in it about who is going to move so somebody else can come in, conducted with total goodwill and enormous stubbornness.

Somebody solved it by going up on the bench.

The reaction was immediate and unanimous and I did not understand a word of it. Four people told him to get down. The woman whose bench it was told him to get down twice. He got down, saying the thing you say when you have done something rude by accident and want everybody to stop looking at you, and the room reorganized itself on the floor with a great deal of muttering.

“Why can’t he be up there?” I asked Ip, outside.

“Because then he’s *over* everyone.”

“Is that bad?”

Ip stopped, and did the thing where the membrane goes very still, which I had learned by then meant she was trying to explain water to a fish.

“He’d get it all first,” she said.

And that is the whole of it, and no adult on that world would ever have thought to tell me, because no adult on that world has ever needed to say it out loud. You keep your membrane level with everybody else’s. Anything else is taking more than your share of the room.

---

Ossa’s naming was on the sixth day and I did not understand what I was watching until it was over.

Everybody comes. There is no officiant and nobody makes a speech, and the whole thing takes about four minutes. What happens is that the family brings the small one out into the street and everybody in the Hollow who can get there goes low around them, the whole street flat in a ring the way they do for the hour, and they stay down for as long as it takes for the person holding the baby to say the name once.

Then everybody gets up and it is a party, immediately, with no transition whatsoever.

I asked Ovick afterward what the going-low was for.

“So she’s in it,” he said, as though I had asked what a door is for. “First thing she’s in. It’s the thickest she’ll ever have.”

They give the newest person the fullest room they can make. Two hundred people put themselves flat on the ground so that a creature the size of a folded cloth spends four minutes at the bottom of everything, in the deepest silence the dish can hold.

I did not ask whether it had been better before.

---

Ovick’s third candidate for the accounts lasted two weeks.

Her name was Tolla. She understood the work in six days and gave it up because Ovick spent four hours explaining the flood-year accounts, which he considers the interesting part and which Tolla considers four hours.

He says he will find a fourth. He is not going to hand anybody the ledger without explaining it. The ledger is the most interesting thing he has, and a man does not stop offering the most interesting thing he has because people keep leaving.

---

Sab and Ren’s wall came to a head that week.

The wall runs between their two properties and it is falling down at one end, and it has been falling down at that end for three years. Neither of them disputes that it is falling down. What they dispute is whose wall it is, and this dispute has been going on for so long and has been so thoroughly enjoyed by both of them that when a mason from up the hill came down and offered to look at it, they turned him away together.

“It’s not settled,” Sab told him, with dignity.

“You could settle it,” said the mason, “and then I could do the wall.”

“You could do the wall,” said Ren, “and then it’d never be settled.”

The mason went back up the hill. Sab and Ren stood and watched him go, entirely united, and then resumed.

Ovick has a document about the wall. It is from before either of them was born and Ovick will not produce it, and I asked him why, and he said, “Because then it’d be over,” and went back to what he was doing.

---

The hour went on being thin.

They kept coming. Not all of them. The two households from the low end did not come back, and one of them told somebody they had stopped bothering, and that got repeated round the dish in the tone people use for a death.

Kellow moved it twice that month, once for weather and once for a thing at the top of the dish. Both times the moving worked. Both times somebody had to be told twice, because the person you check with has been the same person for eleven years and habits are slow.

Orrel came to both.

She came to the one that was moved for weather and she came to the one that was moved for the thing at the top of the dish, and on the second one she was late, because she had been in the north drain, and she came down the street with wet grit still on her and went low in her own doorway at the end of the row without saying anything to anybody about being late.

Nobody said anything to her about it either.

---

The north drain got fixed in the third week of that month.

It was not fixed because Orrel asked. She had asked for two years.

It was fixed because the pump broke, and when everybody was out in the dark with lamps getting the fitting apart, somebody standing in the wet said that if the north run had been taking the fall properly the bottom street would not have been in danger in the first place, and four people agreed with him, and one of them was the man whose house is on the bottom street.

And the next week four people and two days.

Orrel was not one of the four. She was at work on her own section, which is where she is. Somebody told her it was done and she said “right,” and went and looked at it that evening on her way home, and I went with her, and she stood at the top of it for a while looking at a piece of stonework she had described to me in complete detail three weeks earlier.

“They’ve run it shallow,” she said.

“Is that wrong?”

“It’ll do,” said Orrel.

Then she went home. If you asked anybody in the Hollow how that drain came to be fixed, they would tell you about the night the pump broke.

---

Ip’s mother told her she could go up on the rim if she took somebody with her, which is the compromise adults arrive at when they have run out of energy, and Ip took me, on the grounds that I was somebody.

We went up in the evening. She got about ten paces past the line where the thick stops and turned round and came back, and did it again, and again, the way I had done it on my first night without knowing what I was doing.

“It’s horrible,” she said, delighted.

“Then why do it?”

“Because it’s *horrible*.” She did it again. “Sab says everything up there is thin and that’s why they’re all like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like *that*,” said Ip, meaning the entire population of the town above, and went up past the line one more time to be appalled.

I stayed below it and listened to her being appalled, and to the hill beyond her, and to the dish behind me with a little less in it than it had held on the evening I arrived.

Nobody up there was doing anything to anybody. Ulth was in his shed with his trays, and the paste was setting, and the settling-work that seals every roof in the Hollow was coming out of a room three streets up that needed quiet to make it.

---

## Chapter 7: The Renegotiation

It took five weeks and three meetings and it was extremely dull.

The first meeting was in the eating ground and it was not a meeting. Somebody said the word *renegotiate* and forty people said things at once for an hour and a half, and at the end of it the position of the Hollow was that something should be done, and that Ovick should do it, and that whatever he did would probably be wrong.

“That’s the mandate,” Ovick told me afterward, with some bitterness. “That is the entirety of my mandate.”

He went up the hill the following week.

---

Here is what he was working with, and he laid it out for me in advance because he lays everything out for anybody who will hold still.

The Hollow cannot stop the draw. There is no authority in Cairn Down that would make the workshop stop, and the Hollow does not want it stopped, because the money is spent on the pump and because the Hollow’s own roofs are full of what that workshop makes.

The Hollow cannot prove the draw is why the hour is thin. Ren has four still-less summers and no measurement, and Sab has a channel and no measurement, and Ulth has an agreement with a price in it and four years of nobody having complained.

And the Hollow had, four weeks earlier, publicly demonstrated to the entire district that it did not understand its own silence well enough to be trusted with an opinion about it. That last one was mine. Nobody said so. Nobody had to.

“So what do you ask for?” I said.

“Less,” said Ovick. “You ask for less than they’re taking and more than they’re paying and you go home with something in the middle and everyone tells you you should have got more.”

---

Ulth was surprised, which I had not expected and which I should have.

He had been drawing silence out of a neighborhood for four years under an agreement that nobody had ever come back about. Then a floating thing turned up in his shed and told him somebody down there thought it was his fault, and now here was the man who had signed it, up the hill, asking to talk about the volume.

“You signed it,” Ulth said.

“I did.”

“It’s a fair price. It was a fair price then and it’s the same price.”

“It’s a fair price,” said Ovick.

There was a pause of the kind that happens when two people who both expected an argument discover they are not going to get one.

“Then what?” said Ulth.

“The volume,” said Ovick. “Not the price. You take it at the same rate all year and we want it stepped back in the still months, because the still months are when we’ve got least and you’re taking the same off the top of least as you take off the top of most.”

Ulth looked at the door.

“How much back?”

“A third.”

“A third in the still months is the Downside order,” said Ulth. “The Downside order is roofs. Some of them are yours.”

“I know whose they are,” said Ovick. “I’ve got the ledger.”

---

They did that for five weeks.

Not continuously. Ulth had a shed to run and Ovick had a dish to live in, and the actual work of it was four conversations of about forty minutes each, and in between them a great deal of standing about in the Hollow being asked how it was going by people who then told him how it should be going.

The mason came down and looked at the pump channel while all this was happening, on an unrelated matter, and had to be prevented from looking at the wall.

Sab told Ovick to demand it be stopped altogether. Ren told Ovick that demanding it be stopped altogether would be idiotic. They then argued with each other about it for two days, which took them off Ovick for two days, and Ovick said that was the most useful thing either of them had done all year.

---

What he got was a sixth in the still months, and a clause about weather.

That is not a third. But the clause is the part he is proudest of, and the part nobody could follow: in a year with too many still days, counted by four fishermen and a tally, the draw steps back further on its own, for that year, without anybody having to argue about it again.

“It’s the *weather*,” he told me, holding the paper. “It moves with the weather. Ren has been standing in that street for four years saying weather, and now if the weather does it, the draw comes off. That’s Ren’s argument, in the agreement, with Ulth’s name on it.”

“Does Ren know?”

“Ren will know,” said Ovick, “when I have finished explaining it.”

---

He read it out at the eating ground on the Thursday.

He read all of it, including the parts about carriage and the parts about who repairs the channel lid, and he read the weather clause twice because he wanted it understood, and by the second reading a number of people had begun conversations about something else.

Then somebody said, “Is that it? A sixth?”

And somebody else said that a sixth was better than nothing, in a tone that made it clear a sixth was nothing.

And somebody said Ovick should have got it stopped.

And Ovick, who had spent five weeks on this and had got a clause that turns four years of Ren being ignored into a term of the contract, said: “Yes. Right. Well.”

And that was the end of it, and everybody went and ate.

---

I asked him that evening whether he minded.

He was in with the ledger, entering the new terms, and he took a long time answering, and when he did he did not answer the question I asked.

“Twenty-two years I’ve kept these,” he said. “When I started, the man who did it before me kept them in his head, and when he died we lost eleven years of what anybody had paid for anything, and there was a bad time about it that people still talk about wrong.”

“So they know it matters.”

“They know it matters when it isn’t there,” said Ovick.

Then he said: “Don’t tell Sab about the weather clause. I want to tell Sab about the weather clause.”

---

The thing was finished after that.

There was nothing left to work out. The manufactured silence was understood and would not be made again. The draw was renegotiated as far as it was going to be. The hour was thin and would go on being thin, and no arrangement available to anybody was going to change that.

I had come to a place, made it worse, established exactly how I had made it worse, and watched somebody else negotiate the small improvement that was actually available.

That is the whole of my usefulness on Ebb and it was over by the middle of a Thursday afternoon.

I did not go.

That was new. Usually there was still something I was trying to work out. Here there was not. My usefulness was over, and I stayed anyway.

---

Nobody asked me to stay. I was not waiting for anything.

The days went on and I was in them.

I went round the eleven cellars one more time and confirmed what I already knew and told the two households on the low side that they could unseal theirs. I watched them do it. Then I helped nobody with anything for three weeks.

I hung over the pump while a man I had never spoken to explained the fitting to a man who did not want to know. I went up on the rim with Ip twice more and was appalled at the wind on command. I learned the difference between the two kinds of root and had it explained to me six times by people who all disagreed. I was told to move a great many times.

On the third Thursday Kellow asked me whether I would tell the low end the hour had shifted, because Kellow was on the other side of the dish and I was faster than anybody.

It is the only job I was given on that world and it took nine minutes and I did it properly.

---

## Chapter 8: The Thin Hour

The season turned while I was not doing anything in particular.

It happens fast on Ebb. The still months come in over about a week and everybody in the Hollow becomes slightly more cheerful and slightly more careful at the same time, because still is thick and still is also when the dish holds what it is given, and there is a general feeling that you should not waste it.

The loud trades moved their hours without being asked. Somebody re-hung the cloth over the eating ground. Two people had an argument about whether it was early this year and produced, between them, forty years of evidence that it was and was not.

And the roots came in, which are the two kinds, and I was told the difference again by three more people, and all three of them contradicted each other and each of them told me the other two were wrong about it in a way that suggested this was an annual pleasure.

---

I went to the hour on the last Thursday.

That is not quite the right way to say it. I had been to all of them. What I mean is that on the last Thursday I went to the hour, and stopped, and stayed the whole of it in one place, which I had not done before.

They went low in four streets. Doorways and street, both, and the disagreement about which is correct conducted in remarks on the way past, exactly as it was on my first one. Sab in the street. Ren in the street, forty paces off, because they are both street and always have been, which I did not find out until my ninth week and which explains a certain amount.

Kellow had moved it to the Thursday, which is where it usually is anyway.

Ip went low next to her mother and did not stay still, because she is nine.

And Orrel came down the row in her own time and went flat in her own doorway, at the end, with the wet grit still on her, and lay there for an hour.

---

It was thin.

I could hear that it was thin. The hill was close and hard and immediate, and every sound arriving in that dish came in fast and clean and near, and a cart going along the top road came through the whole hour from end to end.

Nobody moved.

I do not know what an hour of that is like for them. I have asked, and been given answers, and the answers are not the thing. The best I can do is what I have: I hung in a street with two hundred people flat on the ground around me in less of a substance than they used to have, none of them going anywhere, for an hour, on a Thursday, because it was the hour.

Then it ended, the way it ends, without anybody calling it.

And two hundred people got up and went and ate and began talking at exactly the same moment, and the noise of it went up like a wall going over, and somebody had done something to a root that somebody else considered a mistake.

---

I stayed still for the whole of it. It is not lying low. I have nothing to lie low with. But I stopped in one place for an hour, in a thin street, which is as near to the thing as I can get.

Nobody remarked on it. Nobody had asked me to.

---

I left the next morning.

I told Ovick the evening before because he was the one I happened to be with, and he said, “Right,” and then, “Hold on,” and went and got the ledger, and made me look at the flood-year accounts for some time, and then said that if I came back through I was to say so in advance.

“I can’t.”

“No,” said Ovick. “No, you can’t. Well.” He shut the book. “Then don’t tell anybody you’re coming and there’ll be no fuss.”

---

Sab and Ren were at the wall, which is still falling down.

They were arguing about the still months, and specifically about whether an early still year counts as an early still year for the purposes of the tally, which is a question the weather clause has created and which is going to keep them going for a decade. Ovick has told Sab about the clause. Sab has decided the clause is his. Ren has decided the clause is Ren’s. Neither of them is entirely wrong, and Ovick is not correcting anybody.

They stopped when I came past.

“You’re off,” said Sab.

“Yes.”

“It’s the draw,” said Sab, “and when the tally comes in this year you’ll see I was right about the size of it, and you can come back and I’ll tell you.”

“It’s the summers,” said Ren, “and he knows it’s the summers, and when the tally comes in it’ll say so.”

I said I hoped they would settle it and both of them looked at me as though I had said something faintly obscene, and I went on.

---

Ip was on the rim, where she is not allowed, with a friend, which is worse.

She came down when she saw me, at speed, and stopped, and did not appear to have anything prepared.

“You’re going.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“You never know,” said Ip. “You didn’t know when you got here either.” She thought about that. “Is it thick?”

“I don’t know that either.”

“Then it might be *better* than here.”

“It might.”

Ip considered this with her whole body, the way she considers everything, and arrived somewhere, and delivered it.

“It won’t be,” she said, and went back up on the rim, where she is not allowed, and did not turn round, and shouted something at her friend about how far I could go.

---

Orrel was at the north drain, which had been fine for two months and which she was checking anyway.

“You’ll be in the light,” she said.

I moved.

I had a number of things I had intended to say to her beyond the apology, and I had been arranging them for some days. Standing at the top of that ditch I understood that all of them were for me.

So what I said was: “They’ve run it shallow.”

“They have.”

“Will it hold?”

“It’ll hold,” said Orrel. “It’ll want doing again in nine years and they’ll do it wrong again in nine years, and I’ve told them, and I’ll tell them then.”

She went back to the scraping.

I stayed a while. She did not ask me anything and did not stop working, and after a bit she said that the low end wanted its own line off the main run and that it was not going to get one, and explained why, at length, and with the fall of the ground drawn out for me in the grit with the end of the scraper.

Then I said I was going, and she said, “Right.”

And then, after I had already begun to move away, without looking up:

“Thursday’s Thursday next week. Tell Kellow, if you’re going past.”

I was going past. I told Kellow.

---

The crossing is up above the rim, in the thin, where the ground goes over toward the north road.

I went up through the line, the place where the thick stops and the world goes hard and immediate, and out into the ordinary air above it, and the dish went small below me the way places do.

Four streets. One pump. A covered channel going up the hill with a sixth less in it for four months of the year. Two hundred people, and a wall that is not going to get fixed, and a drain that will want doing again in nine years, and a small one four weeks old who will grow up in a thin hour and never know the difference, and a woman at the end of a row who will keep coming.

There it is. The black sky, the white line, the shape that opens.

I did not look at it.

I have looked at the last two. I went through this one the way I used to go through them, without stopping, because there was nothing about it I wanted just then.

I went forward.

---
