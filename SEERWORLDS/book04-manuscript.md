## Chapter 1: What You’ll Take

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I know more than I used to. I can move. I can look at a thing until I understand it. I have been to three worlds now, and in every one of them there was a thing everybody believed that was not quite so, and I found it, and I am aware that this has started to feel like a skill.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I arrived indoors.

That is the only way to say it. There was no sky. There was a ceiling of red stone about four times my height above me, worked smooth in some places and left rough in others, and a floor of the same stone, and walls, and a long passage going away in both directions with smaller side passages opening off it. The long passages are what Underhand is built out of. You travel them and you work in them, and every chamber in the settlement opens off one.

The light came out of the walls. There were seams in the rock: amber, faintly glowing, running along at about shoulder height like veins in a leaf. They were the only light there was and there was plenty of it. Everything was warm and orange and close.

Somewhere a long way off, something was being struck. I could feel it through the stone before I could hear it.

I liked it immediately.

Then something came around the corner too fast, saw me, tried to stop, and fell over.


“Sorry. Sorry.”

It was about knee-high and pale and soft-looking, with four short legs and wide dim eyes and a general air of not being finished. It picked itself up in a complicated way that involved most of its body.

“You weren’t here yesterday,” it said.

“No.”

“I’d remember you.” It walked all the way around me, which took a while, because it was not fast. “What are you?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s all right,” it said kindly. “Nobody does yet.”

Then it asked me the question, and it asked it the way you would ask somebody the time.

“What are you going to take?”

## Chapter 2: Then You’re It

Its name was Pell.

It was thirteen and it was, by its own account, extremely close to a decision. It told me this within the first minute and then contradicted it comprehensively for the next hour.

“I don’t understand the question,” I said.

“Which question?”

“What am I going to take.”

Pell stopped walking.

“Your *name*,” it said.

“I’ve got a name.”

“You’ve got a...” Pell made a face, which is a thing soft can do, because their faces have not gone anywhere yet. “What is it?”

“The Dot.”

“That’s a soft name.”

“It’s the only one I’ve got.”

“Yes,” said Pell, with enormous patience. “That’s what I’m saying. That’s a name for now. I’m Pell. Pell’s not a *name*, Pell’s what they called me so they’d have something to shout. You take a real one later.”

“And then what?”

Pell looked at me for a moment, and I watched it work out that I genuinely did not know, and I watched it decide that this was the most interesting thing that had happened to it all year.

“Then you’re it,” it said.


I did not understand that for another twenty minutes, and then something went past us in the passage and I did.

It was enormous. Low and broad and deeper than it was tall, with a back like a shelf, and it was carrying a stack of cut stone that I did not believe until I had counted it. Eleven pieces. It went by at a slow steady walk without appearing to notice the weight, said something friendly to Pell, and kept going.

“That’s a Carrier,” said Pell.

“It’s a different shape from you.”

“It’s a Carrier.”

“But it’s the same...” I stopped. “Is it the same kind of creature as you?”

“It’s the same as me,” said Pell, “except it went and stood in the Name Chamber about forty years ago and said *Carrier*, and then it turned into that.”

I did not say anything.

“It used to look like me,” Pell said. “Everybody used to look like me. That’s what soft *is*.”


Then a Wallwalker came over the ceiling.

It did not climb the wall and cross the ceiling. It came *over* the ceiling, upside down, at speed, with long thin limbs finding holds I could not see, and it dropped into the passage in front of us and landed like a thrown cloth.

“Pell.”

“Sath.”

“Who’s that?”

“Don’t know. It hasn’t taken.”

Sath looked at me with quick bright eyes on a head at the end of a long neck and said, “What are you going to take?” and did not wait for an answer, and went up the wall and away.

“Everyone is going to ask me that,” I said.

“Yes,” said Pell.

## Chapter 3: You Don’t

We passed four soft in a side chamber doing something violent to each other and I stopped to watch.

Three of them were on the floor in a stack and the fourth was underneath, and the whole arrangement was staggering sideways while everybody shouted.

“What is that?”

“Carrier,” said Pell, without looking.

“It doesn’t look like a Carrier.”

“It’s not *meant* to look like a Carrier. It’s meant to be *heavy*.” Pell watched with a professional eye as the stack came apart and all four of them hit the wall. “That’s Ottu on the bottom. Ottu’s rubbish at Carrier. Ottu’s good at Wallwalker.”

“How do you do Wallwalker?”

“You climb up the wall and you fall off.”

“That doesn’t sound difficult.”

“It’s the falling,” said Pell, with feeling. “You have to fall off *like they would*. You can’t just fall off like you fell off.”

I watched Ottu demonstrate. It got about a body-length up, hung there, and came down.

I want to be fair to a fourteen-year-old.

It was not obviously different from falling.

Everybody applauded.

“They do it all day,” said Pell. “You’ll see it everywhere. Everybody does it until they take.”

“And then you stop?”

“Then you don’t have to pretend,” said Pell.


We walked. I asked questions. Pell answered them at length and with total confidence, and about a third of what it told me turned out later to be wrong, which I have decided is exactly the right proportion for a guide.

Here is what I had by the end of that first day.

You are born soft. You stay soft for twelve or fourteen or sixteen years, and during that time you cannot dig or climb or carry or cut, not because anybody stops you but because you have not got the body for it. Everybody feeds you and nobody expects anything.

Then, at some point, you go to the Name Chamber and you say what you are going to be.

And then over the next sixty days you become it.

“Does it hurt?”

“They say it aches.”

“Can you change your mind?”

“Before you say it, as much as you want.”

“After?”

“No,” said Pell.

It said it easily. It was not a heavy word for Pell. It was just the shape of the world, the way *down* is the shape of the world, and I have learned that when a creature says a thing like that easily you should pay attention to it, because it means the whole place is built on it.

“What happens if you pick wrong?”

Pell laughed at me.

“You don’t,” it said. “You’ve had your whole life to think about it. I’ve been thinking about it since I was *six*.”

And then it told me its list, which had nine names on it, and which it revised twice before we got to the end of the passage.

## Chapter 4: The Stock

The settlement was called Underhand and it was about six hundred Orruk in a network of long passages going down and across through the red stone. There was no surface.

I asked twice, in two different ways: where does it come out, and what is above the highest passage.

Both times I got the sort of look you get for asking what is north of north.

Pell showed me all of it in one day, badly, in the wrong order.


The **Name Chamber** is the biggest worked space in Underhand and it took my breath away, in the sense that I do not have breath and it still did something to me.

It is a long room and every surface of it is cut with names.

They go floor to ceiling in columns, hundreds of them, in a script of straight lines and small hooks.

They are not decoration. They are inventory.

All the names any Orruk in that settlement could take is on that wall, in order, with the common ones near the door where the light is good and the rare ones further in.

The chamber smells of cut stone and the dust the cutting makes. There is always some of it in the air. It gets on everything and everybody who works there has it in the creases of their skin permanently.

“Four hundred and six,” said Pell, when I asked. “In use. There’s more further back that nobody takes.”

“Have you counted them?”

“Everyone’s counted them,” said Pell, as if I had asked whether anyone had ever bothered to look at their own hands.


On the opposite wall are the **takings**.

That is the other half of it, and it is the part I kept coming back to. Every name anybody in Underhand has actually taken, cut in as it happened, going back generations. Thousands of them. Little rows of the same marks over and over, some names appearing hundreds of times, some appearing four times in two centuries.

You can stand there and read what six hundred years of Orruk decided to be.

I read it for a long time, and Pell got bored and went to find food.

## Chapter 5: Playing at Names

The soft were everywhere and they were all playing at names.

I do not mean they were talking about names, although they were doing that constantly. I mean that in every open chamber there were small pale creatures **pretending to be adults**, and it was the funniest thing I saw on that world.

Four of them doing a Carrier: three lying on top of each other in a stack and the fourth staggering around underneath, all of them shouting instructions.

One doing a Wallwalker, which involved getting about half a body-length up the wall and falling off, repeatedly, with total commitment.

One doing a Speaker, which mostly involved shouting.

Pell told me it had been a very good Deepdigger when it was smaller and offered to demonstrate. The demonstration involved lying flat and making a scraping noise.

“That’s not what they do.”

“That’s exactly what they do.”

“I’ve never seen one.”

“Neither have I,” said Pell. “Not properly. They’re always down.”


I ate.

Or rather, I floated beside a great many Orruk who ate, and asked them what they were eating, which is how I have learned everything I know about anywhere.

**Palm** is the staple. A pale fungus farmed in sheets on the passage walls, cut off in slabs, faintly sour. Everybody eats it and nobody has anything good to say about it.

**Crawlers** are the good food: small hard-shelled things kept in pens in the warm passages, roasted on hot stone. There is a whole art to it and everybody’s family does it the correct way and everybody else’s family does it wrong.

**Rockmilk** comes out of deep seeps, faintly sweet, drunk warm.

And there is **glowfruit**, which grows only where the veins are strongest and which is given almost entirely to the soft, and which Pell described to me at a length I will not reproduce.

I watched a Deepdigger eat at the next table and stopped counting at the fourth slab of palm.

“They eat a *lot*,” I said.

“Four times,” said Pell, with the weary precision of a creature who has heard this discussed all its life. “A digger eats four times what a Veintender does. It’s because of the...” It gestured at its own chest, which had nothing on it. “The everything.”

## Chapter 6: Looking for the Crack

I began checking things that afternoon, and this is the part I need to set down carefully.

On the last three worlds there had been a thing everybody was sure of that was not so. On the cliff world it was that distance belonged to somebody. On the water world it was that everyone inside you had been let in. On the bright plain it was that a lie had a change waiting behind it.

Three worlds, three cracks, and I had found all of them.

So I went looking for the crack in Orrun.

**Can somebody name you?** I asked four Orruk this and got four versions of the same answer, which is no, and it does not work if they try, and also do not go around asking that because it is the one thing here that is properly a crime.

**Are the good names kept for anybody?** No. Anyone may take anything. There is no permission and no rationing and no cost.

**Does your name decide your work?** No.

And this one surprised me.

Your name decides your *body*. What you then do with the body is your business. There is a Deepdigger in Underhand who keeps crawlers and has never dug anything, and everyone thinks it is a bit odd, and nobody has ever suggested she should stop.

**What happens if you never take one at all?**

I asked Pell this on the way back and got the first answer of the day that came slowly.

“Then you don’t.”

“Does that happen?”

“There’s three,” said Pell. “Here, I mean. There’s three.”

“What are they like?”

“Like me,” said Pell. “That’s what it is. You just stay like this.” And then, because it was thirteen and had run out of interest in the subject: “Are we eating?”

I did not ask anything else about it for a long time.

Every answer came back cheerful, immediate, and exactly the same as what the Orruk had already told me.

I made a note of that and did not think about it properly for another four days.

## Chapter 7: The Digger

At the end of the passage, coming up out of a shaft, I met Ganno.

He was squat and he was enormous. Not tall, but built like something that had been put together to survive being hit.

Arms thicker than my whole body. Claws worn down flat at the tips.

He came up out of the dark, blinked at the lit passage, squinted hard, and kept squinting.

“That’s a new one,” he said, looking at me. “What is it?”

“It doesn’t know,” said Pell.

“Fair enough,” said Ganno. “Neither did I at that age.”

And he went past us up the passage, still squinting, toward the bright end.

---

I went down with Ganno four days later, because I asked, and because he was the only adult in Underhand who did not seem to find my questions tiring.

The way down is a long worked stair and then a longer worked slope and then it stops being worked at all.

The light goes as you descend.

The glow-veins need tending. They are farmed, like everything else here, and nobody has had time to establish them in the cutting passages because the cutting passages are new by definition.

So you go down out of the amber into a kind of brown dimness and then into no light at all except what the diggers carry.

The air goes bad slowly. I noticed it as a change in the way Ganno breathed rather than anything I could detect myself.

And at the bottom, in the dark, six enormous creatures were taking a mountain apart.


He was superb.

Ganno at the rock face was the most impressive thing I saw on that world.

He read the stone: put a claw on it, listened, moved along, put a claw somewhere else.

Then he hit it once in a particular place and a piece the size of me came away clean.

“How did you know it would do that?”

“Grain.”

“I can’t see any grain.”

“No,” said Ganno, not unkindly. “You’ve got the wrong eyes.”

## Chapter 8: The Digging Song

The other five worked the same face and I watched them for a long time, because it was the first time I had seen what a name actually buys you.

They were not fast. Nothing about digging is fast.

What they were was *unstoppable* in a way that had nothing to do with effort.

Six creatures shaped for exactly one thing, doing it at a pace they could keep up for eleven hours, in air that was making my own edges feel strange and which none of them appeared to notice.

One of them, a female with a chipped shoulder-plate, was singing. Not out loud. She was tapping it, one claw against the face between strikes, and two of the others were tapping the answering part without stopping work, and it went on for most of an hour.

“What is that?”

“Digging song,” said Ganno. “It’s very old and it’s very stupid.”

“What are the words?”

“There aren’t any words. It’s tapping.”

“There must be something it means.”

Ganno hit the rock. A piece the size of Pell came off.

“It means we’re all still here,” he said. “That’s the whole of it. You tap, and somebody taps back, and then everybody knows nothing’s fallen on anybody.”

I listened to it for the rest of the shift after that and it stopped sounding stupid.


He talked the whole time and most of it was about other diggers’ work.

The channel two passages over was cut by somebody who had not looked at the seep line and was going to regret it in about nine years.

The chamber above us was beautiful work, forty years old, by a digger who had died before Ganno was named and whose corners he had been admiring his entire career.

There was a stretch of wall further along that had been done in a hurry by somebody he declined to name. He stopped in front of it and looked at it in silence for a while.

That was more damning than anything he could have said.

“Do you like it?” I asked. “The work.”

“It’s good work,” said Ganno.

Which I noticed was not what I asked, and did not notice properly for another two days.

---

He ate on the way up. I have never seen anything like it. Four slabs of palm, a bowl of crawlers, and a quantity of rockmilk that would have been a bath for Pell, and he did it standing up in about the time it takes to say so.

“You eat a lot.”

“I’m a lot.”

“Pell says four times what a Veintender eats.”

“Pell’s low,” said Ganno. “Ask a Veintender what they eat. Go on. Ask one. They’ll tell you *some fruit*.” He wiped his mouth with the back of an arm the size of my entire self. “*Some fruit.* And then they’ll be tired at midday and have a lie down.”

## Chapter 9: The Bright Passages

Then we came up into the bright passages and he stopped.

The vein passages are the most beautiful part of Orrun and I had not seen them properly until that evening.

They are the mid-levels, cultivated for hundreds of years, and the glow-veins there are not seams in the rock. They are a growth, tended and pruned and fed, running in great branching amber rivers across the ceiling and down the walls.

The light is thick. You can see the dust moving in it.

There were Veintenders up on frames, working. Fine-boned, quick-fingered, with eyes that could evidently see something in that light that I could not.

I had not understood, before that evening, what Veintending actually was. I had assumed it was farming, and it is, in the sense that everything on Orrun is farming.

But the work is *reading*.

A vein grows in a color that changes as it goes, and the change is slow.

What the tender is doing up on the frame is looking at a stretch of amber the length of her arm and deciding, from a difference I could not perceive at all, whether it is three days from turning, or eleven, and therefore whether it is cut now or left.

Get it right and the vein thickens and the passage gets brighter for forty years.

Get it wrong and you have killed a thing that takes two generations to replace.

One of them explained this to me while working, in the flat rapid way of somebody who has said it a thousand times and still likes saying it, and I watched her hands do something to a stem that took about four seconds and which she said afterward she could not have done ten years ago.

Ganno stopped in the middle of the passage and looked up at them.

He stood there for a while. Longer than you stop for something you see every day.

Then his eyes started to water, because they are built for the dark and had spent thirty years being built for the dark, and he squinted them almost shut and looked a moment longer anyway.

“Right,” he said, and went on up.


I asked him at the top, because I ask things.

“If you were choosing now, what would you take?”

Ganno did not break step.

“You don’t get to ask that,” he said. Lightly. The way you would say *mind the step*.

And then he asked me whether I had tried the crawlers done the way his sister does them, which is the correct way, and told me at some length why every other way is wrong.

## Chapter 10: The Wall’s Not Sad

The Markmaker’s name was Suth and she had been cutting names into the taking-wall for fifty years and she did not have time for me.

That is not quite fair. She had exactly as much time for me as she had for anybody, which was some, delivered briskly, while working.

“You’re the soft that floats.”

“Yes.”

“What do you want?”

“I want to know if anybody has ever taken a second name.”

“Yes,” said Suth. “Hundreds.”

I had not expected that.

“It doesn’t work,” she said, before I could ask. “Never has. You can stand in that ground and say whatever you like. I’ve watched people do it. I’ve watched people do it *sobbing*. Nothing happens, because the thing’s already gone where it’s going.” She blew dust off a cut. “It’s in the wall, if you want it. Second takings. We record them. We record everything.”

She showed me. There is a whole section, at the far end, going back generations: Orruk who went into the chamber a second time and said something and were not changed by it. Some of them tried three times.

“That’s sad,” I said.

“It’s the wall,” said Suth. “The wall’s not sad. The wall’s just what happened.”

## Chapter 11: Halfofboth

I kept going, because that is what I do, and because one closed door has never yet meant a wall.

**What about a compound name?** Fine. People do it. The stock grows that way. It is riskier because nobody knows exactly what an unusual pairing produces, but it is a taking like any other and it is just as permanent.

**What about an old name nobody has used for centuries?** You may. Anyone may. Nobody knows what you would get, because the outcome-record for most of them is lost.

**What if you took a name that means unnamed?**

Suth put down her tool for the first time in the entire conversation.

“That is the stupidest thing anyone has said to me in this chamber,” she said, “and last warm a young Orruk asked me whether he could take two names at once so he’d be *versatile*.”

“What did you tell him?”

“I told him he could take *Halfofboth* and find out.”

“Did he?”

“He took Channelcutter,” said Suth, “like everybody else with a bit of sense,” and went back to work.

## Chapter 12: What a Wall Is For

I liked her enormously and I do not think it was mutual for some time.

She got faster and shorter with me as the afternoon went on, and I worked out eventually that she had understood what I was doing before I had entirely understood it myself.

“You’re looking for a way round it.”

“I’m looking to see if there is one.”

“There isn’t.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I have cut names into this wall for fifty years,” said Suth, “and my teacher did it for forty before that, and hers before that, and every single thing you have asked me this afternoon somebody asked one of us already, and it is all *on the wall*.” She pointed with the tool. “That is what a wall is *for*.”


She had opinions about name fashion and I got all of them for free.

Certain names come in and out of style. There had been a run on **Highledge** about fifteen years ago because of a Speaker who had made a song about one, and about forty Orruk had taken it in four years, and now there were forty Highledges in a settlement that has work for perhaps nine.

“They’re fine,” said Suth. “They’re all fine. They stand about being decorative and they help with the crawler pens.” She cut a line. “In my day you took what there was work for and you were grateful.”

“Was that better?”

“No,” said Suth. “It was exactly the same and we were smug about it.”

## Chapter 13: When It Works

There was a showing that evening and Pell dragged me to it.

A young Orruk called Rin had taken **Waterworks** sixty days before and had finished changing, and everybody who lived along that passage came out to look at her.

It is a good custom. She came out and stood in the middle and everybody looked at her.

Flat now, and broad, with webbing between her digits that had not been there two months ago and cold-tolerant skin gone slightly blue at the edges.

She was mortified and delighted in about equal measure. Her family were unbearable about it, and everybody had an opinion about how she had come out.

*Good webbing.*

*Bit narrow across the back.*

*Her mother was narrow across the back.*

*You’ll grow into it.*

She was crying. Nobody remarked on it. Apparently everybody cries at their own showing and it would be strange if you did not.

I asked her, later, when the crowd had thinned, what the sixty days had been like.

“Horrible,” said Rin, cheerfully. “Everything aches. Not like being hit. Like being *stretched*. You wake up and your hands are a different shape and you have to learn them again, and then a week later you have to learn them again.”

“How do you eat?”

“Badly. You drop things for about a month.” She held up a hand and turned it over, admiring the webbing. “And you can’t sleep, because your bones are busy. Everybody warns you and nobody believes it.”

“Would you do it again?”

Rin looked at me as though I had asked whether she would like to be alive.

“It’s *done*,” she said. “That’s the good part. It’s done and I know what I am and I never have to think about it again.” She flexed the hand. “You have no idea what it’s like being soft. Everybody asking. Every single day, somebody asking, since you were six years old. And then one afternoon it’s finished and nobody ever asks you again for the rest of your life.”

She went off to be admired by more relatives.

I floated there for a while and thought about the fact that I had been asked eleven times that day.


There is a thing that happens at a showing that took me until the end to notice.

Everybody looks at the new adult, and then, at some point, everybody looks at the soft who came to watch.

Not obviously. Not unkindly. But every soft along that passage was standing at the front, and every adult was checking their faces, and I understood that a showing is not really for the one who changed.

It is an advertisement. It is six hundred creatures saying: *this is what it looks like when it works.*

And it does work. That is the part I could not get past. With the names they know, it works exactly the way they say it will.

## Chapter 14: The Mark

Afterward, walking back, I wandered into the deep end of the Name Chamber where the old unused names are and the light is poor.

And I saw a mark I knew.

I had seen it before. Cut in rock beside a broken ledge above a very long drop, on the first world I can remember arriving in. And once since, in the dark at the near side of the place I go between, so faint I would have gone straight past it.

It is a circle with a dot inside it and I have never known what it was.

It was on the wall in front of me, in a column of names nobody had taken in a hundred years, and it was cut in exactly the same script as everything else in that chamber, which meant that for the first time in my existence I was standing in a place where somebody could **read** it.

I moved closer.

“OUT.”

Suth came down the chamber at a speed I would not have credited.

I had gone past a line scratched in the floor that I had not noticed, into a section she was part-way through recutting, where a dozen old marks had been lifted and were sitting in the dust waiting to go back, and where one wrong knock would have cost her a week.

She was extremely angry. She walked me out by hovering behind me at a distance of about a hand.

“When can I go back in?”

“When I’ve finished.”

“When’s that?”

“When it’s *finished*,” said Suth. “There’s a hundred and nine marks lifted out of that wall and they go back in the order they came out and I am not having anything in there that floats.” She put a rope across the entrance, which I later learned she did perhaps twice in a decade. “Ten days. Twelve if the dust’s bad.”

So that was that.

It was eleven days before the rope came down, and by then a great deal had happened.

---

I ran out of questions on the fourth day.

I had asked everything I could think of and every answer had come back exactly as the Orruk had already said it was, and there was nothing under any of it, and I did not know what to do with that at all.

## Chapter 15: Stonesetter

There was a taking that afternoon and I have not been able to stop thinking about it.

Everybody came in. Perhaps two hundred Orruk, packed into the Name Chamber, with more in the passages outside, and everybody talking at once.

A soft called Ottu, fourteen, small, visibly terrified, walked out into the middle of the floor with its family standing along the wall.

And said, out loud, in front of all of them:

“Stonesetter.”

That was all. Two syllables. Then Suth cut it into the taking-wall while Ottu was still standing there, which takes about a minute and is the longest minute in an Orruk life, and the sound of the cutting was the only sound in that room.

Then it was done and everybody made a noise I have not heard anywhere else and Ottu’s family got to it first.

I asked Pell afterward, in the crush, what happens if you get halfway through saying it and stop.

“Nothing happens.”

“Has anybody?”

“Loads of people.” Pell was up on somebody’s back to see over the crowd. “You just go out and stand there and then you don’t say it and then you go home and everybody’s very nice about it and you come back next warm.”

“Is that shameful?”

“Why would it be shameful?”

“On some worlds it would be.”

“Then those are stupid worlds,” said Pell, with the total confidence of somebody who has never been to one. “It’s the biggest thing you ever do. Of course you can go home.”

There was food afterward, in the passage outside, and everybody ate an enormous amount of it and argued about crawlers. Somebody’s grandmother told me at length about her own taking, sixty-one years ago, and what she had nearly taken instead, and what became of the two other Orruk who took the same name the same warm.

She had thought about it every day for a year beforehand.

She said she had not thought about it once since.

Sixty days from then it would start to be a Stonesetter. In a year it would be one. In eighty years it would still be one, and it would be laid in a niche in the deep passages with that mark cut above it.

It had decided at fourteen and it would never have to decide again.

I did not know whether I had watched something wonderful or something terrible. I still don’t.

## Chapter 16: A Habit

I went back with Ganno, who had come up for it because Ottu is his sister’s boy.

“You’re quiet,” he said.

“I’ve been looking for something.”

“What?”

So I told him. I told him about the cliff world and the markers, and the water world, and the bright plain and the notches on the columns. I told him that on every world I have been to there is a thing everybody is certain of that is not quite so, and that finding it is the only thing I know how to do.

“And I’ve been here eleven days,” I said, “and I’ve checked everything I can think of, and there isn’t anything they’ve got wrong.”

Ganno thought about that.

“No,” he agreed.

“Everything is exactly what you all say it is. The naming works. It’s permanent. Nobody’s mistaken about any of it.” I had not said this out loud before and it came out worse than it had been in my head. “I keep expecting to find the crack and there isn’t one.”

Ganno walked for a while.

“Say it again,” he said. “The one about the water.”

So I told him about the water world properly.

I told him about the shells that cannot move, and the doors, and a creature who had something inside her that she never let in. I told him how everybody on that bank knew that anyone inside you had been admitted, and how that had been wrong for twenty years and nobody had ever counted.

“And you counted.”

“Somebody had to.”

“And they’re always glad? When you find the thing?”

I thought about it.

“No,” I said. “Not always. On the last one there was somebody who was ninety-one, who had hedged every sentence her whole life because she had been taught the sky might depend on it. She stood there while Ilva and Virr explained that the danger was much smaller than she’d thought, and then she went home. I don’t think I gave her anything. I think I took something off her.”

Ganno made a noise that was not quite a laugh.

“There you are, then,” he said.

“There I am what?”

“You go into places and you find the thing they’ve got wrong and you tell them.” He shifted the load he was carrying from one arm to the other without slowing down. “And you’ve come here and we haven’t got one, and it’s put you all out of shape.”

“That isn’t...”

“It’s a bit funny,” said Ganno.

“It isn’t funny.”

“It’s a bit funny,” said Ganno.

Then he said: “Why were you looking?”

---

I did not have an answer.

I have thought about it a great deal since. The best I can do is this: I had started treating worlds as things with faults in them. Arrive, find the fault, explain it to somebody, leave. Three for three.

I had got good at it and I had stopped noticing that it was a thing I was doing.

“Because it’s what I do,” I said, eventually.

“That’s not a reason. That’s a habit.”

“I know.”

“They’re easy to mistake for each other,” said Ganno. “I’ve got a few.”

## Chapter 17: Veintender

We came up into the bright passage and he stopped, the way he always stopped, and looked up at the veins and the Veintenders working on their frames.

And then, without any lead-up at all, and without looking at me:

“I took Deepdigger,” he said.

“I know.”

“Everyone in my family took Deepdigger. My mother, her brother, both of hers. Nobody said a word to me about it. That’s not allowed and they wouldn’t have anyway.” He was watching the frames. “It was just what was in the room. It was what got the good end of the table at a naming. I was fifteen and I wanted to be the thing that got the good end of the table.”

“That seems reasonable.”

“It was reasonable. That’s what nobody understands about it.” He squinted, and his eyes had begun to water, and he did not look away. “It was the sensible choice and I made it for sensible reasons and everybody in that chamber was pleased with me. Best day of my life at the time. I’m not being clever about it. It genuinely was.”

I waited.

“I should have taken Veintender,” he said.

He said it the way you would mention that it had been a long day.

Then he started walking again, and I did not know what to do, so I went with him and said nothing, which I think may have been the first genuinely useful thing I did on that world.

At his door he stopped.

“There’s something I want to ask you,” he said. “Not tonight.”

---

I did not sleep, because I do not, and I spent the whole night working on it.

## Chapter 18: Your Sister in the Crawler Pen

In the morning Pell and three other soft were sending tapping down the passages and I got pulled into it on the way past.

You knock on the stone and it carries. Everybody knows the pattern for *come here* and *food* and *not now*, and beyond that the soft have built up an enormous unofficial vocabulary that the adults either do not know or pretend not to.

There is a rhythm that means *the Highledges are being ridiculous again*.

There is one that means *somebody is coming, look busy*.

There is a very long one that Pell taught me twice, and which took most of an hour, and which means, as far as I was able to establish, *your sister has fallen in the crawler pen*.

“Is that useful?”

“It was useful *once*,” said Pell.

They had me tapping messages to a chamber three passages over for most of the morning, and the answers came back, and about half of them were insults about my rhythm.

I have been given a great many things on a great many worlds. That morning is one of the ones I kept.

## Chapter 19: Five Things

Then I went and found Ganno, and I brought him what I had.

I had been thorough, which matters, because of what happens next.

I had not gone back to retaking, or compound names, or the old names in the deep end. I had established four days earlier that those do not work, and a creature that presents you with something it has already disproved is not helping, it is performing.

What I had was practical.

“You can dig mornings,” I said. “Half-shifts. There’s nothing that says a digger has to do a full face.”

“There isn’t.”

“So you’d have afternoons in the bright passages.”

“Go on.”

“You could pair with a Veintender. You do the heavy work: the frames, the anchor points, the moving. They do the fine work. Neither of you is doing the part you can’t do.”

“Right.”

“Your tools are wrong. All of them. They’re made for hands like a Veintender’s because Veintenders make them, and nobody has ever made a vein tool with a thick haft, because nobody with your claws has ever wanted one for long enough to ask.” I had been quite pleased with this one. “Somebody could make you one. I asked. Sath’s mother makes handles.”

Ganno did not say anything.

“And your eyes hurt in the light. There’s shell they use over the crawler pens for shade. You could have a piece cut for you. You’d see less well, but you’d see for longer.”

“Anything else?”

“The big old veins near the living passages don’t need fine work. They need bulk pruning. That’s not delicate. You could do that now, today, and nobody would think anything of it.”

I stopped.

I had five things and I had thought about all of them and none of them was stupid.

## Chapter 20: Permission to Visit

Ganno waited to be sure I was finished.

Then he said: “Half-shifts I did for two years. Pairing I tried with a Veintender called Hesk who was very kind about it and who I could not stand being pitied by. The bulk pruning I do. I’ve done it for years. I’ve got a vein outside my own door I’ve been tending since I was twenty-four.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You didn’t ask,” said Ganno, without any edge. “The eye-shade is new. I hadn’t thought of that and it might help and I’ll try it. That’s a good one.”

“Then...”

“I didn’t ask you to fix it,” said Ganno.


I did not have anything to say to that.

“I went and did all of this,” he said. “All of it, and more than that, when I was twenty-two. I spent a year in the vein passages. Do you know what I found out?”

“No.”

“That I could stand in the light.”

He was not squinting now; we were in his own quarters and the light was low and his eyes were comfortable, and I remember noticing that.

“I could stand in it any time I wanted,” said Ganno. “Nobody ever stopped me. Nobody has ever stopped me in thirty years. I can walk up there tonight and stand under those veins until I fall over.” He turned one of his hands over. “What I can’t do is *see* it. Not the way they see it. Hesk could look at a vein and tell you it was three days off turning and I could look at the same vein for an hour and see a light.”

“So the thing you wanted...”

“Wasn’t the job,” he said. “Everybody thinks it’s the job. It isn’t the job. I’ve had the job, near enough, and it was worse than not having it.” He put the hand down. “I wanted the eyes. I wanted the hands. I wanted to be the thing that lives in that light, and what I’ve got is permission to visit.”

## Chapter 21: What You See

“There isn’t anything I can do about that,” I said.

“No,” said Ganno. “There isn’t.”

“Then what do you want?”

And he told me.

“Tell me what you see when you look at me,” he said. “Not the digging. Everybody here has known me for thirty years and every one of them looks at me and sees a good digger, and they’re right, and I can’t say a word about it to any of them without it sounding like I’m complaining about a life half of them would want.” He looked at me with his small dim eyes. “You never saw me choose. You’re the only thing here that never saw me choose.”

---

I tried.

Here is what I actually said, and it was not good enough.

I said that he was the best digger in Underhand.

I said that he could read stone by touch and that I had watched him take a face apart in one hit.

I said that he had eaten four slabs of palm in the time it takes to say so and that Pell had underestimated it.

I said that his corners were good and that other diggers said so.

I said, I think, eleven true things.

Ganno listened to all of them.

And then he said, “Thank you,” in a way that meant we were finished, and I understood that I had answered a completely different question and that he had been kind about it, and that being kind about it was the worst part.

I went away and did not know how to go back.

## Chapter 22: Wend

There are three Orruk in Underhand who never took a name, and I met one of them because I got lost.

Her name was Wend and she was sixty years old and she was soft.

That is the whole of it. She was the size of Pell and shaped like Pell and had the same wide dim eyes and the same unfinished look, and she was sixty, and she was sitting in a small warm chamber off a side passage sorting crawler shells into two piles for reasons that were entirely her own.

“You’ll be the floating one,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Sit down.”

“I can’t.”

“No,” she agreed. “Well. Hover, then.”


She was not sad and she was not brave about it, and both of those would have been easier to write down.

She was comfortable. She had lived in that chamber for a long time. She could not dig or climb or carry or cut, and everybody fed her, and she sorted shells and knew everything about everybody and appeared to find the whole arrangement perfectly satisfactory.

We talked for most of an afternoon about nothing much.

Eventually I asked.

“Why didn’t you take one?”

And Wend said, “Have you had the crawlers done the way Ganno’s sister does them?”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No,” said Wend, pleasantly, and told me about the crawlers.

I did not ask again. I have asked every rude question I could think of on four worlds and that afternoon I let one go, and I don’t know whether that was manners or cowardice.

It was only walking back that I noticed the thing.

She had not asked me what I was going to take.

Every single Orruk in that settlement had asked me: Pell in the first minute, Sath off the ceiling, Suth twice, Ganno’s sister, two Carriers, a Speaker, the entire crawler-pen family, and a Highledge who then told me at length what I should take and why.

Wend had had a whole afternoon and had not mentioned it.

## Chapter 23: The Blank Ones

The way to the deep end of the Name Chamber goes past the niches, and I had been past them a dozen times before I understood what I was looking at.

They are cut into the walls of the lower passages, at about chest height, one after another for as far as the worked stone goes. Each one is the length of an Orruk and about as deep, and there is somebody in every one of them, and above each one there is a name.

You walk to the deep end of Underhand past everyone who has ever lived there.

I stopped, that evening, because I had time.

The nearest ones are recent and the marks are sharp. Further down they get older and the cutting gets shallower and in places somebody has been back and recut a name that was wearing away, which is apparently a thing you do for your own family and which nobody organizes.

There are Carriers and Stonesetters and Channelcutters. There are eleven Deepdiggers in a row, which is a family, and Ganno’s mother is the fourth of them.

And every so often, one has no name above it at all.

I asked Pell about those the next day and got the shortest answer I ever got out of Pell.

“They didn’t take.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s all. You get a niche either way.” Pell thought about it. “Somebody has to cut something. If there isn’t a name there’s nothing to cut.”

So they leave it blank, and put you in the wall with everybody else, and that is the whole of it.

I went and looked at the blank ones for a while. There were more than I expected and they were spread all through, in among the named, going back as far as the cutting goes.

Nobody had ever mentioned them to me. Not once, in a month, in a settlement where everybody talks about naming constantly.

## Chapter 24: That’s a Name

I went back for the mark that evening.

Suth was in the deep end with her tools and her lamp and the recut section finished and back in the wall, and she let me in, and I took her to the column and showed her.

“That one. What is it?”

Suth looked at it for about as long as it takes to look at a thing you have seen ten thousand times.

“That’s a name,” she said.

I did not say anything.

“Old one,” she said. “Nobody’s taken it in a very long time.”


I had gone in with several questions and most of them fell over, because they were the wrong shape for that world. A name here is not a person’s. Nobody would ask *whose* it is any more than you would ask whose *Carrier* is. It belongs to everybody and to nobody and it had been sitting on that wall longer than anybody present could account for.

So I asked the two questions that fit.

“What would it make you?”

“Don’t know,” said Suth. “Outcome-record’s gone. Some of them we’ve lost. That end of the chamber’s older than the recording.” She held her lamp up to it and looked at it a bit more carefully than she had. “Nobody’s taken it in long enough that nobody knows what it does. That’s most of what’s down here.”

“Has anybody ever taken it?”

“Not in my fifty years,” said Suth. “Before that I couldn’t tell you.”

Then she picked her tools back up, because it was a wall and she has four hundred of them.

I had a question I did not ask.

## Chapter 25: The Thing Properly

I went and found Ganno, and I did the thing properly this time.

He was outside his own door with his vein, which is about the length of my body and grows on the wall beside his entrance and is, I can now say with some authority, in poor condition.

“You’re back.”

“I got it wrong.”

“You got it fine.”

“I got it wrong,” I said. “You asked me what I see and I told you what you do. Those aren’t the same and I knew they weren’t while I was saying it.”

Ganno put down what he was holding.


I was not good at it. I am not eloquent and I did not become eloquent that evening, and what came out was slow and in the wrong order.

I said that every evening he comes up out of the dark and stops in the bright passage, and that he stops for longer than anybody stops for something they see every day, and that he stands there until his eyes water and then he stands there a bit longer.

I said that he had a vein outside his own door that he had been failing to grow for twenty-six years, and that he could have stopped at any point and had not.

I said that he had talked to me about other diggers’ corners with more feeling than he had ever used about his own work.

I said that when he told me about Hesk, the thing in his voice was not that he had been bad at the work. It was that he had been pitied while he did it.

And then I said the thing I had, which was not much, and which did not come out well.

I said: I don’t think you’re looking at the light.

I said: I think you’re looking at them. The ones who can see it.

I said there is probably a word for wanting something like that, and I do not know it, and I have been to four worlds now and I have never once wanted anything the way you want that.

And I said: you have wanted it for thirty years and you have never said it out loud to anybody.

Then I stopped, because I had run out.

---

Ganno did not say anything for a while.

He picked his tool back up eventually and did something to the vein that I do not think helped it.

“Right,” he said.

That was all. And then, after another while: “It’s a terrible vein.”

“It’s a terrible vein,” I agreed.

“Twenty-six years.”

“Yes.”

“You’d think it’d have picked a side.”

He went on working at it, badly, in the dark outside his door, and I stayed and watched him do it, and nothing was fixed and nothing was going to be.

He would go down again in the morning. He went down the next morning and the morning after that.

But somebody knew, and there had not been anybody who knew.

## Chapter 26: Channelcutter

Pell took a name eleven days later.


The whole of Underhand came, because the whole of Underhand comes to all of them. The Name Chamber was packed to the walls and the passages outside were full and Pell’s family had been arguing about where to stand since the previous evening.

Pell had been sick twice that morning and told everybody about it.

It went out into the middle of the floor. It looked very small out there, the way they all do, and it stood in the place where the floor is worn down from a thousand years of creatures standing in exactly that spot, and it did not say anything for long enough that somebody coughed.

Then it said:

“Channelcutter.”


Which is nothing. Which is the most ordinary name in the stock. There are perhaps a hundred Channelcutters in Underhand and they keep the water running and it is steady, unglamorous, entirely necessary work that nobody has ever written a song about.

Pell had been agonizing for a year. The list had been nine names long and then fourteen and then six. It had found me twice in the middle of the night to discuss it.

And it stood there while Suth cut it into the wall, and when it was done it turned around with its whole face lit up, and it was the happiest I have ever seen a creature be.

Its family got to it. Somebody was crying. Somebody had brought food, which is apparently traditional and is also apparently a running argument, because somebody else had brought the same food.

Pell found me about an hour later, in the crush, and came over at a run and stopped and looked at me with its whole unfinished face.

“Well?”

“Well what?”

“Was it *good*?” said Pell. “Did I say it well? Sath says I said it too quiet. Did I say it too quiet?”

“You said it exactly loud enough.”

“Suth had to cut it *twice*, she said the first stroke went wrong...”

“Everybody heard you.”

“Right,” said Pell. “Right. Good.” It stood there vibrating slightly. “Channelcutter.”

“Channelcutter.”

“That’s what I am.”

“That’s what you are.”

“Say it again.”

I said it again about nine times over the following hour, and every single time Pell reacted as though it were news.

I did not say anything to Pell about Ganno. Ganno was there, at the back, and he did not look troubled, because there was nothing to look troubled about. A soft had chosen something sensible and was delighted.

In sixty days Pell will start to change. In a year it will be cutting channels. In thirty years it will know whether it was right, and nobody in that chamber could tell it, and it would not have listened.

## Chapter 27: Ringing

There was ringing that night, which I had heard three times from a distance since I arrived and had never once been in the room for.

It is the nearest thing Underhand has to music and it is made out of the building.

There is a name for it. Ringers are adored, and Underhand has two.

A Ringer goes round a chamber with a short hafted mallet and strikes the walls. Not randomly.

Every chamber in that settlement has places on it that ring, and a good Ringer knows where they are in every chamber she has ever worked. The whole art is knowing which stone in which chamber will do what.

The Name Chamber is the best instrument in Underhand. Everybody says so. It is high and wide and the far end is cut into columns of names, and when you strike the wall behind the takings the sound comes back off four hundred years of small cuts and arrives changed.

I expected it to be interesting. I did not expect it to be beautiful.

It went on for two hours.

It is slow. Much slower than I would have guessed, with long gaps where the last strike is still coming back. About half of it is the room rather than the mallet.

The Ringer that night was old and worked in a wide circle and never once hurried, and six hundred creatures sat on the floor of that chamber in the amber light and did not talk.

Pell fell asleep on somebody.

Ganno was against the back wall with his eyes shut, which is the only time on that world I saw him comfortable in a lit room.

## Chapter 28: The Quiet Showing

There was a quiet showing two days after.

An Orruk called Merrow had taken a compound name out of the deep end. Nobody had used it in three hundred years.

She had finished changing, and it had not come out well.

Not wrong. Nothing had failed. It had simply produced a body that was long and thin and oddly jointed and good for nothing anybody in Underhand currently needs.

She had known the risk. Everyone knew the risk. That is what taking an old name is.

Everybody came out anyway. Same as for Rin. Everybody stood and looked, and nobody said *good webbing* or *bit narrow across the back*, and nobody said much at all, and then everybody went and ate together for a long time.

It is the kindest thing I saw on that world and it took about an hour and nobody explained it to me.

## Chapter 29: So Take One

Suth offered me a name the day before I left.

She did it in the Name Chamber, briskly, while working, in exactly the tone she would have used on any soft who had been hanging about her wall for a month.

“You’ve been in here every day for four weeks.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve read more of that wall than most Orruk read in a lifetime.”

“Probably.”

“So take one,” said Suth.


She was being kind, and that is the part that stayed with me. It was not a test and it was not a challenge; it was a professional making a sensible suggestion to a young creature who had been dithering for a month, and she went straight back to cutting afterward as though she had recommended a good place to eat.

“I don’t think I can,” I said.

“Everybody can.”

“I don’t think I’ve got the thing that changes.”

Suth stopped and looked at me properly, possibly for the first time.

“Hm,” she said.

Then she went back to it, and I thought that was the end, and about a minute later she said, without looking up:

“I cut the niches as well. Did you know that?”

“No.”

“Same tools. Same hand.” She blew dust off the line she was working. “Fifty years I’ve been putting names on this wall and putting the same names on the other one at the far end when they’re finished with them. Sometimes the same Orruk twice in a lifetime. I cut Ganno’s mother in.”

I did not say anything.

“Every so often I don’t cut anything,” said Suth. “There’s nothing to put. You get the niche the same as anybody, you just get it blank.” She started the next stroke. “It’s not a punishment. Nobody’s ever thought that. It’s only that there’s a space on the wall and I’ve got nothing to write in it.”

“Does that bother you?”

Suth considered it with the seriousness of somebody being asked about their actual trade.

“It bothers *me*,” she said. “It’s a wall with a gap in it. But it never seems to have bothered them.”

And then: “Well. It’ll be here.”

## Chapter 30: A Worse Question

I have turned that over since.

Not whether I should have.

Whether I *could*: whether there is anything in me for a name to take hold of, or whether I would have stood in that ground and said a word into a room full of Orruk and had absolutely nothing happen, in front of all of them.

And underneath that, the part I did not say to Suth: I do not know if I want to find out.

On three worlds I have gone looking for what I am. I have been assuming the answer is out there somewhere, hidden, waiting for somebody sufficiently careful.

Orrun is the first place that offered me the other possibility. That six hundred creatures around me are not what they discovered. They are what they said out loud, once, as children, in a chamber, and then became.

I did not take a name. I was not ready and I do not think I will be for a long time.

But I have stopped assuming there is an answer being kept from me somewhere, and started wondering whether there is one at all, and that is a much worse question and I think it is the right one.

## Chapter 31: Right

I said goodbye to Wend, who was sorting shells, and who told me about the crawlers again, and who still did not ask.

I said goodbye to Pell, who had begun to ache in the shoulders, which is the first sign, and who was insufferable about it.

Ganno walked me down.

We did not talk about any of it.

He told me about a chamber four levels down that he was going to start next warm, and how he intended to bring the roof, and why the digger before him had got it wrong.

He was still going to be doing this in twenty years. He said so cheerfully.

At the bottom of the worked stair he stopped, because that is as far as the light goes.

“Right,” he said.

“Right,” I said.

And that was the whole of it, and it was enough, and both of us knew it.

## Chapter 32: The Way Down

I went down.

Past the cutting passages and the six enormous creatures taking a mountain apart in the dark. Past the last chamber anybody has finished. Past the last chamber anybody has started.

To where the worked stone stops and the rock is only rock, and there is no light at all, and nobody has ever needed to come.

A dead end. Red stone, unopened, going nowhere.

And it opened.

The black sky. The white line. The shape.

I have no idea why it was there and not somewhere else, and I have stopped expecting to be told.

---

The last thing I did on Orrun was look back up the way I had come: at the dark, and then the dim brown, and then a long way above, the amber of the vein passages where the light is thick and the frames are and where somebody was going to stop this evening and stand there until his eyes watered.

Then I went forward.
