## Chapter 1: What Turns Up

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to thirteen worlds before this one. I arrive without warning, in whatever place the crossing puts me, and what happens next is usually the same: nothing. Nobody is expecting me. Nobody knows what I am. I float about for a day or two working out what the world runs on, and eventually somebody notices me and is startled, and we go from there.

For the last four worlds I have also been noticing something I used not to notice: when I stay, when I go, and whether either of those is actually being decided by me.

That is how it had gone thirteen times.

This time I came out in a walled yard, in daylight, about four feet off a swept stone floor, and a person shouted.

Not in fear. In the voice of somebody at work who has seen a thing and is passing it on.

“Live one! Weighing floor!”

And then somebody came and got me.

---

I want to be precise about how strange this was, because it took me most of the first day to understand that nothing strange had happened at all.

The person who came for me arrived at a fast walk, carrying a frame, which is a wooden thing with straps made for lifting. They were broad and low, about waist-height to a person if you know people, and they went on four short thick limbs with a fifth rising out of the shoulder, which is the one they work with. It is like an arm and it is not like an arm. It is thicker than mine would be if I had one, and it moves in every direction, and it ends in something between a hand and a hook.

They stopped in front of me and looked at me for perhaps two seconds.

“Can you move on your own?”

I said I could.

“Right. Weighing floor. Follow me, don’t go up, there’s a beam.”

And they turned round and walked off, and I followed, and that was my arrival on that world.

Nobody was startled. Nobody ran to fetch anybody. Nobody asked me what I was.

---

The weighing floor is a flat stone area at the north end of the yard with a set of scales built into it, a rack of measuring poles, and a bench where somebody sits with a slate.

They measured me.

They put a pole up against me and read off a number. They tried to put me on the scales and I weigh essentially nothing, so the person with the slate wrote a mark that means *under.* They asked whether I could be moved by hand and I said not really. They asked whether I ate anything and I said no. They asked whether I would die if left in a dark room and I said not as far as I knew, and they wrote that down too.

Then two of them had an argument about me.

It was a genuine argument, conducted about a foot from me, and it was about whether I counted as an object or an animal, which apparently has procedural consequences that neither of them was willing to concede. Objects go one way. Animals go another. Something that talks and does not eat and cannot be lifted was, in the words of the older one, *exactly the sort of thing that ruins a Tuesday.*

I said I would rather be an animal, if it made any difference.

They both stopped and looked at me and then the older one said, “It’s got a preference,” in the tone of somebody whose day has just got worse, and wrote something on the slate.

---

Here is what I could see from the weighing floor, and what it took me another day to believe.

The yard is enormous. Walls all round, higher than they need to be. Inside them: sheds, pens, tanks, a covered area with tables, and doors going down. There are people working in every direction and they are all busy.

And in the middle of it, on the swept stone, is nothing. A wide empty space that nobody crosses if they can go round it, kept clear, swept twice a day.

That is where things arrive.

Not falling. Not appearing in a flash of anything. A thing is not there, and then the thing is there, and usually it is confused.

It happens several times a week. It has happened for as long as anybody has any record of, which on that world is three hundred years, and before that for as long as there are stories, and before that presumably as well.

Nobody knows why.

---

I asked.

Of course I asked. I have been to thirteen worlds and every one of them has had a rule that shouts. A world where the ground remembers being walked on, a world where sounds come back, a world where a family’s weight is a number you can read off a person. On every one of those worlds you could put a question to the world and the world would do something about it. Not answer, exactly. But respond. There was always a channel.

So on the first evening, standing over the swept empty middle of that yard with nobody much about, I asked it.

I asked something ordinary. I asked what was coming next.

I said it out loud, the way you would ask a room.

Nothing happened.

I want to be exact about what nothing means here, because I have had things not answer me before and it has always felt like being refused. This did not feel like that. There was no pause. There was no sense of something declining to speak. It was like asking a wall what it thought, except that I have been on a world where you could reasonably ask a wall.

A fetcher going past with an empty frame slowed down and looked at what I was looking at, which was nothing, and then at me.

I said I had asked it a question.

“Asked what a question?”

I said the world. The place. Whatever puts the things there.

And the fetcher said this. I want it written exactly, because it is the most important thing anybody said to me on that world, and it was said in complete innocence by somebody who wanted to get their frame put away:

**”Why would it?”**

Not *it doesn’t work like that.* Not *nobody’s ever managed it.* Not *we gave that up generations ago.*

**Why would it.**

The way you would say it if somebody told you they had asked the weather for its opinion. Not sad. Not resigned. **Puzzled that the question had occurred to anybody.**

I said no reason, and the fetcher said right, and went and put the frame away.

I did not ask again.

---

They put me in a room.

It is off the west side of the yard, third along, and it is a perfectly good room: dry, dark if you shut the cover, about the size of a large cupboard, with a bracket in the ceiling that I understand is for hanging things from and a channel in the floor for water that does not apply to me.

The person who showed me in was the older one from the argument, whose name is Ravel. Ravel said it would do for now and that they would sort out something permanent in due course, and left.

Nobody had explained anything.

Nobody had told me where the things came from, or why, or what happened to them afterward, or what I was expected to do, or how long I was staying.

And I floated in the dark in a good room in a walled yard on a world that had not so much as twitched when I spoke to it, and understood, slowly, that nobody had explained anything because **nobody there thought anything needed explaining.**

## Chapter 2: Fetchers and Setters

In my first week at the Fetching Ground, three things arrived.

A crate came first, on the Tuesday, about the size of a table and made of a material nobody could identify. It took four of them to move it. Nobody had opened it by the time the plant arrived, because the argument about the correct way to open it was still going on.

On the Thursday a load of grain arrived, perhaps two cartloads of it, in a heap on the swept stone, and this was greeted with real pleasure, because grain is worth money and does not have to be argued about.

And on the Saturday morning a plant arrived. Rooted, in a lump of soil that was not soil from anywhere on Anneth, about my height, with leaves like flat hands.

The plant is what showed me the job.

---

The people who go and get things are called fetchers. It is the most visible work on the Ground and the least skilled, and fetchers know it and are cheerfully rude about everybody else all day long. There are nine of them on the day shift.

The people who decide what a thing needs are called setters. There are two, and Ravel is the senior one, and it is the only job on that Ground that anybody is quiet about.

Here is what a setter does.

Something arrives that nobody has ever seen. It might be alive. It might die in an hour in the wrong air, or an hour in the right air, or it might be a rock. Nobody is going to tell you which. There is no label, no sender, no explanation, and no way to ask.

**And you have to decide, that morning, where to put it.**

Ravel walked round that plant for about six minutes. Touched the soil. Smelled it. Looked at the leaves against the light and then away from the light. Asked for a bowl of water and put a leaf-tip in it and watched. Went and looked at the swept stone where it had arrived, which tells you nothing, and looked anyway.

Then Ravel said: north tank, half-shade, brackish, and if the edges go yellow by Thursday move it to the cold room and don’t wait to ask.

I asked how much of that was known.

“None of it,” said Ravel. “You look at it and you decide.”

I asked what happens when a setter is wrong.

“Then it dies,” said Ravel, “and you write down what you did, so the next one has a better guess.”

---

Which brings me to the book.

The book is three hundred years of everything that has ever arrived at the Fetching Ground and what was done about it. It is kept by a person called Tollen, who is in the seventies, and it is not one book. It is about forty, in a room with a stove, and Tollen knows most of it by heart in the way you know a house you grew up in.

Tollen came for me on the second day with a slate and a look of anticipation.

“You’re not written up. Nobody wrote you up. There’s a live one on the floor for two days and no entry.” Tollen was delighted about this in the way people are delighted by an outrage they can fix. “Right. Kind?”

I said I did not know what that meant.

“What are you. Object, material, plant, animal, person, other.”

I said I did not know.

“Say person for now, we can amend.” Tollen wrote. “Arrived. I’ve got the day off the fetcher. Condition on arrival, well. Disposition, west three, temporary.” More writing. “Origin?”

I said I did not know that either.

And here is the moment, and it went by very fast.

Tollen wrote **unknown**, in the column, without stopping, without looking up, without the smallest change of any kind, and moved on to the next column.

I said, because I have been on thirteen worlds and on most of them this is where the conversation becomes about me, that I meant I genuinely did not know. Not that I would not say. That there was nothing there. No memory of before, no name, nothing at all.

“Yes,” said Tollen. “Unknown.”

I asked whether that was not remarkable.

Tollen put the slate down and gave me perhaps four seconds of real attention, which from Tollen is a great deal, and then reached along the shelf and pulled out a volume at random and opened it at random and turned it round so I could see.

Two thirds of the entries said **unknown** in the origin column.

“That’s a Tuesday in the eleventh year of Sennick,” said Tollen. “Four things. Nobody knows where any of them came from. Nobody knew then either.” Tollen took the book back. “It’s a column. It has to say something. It says unknown.”

Then Tollen wrote something in the kind column, and I asked what it was, and Tollen said: “Unanswered.”

I knew the word. Or I knew a word that came to me the same way. An old Kesh had used it for something else entirely, and in a memory older than anybody living on Anneth I had heard somebody say *for the unanswered* beside a crossing being made. Neither of those had been a filing category.

I did not decide that the words meant the same thing. I asked what this one meant.

---

I got the only definition of it that Anneth has.

An arrival is *unanswered* when it can direct itself and talk to you, but **there is no answerable origin to be had for the record.** Not that it will not say. Not that it has forgotten because something hit it on the head. **That there is nothing there to be given.**

I asked how you would know that about a thing that could not yet talk.

“You wouldn’t,” said Tollen. “You put pending. You come back to it.”

Apparently that happens. A thing arrives that cannot answer questions because it is too small or too frightened or too newly whatever it is, and the kind column says *pending* for a year or four or however long, and then somebody asks it, and it either has an origin or it does not, and the column gets filled in.

I asked how many unanswered there had been.

“Not many,” said Tollen, and did not say a number, and went off to do the entry properly, and that was that.

**That is the whole of what the word means on Anneth.** It is not a mystery, or a species, or a category of being. **It is a column heading.** They needed somewhere to put self-directing arrivals with nothing in the origin box, and three hundred years ago somebody chose a word, and the word stuck, and it means exactly as much as *object* and *animal* mean, which is: enough to file it.

Whether the older uses of the word had anything to do with this one, I could not tell. A repeated word is not proof of a repeated meaning. I had learned that much.

---

The book is also, I should say, the single funniest thing on that Ground.

Because it is not only a record. It is the last word in every argument.

I watched a dispute about the north tank, specifically whether the plant should go in it at all given what was already in it, get settled by Tollen coming in without being invited, carrying a volume with a finger in it, and reading out, at length, with enormous relish, what happened in the fourth year of Marrow when somebody put two things in one tank.

Everybody groaned. Everybody knew what was coming. Nobody could argue with it, because it happened.

Tollen does this about twice a week and has done for fifty years.

---

And on my fourth day I got a job.

Her name is Brit, she fetches on the day shift, and she came and found me while I was watching them move the crate.

“You don’t sleep.”

I said I did not.

“You said that to the weighing floor. It’s on the slate.” Brit sat down on a barrel, which is what Annethi do instead of leaning. “How would you like to do the round.”

The round is this. Every room on the Ground gets inspected on a rota: every shed, pen, tank, cold room, dark room and deep room, whether or not there is anything in it. You go in, you look properly, you check the damp and the airflow and the temperature and whether anything has got in through anything, and you report what you find to somebody who can fix it.

There are four hundred and six rooms.

Most of them are empty. Some of them have been empty for decades. There is a room on the east side built for something that arrived once, in Tollen’s grandmother’s time, and has not been used since, and it is inspected on the same rota as everything else.

“Why?” I said. “If it’s empty.”

“Because the one time you don’t,” said Brit, “is the time.”

Brit hates the round. Brit has worked out how many hours of her life she has spent doing the round and told me the number twice and it is not a small number. And she had spotted, before anybody else on that Ground, that there was now a thing living in west three that did not sleep, did not eat, could go anywhere, and had no other duties.

“You’d only be looking,” she said. “You can’t fix anything. You’ve got nothing to fix it with.”

I said that was true of me almost everywhere.

“Well then,” said Brit.

So I took the round. For once, somebody had given me a job I could do.

## Chapter 3: Eleven Rooms

The round took me underground on the second night, and that is where I found the thing that was actually wrong.

Under the Fetching Ground, cut into the rock, there are eleven rooms.

They are old. They were cut when the Ground was cut, which is three hundred years and change, and they are cold and dry-aired and completely dark, and they are the best rooms on that whole site. The Annethi see well in the dark. Their eyes are set wide and low and they are more comfortable underground than up in the light, so the deep rooms are not a dungeon to anybody there. They are the good rooms. The careful rooms.

Four of them are standing in water.

Not flooded to the ceiling. Standing. A hand’s depth in two of them, more in the others, water that comes up through the floor and does not go away and has been getting worse for about ten years.

Two more are damp in a way Ravel does not like.

That leaves five.

---

I asked what the deep rooms are for, and it is the only question I asked on that world that got a serious answer.

“Living arrivals,” said Ravel. “The ones that can’t be up here.”

Perhaps twice in a generation something arrives that cannot take the light, or cannot take the air, or is simply so frightened that being in an open yard with sixty Annethi walking about will kill it before anything else does. You cannot ask it what it needs. **You have to guess, fast, and the deep rooms are where you put a thing while you guess.**

Cold, dark, still, quiet. If you are wrong you have not immediately made it worse.

“And without them?”

“Then it stops in the yard,” said Ravel, “while we work it out. And some of them we’d work out in time.”

Nobody has died of it. Ravel was clear about that and said it twice. In ten years of the water coming up, nothing has arrived that needed a room they did not have.

“That’s not a plan,” said Ravel. “That’s luck. I’ve been saying so for three years and I’m tired of the sound of it.”

---

Here is what the Fetching Ground can do about it, and there are three answers, and every one of them is defensible, and the town has been arguing about it for three years without moving.

**Cut new rooms.** West side, deeper, into better rock. Permanent. If you leave out what it costs and how long it takes, it is plainly the right answer. Tollen can name eleven arrivals in three hundred years of the book that would have died in a yard, and says so, and is not wrong. It would take four years of everything the Ground earns.

**Line and pump the rooms they have.** Seal them, put in a pump, keep the water down. Cheap. Quick. Buys perhaps fifteen years, and everybody agrees on the perhaps.

**Build up instead of down.** Above-ground rooms with forced dark and a cooling method that has never been tried on Anneth by anybody. Cheaper than cutting by a long way. Might not work, and if it does not work you find out in the worst possible circumstances.

Ravel wants the lining. Not because Ravel thinks it is best. Ravel has said so out loud to me, unprompted. It is because Ravel has watched two better plans die of being unaffordable and would rather have fifteen years in hand than be correct again next winter.

Tollen wants the cutting, and has the book on his side, and has had it for three years.

And the third one is Yesh’s.

---

I should explain how the Ground pays for things, because everything that happened afterward runs off it.

There is one capital purse a summer, meaning what is left after wages and food and the ordinary running of the place. There is one construction gang, which is eleven people and their tools and the winter draft animals. The gang works the summer. That is when you can dig on that plain at all.

One purse. One gang. **One summer’s works.**

A major build takes the gang and the summer. So does a major cut. **You cannot do two major works in one summer**, because there is one gang and one summer and the frosts come when they come. Every Annethi on that Ground has known this since childhood the way you know that water is wet.

What I did not know yet was what they counted as *works*. Nobody had needed to teach me the edges of a category everybody there had grown up inside.

*(What money is left over at the end of a year, plus whatever the winter market brings in, pays for repairs, meaning the ordinary maintenance that gets done in the cold months when the gang is not on a summer works. It is not enough to build anything. It is enough to mend.)*

---

Yesh is nineteen and is the setter’s assistant and is unanswered, and I did not find either of those things out from Yesh.

Yesh is a head taller than everybody else on that Ground and stands upright on two legs, long-limbed and thin, with two hands that are almost uselessly weak by Annethi standards. Every latch, rope, handle and door on Anneth is built for one strong grip, and Yesh has spent nineteen years dealing with that. There is a stool at the counting bench that is Yesh’s stool. There is a modified latch on the drawing-room door that somebody made about twelve years ago without any particular fuss.

Nobody finds any of this remarkable. Yesh arrived as an infant, in the middle of the swept stone, and the Ground did what the Ground does, which is receive whatever turns up and work out what it needs.

I mention it because Yesh and I are both in the same column of Tollen’s book and we do not have a single thing in common to look at.

---

Yesh has drawings.

Yesh has had drawings for two years, and a site picked out on the flat above the north wall, and a costing done three times, and a method for the cooling that involves a water jacket and a great deal of shade and which I did not understand the first time it was explained to me and did understand the third time.

And as of that season, for the first time, Yesh has the purse and the gang.

One trial room. Not the whole scheme. One room, built properly, to find out whether the thing works before anybody bets a settlement on it. Two years of arguing for it, and Ravel finally said yes in the spring, and the gang was booked for the summer.

I have never seen anybody as pleased about a hole in the ground as Yesh was about that site, and it was not yet a hole. It was pegs.

Yesh took me up there on my sixth day and walked me round the pegs and told me where the jacket would go, and then said the only thing Yesh said to me for the first three weeks that was not about the room.

“You’ve seen thirteen of these.”

I said thirteen of what.

“Worlds. Places. Buildings.” Yesh looked at the pegs. “I’ve seen one.”

And asked me to look at the drawings.


## Chapter 4: What It Looks Like From Outside

Two days before the shift meeting, Ravel walked the west side and let me come.

I want to be clear that this was not a consultation. Ravel walks the west side about once a month and had been going anyway, and I had been through four hundred and six rooms in three weeks and was therefore, at that particular moment, the person on that Ground who had most recently looked at everything.

We went along the top of the west face, which is a long low shoulder of rock with grass on it and nothing else, and Ravel pointed out where the new rooms would go if they were ever cut. There is a mark on a stone up there from a survey somebody did nineteen years ago and abandoned.

Then we went down into the deep rooms and stood in the two that are damp but not yet standing in anything, and Ravel showed me the line on the wall where the water was ten years ago, which is about a hand below where it is now.

And then, back up in the light, Ravel took out Yesh’s drawings and put them on a barrel and let me look at them, without saying anything about them at all. Not what Ravel thought. Not what anybody thought. Just: here they are, have a look.

I had seen them once already, up at the pegs, with Yesh talking over the top of them the whole time. This was different. This was a person showing you a thing they have not made their mind up about.

“You’ve been to thirteen of these,” said Ravel eventually.

I said I had.

“Anything like this? Anywhere?”

---

Here is what I said, and I have written it out in full, because every word of it was true.

On Tarrow I had learned what happens when I mistake an outside measurement for a neutral decision. I remembered that. I was careful not to tell Ravel which of the three answers the Ground should choose. I thought I was doing something smaller.

I said I had seen this before. Not the water and not the rock, but the shape of it. On a world of terraced fields I watched a family patch a wall for four years running because the proper repair was a season’s work they could not spare, and in the fifth year the wall came down in the spring and took the terrace with it, and they did the proper repair anyway, having by then paid for it twice.

I said that I had been on a world where a road was allowed to weaken because keeping it was expensive and losing it was slow, and that by the time the argument was settled the argument had settled itself.

I said the deep rooms have been getting worse for ten years and that the Ground has been discussing it for three of them, and that in three years nobody has produced a single figure that anybody trusts. That every argument I had heard since I arrived was about what things would probably cost, and that *probably* had been doing an enormous amount of work in a place that writes everything down.

And I said that before they spend anything on anything, somebody should find out properly what the permanent answer costs. Because they have been saying for three years that the cutting is unaffordable, and as far as I could tell nobody actually knew that. They believed it. They had reasons to believe it. **But nobody in that place had ever put a real number against it**, and it seemed to me a strange way to run a decision, in a town with three hundred years of books.

I thought I was asking for information before a decision. I did not understand that, on Anneth, getting that information was itself one of the decisions competing for the summer.

Ravel listened to all of it without interrupting.

Then Ravel said, “That’s fair.”

And then Ravel rolled up the drawings and said nothing else, and we went back down to the yard, and I thought no more about it.

---

The shift meeting happens at the turn of the season in the covered area with the tables, and everybody who is not actually holding something comes to it.

I had been to two by then. They are not solemn. There is a great deal of arguing and a certain amount of food and at least one procedural dispute that everybody enjoys more than they admit. My second one had a forty-minute row about whether the north tank counted as one tank or two for the purposes of the rota, which was settled by Tollen and the book, to nobody’s satisfaction.

This one was about the deep rooms, because they all are.

Tollen went first, as Tollen always does, and read out the eleven. Everybody has heard it. Nobody stops him, because the eleven are real, and because underneath the performance is a man who has read the whole book and would like the room to remember that the deep rooms are not a convenience.

After that it went the way it has gone for three years. The lining, at length, by about nine people. Yesh, nineteen and the youngest speaking by twenty years, saying the trial room was booked for the summer and would settle the above-ground question for good at a fraction of the cost of anything else. Everybody correct. Nobody moving.

And then Ravel raised the survey.

---

I want to be exact about this, because it matters.

**Ravel raised it. Not me.**

Ravel said that the Ground had been arguing about the cutting for three years on the strength of a number nobody had, that this was a poor way to run anything, and that before the Ground spent a season’s purse on any of the three, somebody should establish what the west-side cutting would actually cost.

Somebody asked whether that was a new position for Ravel, since Ravel had wanted the lining since before some of them were born.

Ravel said it was not a position at all. Ravel said that if the number came back affordable, Ravel would rather know that before spending the purse on a repair that buys fifteen years. And if it came back unaffordable, then that was three years of arguing finished for good and everybody could stop.

**And then Ravel said that the floating thing had made the same point on the west side two days ago and had made it better.**

That is the whole of my contribution to that meeting. One sentence, reported by somebody else, as one item among several.

I did not speak. Nobody asked me to. I was up on a rail at the back where I would not be in anybody’s way, and about six people turned round and looked at me for a moment, and then turned back, because the meeting was not about me.

---

They argued about it for another half hour and I could not tell you which way it would go.

Tollen was for it immediately and said so, because Tollen has wanted the cutting for three years and a survey is the first thing in three years that has moved toward it.

Yesh was against, and made an argument I thought was good: that the question the Ground actually needs answered is whether an above-ground room works, that the trial would answer it this summer, and that nobody needs a price for the cutting to know that the cutting is enormous.

A woman from the night shift said she would rather have the number than not have it.

Somebody said the rooms had held for ten years and would hold another.

Somebody else said that was luck, and Ravel said quietly that luck was not a plan, which is the only time I heard Ravel say the same thing twice.

And a man who had not spoken at all, one of the gang I think, though I did not know that then, said the thing that decided it. He said: if we line them this year and in five years somebody finds out the cutting was affordable all along, what do we say then.

Nobody had an answer to that.

**So they voted, and the survey carried.**

Not overwhelmingly. Not the way a room votes when somebody has swept them. It carried the way a room votes when it has argued itself tired and one option has stopped looking worse than the others.

---

Nobody said anything about the gang.

I want that on the record, and I want it recorded plainly, because it is the whole of what happened and there is no villain in it.

Nobody stood up and said *that means the crew goes west all summer.* Nobody said *that is Yesh’s summer.* Nobody argued the point on those grounds, and Yesh did not raise it either.

They did not say it because it did not need saying. Every Annethi in that covered area had known since they were four years old what a survey is: eleven people with picks on a rock face for a season, and a purse to feed them. It is as obvious there as knowing that a boat needs water.

And I heard *find out what it costs* and thought of a man with a slate.

---

Afterward Yesh went off to eat with two other people and did not look particularly cast down, and Tollen was pleased in a restrained way, and Ravel put the vote in the record.

I stayed on the rail for a while after they had gone.

I have been to thirteen worlds and on almost all of them I have been the thing in the corner that cannot lift anything. I have watched people work out problems I could see the answer to and been unable to hand them so much as a rope. On the last world I could not put one inch of surface on a road that forty people were killing themselves to keep.

And on this one I had looked at a west face with a person who was not asking me for anything, and said what I had seen elsewhere, and two days later a room full of people had decided something with that in it.

**Not because of me. I know the difference.** Ravel made the argument, the room made the decision, and a man from the gang said the sentence that finished it.

But something I had seen somewhere else had turned out to be worth saying here.

I went off round the deep rooms that night in an extremely good mood.


## Chapter 5: A Season

The board went up outside the tally house two mornings after the meeting, in Ravel’s hand, and it was four lines long.

**Capital, this season: west-side survey.**

**Gang: west, from the eighth day.**

**Draft animals: west.**

**All other works: next season.**

I read it twice on my way past and understood the first three lines and not the fourth, and went to find somebody to explain the fourth, which is how I found out.

---

Brit explained it, without any idea that she was telling me anything, while carrying one end of a trestle.

“Trial room’s off.”

I said off how.

“Off till next year. Gang’s west.” Brit put the trestle down. “You can’t dig a hole up there and a hole down there with the same eleven people, can you.”

I said that a survey was measuring.

Brit looked at me the way you look at somebody who has said something about your own trade that is not so much wrong as strange.

“It’s a season of test cuts,” she said. “Bore holes, four of them, down to whatever’s under that shoulder. Cut a bench in so you can get a cart to it. Sample the rock, weigh what comes out, work out what the water’s doing under there.” She picked the trestle back up. “That’s the gang and the animals and the purse from the eighth day till the frosts. What did you think it was?”

I said I had thought it was somebody with figures.

“Figures of what?” said Brit, and went off with the trestle.

---

I want to put down exactly what I understood at that point, because it was not everything and it took days to become everything.

I understood that I had said a thing on a west face two days before a meeting, that Ravel had carried it into the meeting, and that the meeting had voted.

And I understood that when I said *somebody should find out properly what it costs*, I had been imagining a person at a table with a slate and a week’s work, and that this is not what those words mean on Anneth, and that nobody had corrected me because nobody had heard anything that needed correcting.

That is what I understood standing outside the tally house.

---

I went and found Yesh at the site on the flat above the north wall, because it seemed better than not going.

The pegs were still in. Somebody had taken up the string.

“You’ve seen the board,” said Yesh.

I said I had. I said I had not understood what a survey was.

Yesh thought about that for a moment. Yesh genuinely thought about it, standing there with the string wound round one weak hand, and then said it.

“No,” Yesh said. “Why would you.”

Not bitterly. Not generously either, which would have been worse. **As a fact about somebody who has been on the Ground four weeks and has never had a gang or a season to think about.**

I said I was sorry about the room.

“It’s a summer,” said Yesh. “I’ve already given it two years. I can give it another.” And then, because Yesh is nineteen and honest: “I’d rather not.”

Then Yesh asked whether I was doing the round that night, and I said I was, and Yesh said to look at the seep in the fourth deep room because it had looked different on Tuesday.

And that was the whole of it. **Yesh did not accuse me of anything and did not tell me it was not my fault**, and both of those would have been easier, and I have noticed that people who are actually good at their work tend not to do either.

---

Then the season happened, which is the part I did not expect.

The gang went west on the eighth day. They took the animals and the tools and a cook and were gone from the yard by the time I came off the round, and the yard was quieter for about four days and then was not, because the Ground does not stop.

**Things kept arriving.** That is the thing about a place like that: whatever is going on with the purse, whatever is being argued about, whatever anybody has decided. The swept stone in the middle of the yard does not know and does not care. Something turns up. Somebody fetches it. Somebody works out what it needs.

In the first month after the gang went west there arrived: a quantity of dressed timber, which was welcome; a tank of liquid that had to be got into the cold room in four hours and was got there in three; a thing that was probably a tool and that nobody has yet worked out the use of; and about a hundred small hard objects like beads, which are still being counted.

In the second month, an animal.

I should tell that one properly.

---

It came at night, on the swept stone, and it was the size of a cart and had too many legs, and it was terrified.

The night shift woke Ravel. Ravel came down in about ninety seconds and stood at the edge of the stone and watched it for four minutes without going any closer. Watching, I had learned, is most of a setter’s job.

Then Ravel said: north pen, take the light away, nobody in there with it, and get Tollen.

Tollen came with two volumes under one arm and found the entry in about six minutes: something similar, in the ninth year of Corran, a hundred and eleven years ago, and what had been done with it, and what it had eaten, and how long it had lived.

It had lived four days.

“Then we can do better than that,” said Ravel, and they did. It ate what the book said it might eat, which took two attempts, and by the second week it would let a fetcher into the pen with it.

**Nobody mentioned the deep rooms once.** They did not need them for that one. It was a night animal and the north pen was dark enough and the whole thing was managed above ground by six people who knew their jobs.

But I was on the round every night that month, and every night I went down into the deep rooms, and four of them were standing in water, and two were damp, and the five that were good were empty and dark and waiting for something that had not come yet.

---

Yesh worked all season.

Not on the room. On the ordinary work as setter’s assistant, which means being everywhere at once, and on the drawings, which got better. Yesh redid the water jacket twice that season on the strength of nothing but time to think about it, and the second version is simpler and cheaper than the one that had been funded in the spring.

I said as much once, and Yesh said, “Yes, well,” which is the whole of what Yesh had to say about that.

And in the ninth week Yesh came off shift and found me and asked whether I would come up and look at the seep in the fourth room again, and we went down together, and Yesh stood in the water in the dark with a lamp that Yesh did not need and I could not hold, and said:

“It’s a hand higher than the mark from ten years ago.”

I said I knew. I had been writing it down every night since I took the round.

“I know you have,” said Yesh. “I read them.”

---

The gang came back from the west face at the frosts, which was later than anybody expected, and there was a great deal of talk in the yard about what they had found, all of it secondhand and none of it reliable.

What was certain was that they had cut a bench into the shoulder and put four holes down and brought back a considerable quantity of rock in bags, and that the survey had taken every day of the season and every animal and the whole purse.

And that Ravel had the figures, and would not say anything about them to anybody, and had gone very quiet.


## Chapter 6: Unaffordable

Ravel gave the figures out on the fourth day after the gang came back, in the covered area, to about forty people, standing up, reading off a slate.

**Four years of the Ground’s whole margin. Possibly five.**

The rock under the west shoulder is harder than the rock the old rooms were cut from, which sounds like good news and is not: harder rock is slower rock, and slower rock is more days, and days are the thing the Ground has least of. Two of the four bore holes found water at a depth nobody expected. The bench they cut to get the carts up will need doing again properly, because it was cut for a survey and not for four years of hauling.

Ravel read all of it out and then read out the total again, because somebody asked, and then put the slate down.

“So that’s that,” said Ravel. “It’s not four years. It might be five. We’re not doing it.”

And that was the meeting.

---

Tollen took it hardest, and took it well, which are not the same thing.

Tollen has wanted the cutting for three years, has the whole book behind him, and had been more nearly right about the deep rooms than anybody. And what the survey said was: yes, and you cannot have it.

He did not argue. He asked two questions about the water depth, listened to the answers, and said that was clear enough.

Then he went and got the volume with the eleven in it and read them out again, which nobody had asked for. Not to make a case. The case was over. He read them out the way you would read out a list of people who are not coming, and then he closed the book and said the entries would want a note against them saying the survey had been done and what it found, so that in a hundred years nobody would have to argue about it again.

**Then he went back to work**, which is the thing about that place.

---

I want to set down what I understood, and it took me the four days between the gang coming back and Ravel reading the slate, and I got there in the wrong order.

The first thing I thought was: I was right.

That is not false modesty in reverse. It is what I thought, standing in the covered area listening to the figures, and it is true. **The cutting is the best permanent answer to that problem.** It was the best answer before the survey and the survey did not change it. Four years of margin buys three hundred years of rooms in better rock above the water; a lining buys fifteen years and then the same conversation. Every word I said to Ravel on the west face was true, and the numbers that came back proved the expensive half of it and disproved nothing.

**And Yesh has lost a summer, and the rooms are exactly as wet as they were a year ago, and a thing I said is part of the reason both are true.** I cannot take it back out of the chain. I was not the whole chain, and I was in it.

Both of those. At the same time. That is what took four days.

---

Here is where I got to, and I would like to put it plainly because it is not complicated once you see it and I could not see it for a month.

**I answered a different question from the one they were asking.**

The question I answered was: *what is the best permanent solution to the deep rooms?* I am good at that question. I have been to thirteen worlds and watched people patch things that needed replacing, and I have seen what it costs to be wrong about it, and when Ravel asked me what I had seen elsewhere I gave a true answer to that question and it was worth hearing.

The question the Ground was actually asking was: **what can we responsibly do this season, with one purse, one gang, five usable rooms and water that is a hand higher than it was ten years ago?**

**Thirteen worlds had taught me enough to ask the first question and not enough to answer the second.** I did not notice that they were different questions, because from outside they look like the same question wearing different clothes.

They are not.

The first question asks what solution lasts. The second asks what these people can responsibly do now. Its answer is only true here, this year, for these forty-odd people, with this much money and these eleven pairs of hands. Every single person in that covered area had spent their whole life inside the second question and knew things about it that I do not know and cannot learn by looking.

I had a view from outside. **A view from outside is worth something and it is not worth what I thought it was worth.**

Nobody had to hide anything from me for me to get this wrong. They just had to be right about something so ordinary that saying it out loud would have been strange.

---

Nobody blamed me. I want to record that, because it would be easier if somebody had.

Ravel asked me, three days later, whether I had seen the seep figures for the fourth room, because I had been writing them down all season and Ravel wanted them. That was the whole of Ravel’s response to any of it, and Ravel had put the proposal to the meeting and knew perfectly well where it came from.

Brit said, when I told her, that everybody has a first season and mine was more interesting than most.

And Tollen wrote it in the book. Not as blame. As a record. *Survey undertaken, west shoulder, on the recommendation of the shift meeting.* My name is not in it, because I do not have one, and because it was not my recommendation. It was the meeting’s. That is true and it is also a fact that lets me off, and I noticed myself being let off, and did not enjoy it.

---

Yesh found me the evening after the figures, on the flat above the north wall, where the pegs are.

We looked at the pegs for a while.

I said the room would have been standing by now.

“It would,” said Yesh.

I said I had been thinking about what I said to Ravel on the west face, and that I had got something wrong, and I started to explain it, the two questions and the thing about outside, and Yesh let me get about half of it out and then said:

“I know.”

Not unkindly. **Yesh had known since the eighth day** and had not said, because Yesh is not a person who explains other people to themselves.

Then Yesh said it.

“The bit I mind isn’t the year,” Yesh said. “It’s that we’ve had a season with five rooms and the water coming up, and nothing came that needed six.”

I said that was lucky.

“It was,” said Yesh. “And if something had come, it wouldn’t have cared whose idea the survey was.”

Then Yesh said it was cold and went down.


## Chapter 7: Line and Pump

They lined the deep rooms over the winter and into the spring, and it took eleven people nine weeks, and it is the least interesting thing I have ever watched anybody do.

It is repair, not works. It came out of the year’s remainder and the winter market, which is what that money is for, and it was done in the cold months when the gang was not on anything else. **It did not touch the summer.** Ravel was extremely clear about that with everybody who asked, and several people asked.

You cannot line a room that is standing in water, so first they pumped. The pump is loud and slow and it broke twice. Then the clay, mixed on the yard and carried down in tubs and put on by hand, using one Annethi’s worth of grip on a wooden float, from the floor up, in bands.

Nine weeks. Six rooms: the four that were standing and the two that were damp.

The other five were left alone, because they are dry and there is no purse for improving things that are not broken.

---

Ravel got what Ravel had wanted for three years and did not enjoy it at all.

I asked, at about the fourth week, whether this was a good day.

“It’s the right thing to do this year,” said Ravel, which is not an answer to that question and was the answer I got.

Later, while mixing clay and not looking up, Ravel said the rest of it.

“Fifteen years. That’s if the pump holds and the water doesn’t do something new. Fifteen years is one setter’s working life and a bit. Somebody stands where I’m standing in fifteen years with the same rooms and worse water and a purse that’s the same size, and they’ll have to think of something I couldn’t.” Ravel put more clay on. “That’s not a victory. That’s a debt with a long date on it.”

I said the eleven in the book would have wanted the fifteen years.

“They’d have wanted the fifteen years,” Ravel agreed. “That’s why we’re doing it.”

---

Yesh gets the trial room back.

I want to be exact about how, because when Ravel told me I looked for a reason that involved me and there is not one.

**The trial room was approved in the spring before I arrived.** It had a purse and a gang and a site and a date. It lost them to the survey, which took a season, and now the season is over.

**And the summer is free.**

That is the whole of it. The lining was winter repair and did not cost the gang a summer. The emergency, such as it was, has been bought fifteen years of quiet. **So there is a summer’s works and a purse and nothing urgent to put them on**, and a design that has been waiting two years and a bit, and everybody involved has known for two years that the question it answers is worth answering.

So Yesh builds.

Ravel said so at the shift meeting in four sentences, and there was no argument, because there was nothing to argue about.

---

The survey did produce numbers. I want to be honest about them because I went looking, and what I was looking for was a way that the season had been worth something.

They know the rock under the west shoulder now. They know where the water sits under there. Somebody drew a proper section of the shoulder, which nobody had ever had, and it is on the wall in the tally house and Tollen has copied it into the book.

**None of it was needed for anything.** The cutting is not happening. The section on the wall is a good section of a shoulder that nobody is going to dig, and it will still be a good section in a hundred years if anybody ever wants it, and that is the most that can be said.

**Yesh’s room is not being built because of anything the survey found.** It is being built because it was going to be built last year.

Yesh gets this year what Yesh should have had last year, and nothing came of the difference.

---

I said something about that to Yesh, up at the site, on the day they put the string back on the pegs.

I said I had wanted the survey to have been worth something.

Yesh thought about it, in that way Yesh has of taking a thing seriously, and said:

“It’s a good section.”

I said that was not what I meant.

“I know,” said Yesh. “But it’s a good section, and it cost a summer, and both of those are true and neither one pays for the other.” Yesh went back to the string. “The room would have been standing all last winter. I’d have known by now whether the jacket works. If it doesn’t work I’ve lost a summer finding that out too, and I’d rather have lost last summer than this one.”

Then Yesh said, without any particular weight: “It’s all right. It’s not all right in a way where you can do something. It’s all right in the way where it happened.”

---

I did none of the work that made those rooms dry.

I want to put that down flatly, because I did do things that winter and none of them were that. I cannot pump. I cannot mix clay or carry a tub or hold a float. When they wanted somebody to hold a lamp in the fourth room, they could not use me, because I cannot hold a lamp.

What I did was the round. Every night, four hundred and six rooms, the same as every night since the fourth day I was there. I wrote down the seep figures in the six rooms they were lining and gave them to Ravel each morning, and Ravel used them, and that is the nearest thing to a contribution I made.

Brit says the round is not nothing. Brit would say that, because Brit does not want the round back.

And the deep rooms hold.

Six lined, five dry, eleven usable for the first time in ten years. On the last night of the ninth week I went down and did the round and there was no water in any of them, anywhere, and the fourth room, which had been standing in a hand of it since before I arrived, was dry to the floor and smelled of clay.

I stayed down there longer than the round needed.

Fifteen years, Ravel says. Then somebody stands where Ravel is standing with the same rooms and worse water.

But that night they were dry, and something could have arrived that needed one, and there would have been a room ready for it.


## Chapter 8: A Place for Unanswered

Yesh came and found me at the site four days before the gang started, when it was still pegs and string and a great deal of measuring.

I had been up there most nights on the round, because the site is inside the wall and therefore inside the rota, which Yesh finds funny and I do not.

We went over the jacket again. Yesh has been over the jacket with me perhaps nine times and I understand about two thirds of it and Yesh has stopped pretending otherwise, which I prefer.

Then Yesh sat down on the stone that is going to be the doorstep and said:

“Ravel’s closing your record.”

I said I did not know what that meant.

“Settling period. It’s a year, near enough, for anything that can talk. Then they close the intake and put a disposition on it.” Yesh was looking at the pegs, not at me. “You’ll get the offer.”

I asked what the offer was.

“You’ll see,” said Yesh. “It’s not a surprise. It’s the same one everybody gets.”

---

And then Yesh told me a thing I had not asked about, which was that Yesh is going to leave.

Not soon. After the room. After the room is built and run through a summer and a winter and either works or does not, because you cannot leave a thing like that half-answered and Yesh would not want to.

But after.

“Where?” I said.

“Don’t know. Down the plain road. There’s four towns I’ve never seen and I’ve been in this yard nineteen years.”

I said that a lot of people at the Ground had been in that yard for nineteen years and more.

“They have,” said Yesh. “Good luck to them. I’d like to have gone somewhere.”

I asked carefully, because it is not a thing you ask, whether that was about being unanswered.

Yesh actually laughed at that.

“No,” said Yesh. “It’s about wanting to see four towns.”

---

Here is the part of that conversation I have set down most carefully, because we did not agree and neither of us was wrong.

I said that from where I was standing, Yesh had something I had never had once in fourteen worlds. That the Ground built a stool for Yesh at the counting bench without anybody making a speech about it. That there is a latch on the drawing-room door somebody altered twelve years ago and nobody has ever mentioned it since. That Yesh had been argued with for two years about the trial room by people who took the argument seriously, which is not a thing that happens to somebody a place is merely tolerating.

I said that Yesh knows exactly where Yesh grew up, and who made the stool, and who altered the latch, and what work is waiting tomorrow, and precisely which four people will argue about the trial room and in what order.

Yesh said all of that was true.

“It’s a good place,” said Yesh. “It’s the best place I know of, and I’ve thought about it a lot, and it’s not close.” Yesh moved a peg about two inches for no reason I could see. “And I still want to go and look at the four towns.”

I said I understood that perfectly well. I said that if somebody put those two things in front of me, a place like this one and four towns down a road, **I do not think I would go for the towns.**

“No,” said Yesh. “I would.”

And that was the whole of the disagreement. Not about whether both things can be true. **About which one you pick when they are.**

---

We got no further than that.

I did not tell Yesh to go, and I did not tell Yesh to be satisfied, and Yesh did not tell me anything about what I should do either. **Neither of us moved the other one an inch.** We sat on the doorstep and disagreed and then Yesh went to eat and I went and did the round, which is what the Fetching Ground is like.

---

Ravel closed the record eleven days later, in the tally house, with Tollen there because Tollen is always there when the book is out.

It took about six minutes.

Ravel read out what was in the intake: the day I arrived, the condition, the measurements, the disposition, *west three, temporary*, and the fact that it had been temporary for a year, which Ravel said in the tone of a person who does not like a column left open.

Tollen supplied the kind. *Unanswered.* And then, because Tollen cannot help it, supplied the history as well: nine of them in three hundred years and change. Tollen has the entries. The oldest is from the second book and the newest before me was a hundred and forty years ago, and no two of them were remotely alike. One was the size of a cart, one could not be seen properly in daylight, one lived in the east room for eleven years and did the tallies, and one, in Tollen’s phrase, *never did settle to anything.*

I asked what they had in common.

“Nothing,” said Tollen. “That’s the column. They could talk and they couldn’t say where they were from.”

Nine arrivals in three hundred years did not explain the old Kesh word, and they did not explain *for the unanswered*. They told me something about Anneth and its recordkeeping. That was enough.

---

And I thought about the second world I ever went to.

There was something at the near side of the crossing, in the dark, and it moved the way a thing moves when it has been waiting, and it did not come closer, and it knew me.

I have never known what it was.

I did not say anything about it in the tally house. Nobody there could have told me anything. It is not on Anneth and it never was, and nine entries in a book do not add up to an answer about a thing on another world in the dark.

I only thought about it, and then Ravel got to the disposition.

---

“Right,” said Ravel. “Permanent.”

And then said the thing that the standing procedure says. It is written down, in the book, in four different hands going back two hundred years, and they read it out.

**That the Ground receives what arrives. That anything that can direct itself and wants a place has one, because that is what the Ground is for and has always been for. That there is work if it wants work and none if it does not. That the east room is empty.**

Then Ravel added, off the page, in the tone of somebody getting a job finished before the light goes:

“There’s the east room or west three, and west three’s yours already so you may as well keep it. Tollen’ll want to know which for the entry. There’s no hurry. Nobody’s short of a room.”

I said I would think about it.

“Course,” said Ravel, and closed the book, and said the pump wanted looking at before the frosts because it had been making a noise on the Tuesday, and went off to look at the pump.

---

I want to put down what the offer was, because it went past very fast and it is the only one I have ever had.

It was not a proposal. Nobody was asking me for anything. Nobody said they wanted me, or that I would be missed, and **none of that was a condition of anything.** Ravel did not have to say it and did not say it.

**What Ravel said was that there was a room.**

And the reason that meant anything at all is that I had been through the east room forty times. It is on the round. It is two doors down from a room built for something with a shell, and along from a room that has to be kept at the temperature of a cold morning, and near the one with the ceiling bracket that is no use to anything that cannot hang.

**None of those rooms were built for me.** Not one of them. They were built over three hundred years by people who did not know what was coming and built anyway, and then kept them swept and dry and inspected on a rota by somebody who complains about it constantly, in case.

**There was a room, and there had been a room for a hundred and forty years before I arrived, and it was not for me. It was for whatever came.**

That is what was being offered. Not a place made for me.

**A place that makes places.**


## Chapter 9: Going

I took eleven days, and I did the round every one of them.

Nobody asked, which I had not expected. Ravel had a pump to see to and then a delivery of clay and then the ordinary business of a receiving yard in early summer, and did not raise it once. Tollen wanted the entry finished and said so twice, but Tollen wants every entry finished and has said so about everything for fifty years. Brit asked whether I was keeping the round, which is a different question and was asked entirely out of self-interest.

Nobody was waiting on me. **The offer did not require an answer by any particular day**, because a place that has kept a room aired for a hundred and forty years is not in a hurry about a room.

---

On the fourth day I went and floated in the east room for a while, and did not mention it to anybody.

It is a good room. Better than west three, if I am honest about it: deeper in, drier, with a stone shelf along one side and a bracket in the ceiling and a channel in the floor, and a door that shuts properly.

It was built in the year of somebody-or-other for something that arrived and needed a dark place with air moving in it. That thing lived in there for eleven years and did the tallies and then died, and the room has been swept and aired and inspected every fortnight ever since by whoever had the round.

Nobody put it there for me. Nobody who cut that room has been alive for a century.

**And it would have done. That is the thing I want to be exact about. It would have done perfectly well.**

---

Here is what I decided, and it took eleven days mostly because I kept waiting to find a reason.

**Anneth is home.**

I do not mean that the old voice in the cut was telling me to come here. I have no evidence of that. Its *home* is still its word, attached to a question I cannot answer. This one is mine.

I am going to write it down plainly because I have spent a long time not knowing what the word would look like if I ever met it, and it turns out it looks like a room somebody else built for somebody else.

Not where I am from. I do not know where I am from. Nobody on Anneth knows where they are from either, in the sense of the arrivals, and it has never once occurred to any of them that this is a thing to be troubled by.

Not where I was meant to end up. Nothing there was waiting for me. Nine unanswered in three hundred years, no two alike, and a column in a book.

I had tried other answers without knowing they were tests. I had stayed after my usefulness was finished. I had let a household count me. I had gone back exactly to a place I had left. None of those told me where I belonged.

**A place built to receive whatever comes, by people who go on building it without knowing what is coming.** That is what Anneth is. It had a room in it for me, and work if I wanted work, and none if I did not. It did not need an origin from me, or a promise to remain.

That is home. I am satisfied that it is.

---

And I did not want to stop.

There is not a better sentence than that one.

Not because there is something wrong with the Ground. There is nothing wrong with the Ground. Not because anybody lied to me, or because the offer was less than it looked, or because I found something out at the last minute that changed it. Nobody lied and nothing changed and the offer was exactly what it appeared to be, which is the only reason any of this is worth writing down.

Not because anybody needs me somewhere else. Nobody does, at that moment. I have been needed for particular things before. I have even been counted on. **Being needed has never told me where to stay.**

**I could have stayed. I would have been received. I did not want to stop.**

That is the whole of it and I am aware of how small it is.

---

I told Ravel on the twelfth morning, in the tally house, while Ravel was doing the clay account.

I said I would be going.

“Right,” said Ravel, and reached over and turned the book round and found the column and wrote something in it, which took about four seconds.

I asked what Ravel had put.

“*Departed.*” Ravel turned the book back. “There’s a word for it. There’s a word for all of them.”

And that was Ravel’s entire response, and I am glad of it. Nobody argued with me. Nobody asked why. Nobody said they were sorry to hear it, and I do not think anybody was, particularly, and that is not a complaint. **It is the same thing that made the offer real.** The Ground receives what arrives and provides for what stops and writes down what goes. It does not require anybody to want anything.

Tollen came in later to check the wording and was annoyed about the disposition column, because *departed* is correct and unhelpful and does not say where, and I could not help with that.

“Unknown, then,” said Tollen, and wrote it, and was not troubled at all.

---

I said goodbye to Brit, who wanted the round back and was extremely poor at hiding it, and who told me the correct order to do it in, which is not the order I had been doing it in and never was.

I said goodbye to Ravel at the pump.

And I went up to the site on the flat above the north wall, where they had got the footings in and a course and a half of the north side up, and Yesh was there with a slate and clay on both weak hands, and told me at some length about the jacket, which will either work or not.

I said I was going.

“I know,” said Yesh. “Tollen’s told everybody.”

I said I hoped the room worked.

“So do I,” said Yesh, and then, having thought about it: “Four towns. After.”

I said I would probably not be able to find out how any of it went.

“No,” said Yesh. “That’s how it is with people who go.”

---

The crossing is out past the north wall, where the plain starts and there is nothing at all in any direction.

I did not look at it on the way. There is no point. I have been through fourteen of them and I have never learned one thing from looking.

But this crossing was different in one way that had nothing to do with the crossing. **I had somewhere I could stay, and I was leaving it anyway.** Going on was mine now in a way it had not been before.

Behind me the Ground was doing what the Ground does, which was a great deal of noise about a delivery of clay.

And then, as I went, something arrived on the swept stone in the middle of the yard. I did not see what, only that the shouting changed, and somebody went at a jog to fetch it.
