## Chapter 1: The Distance

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I know a few other things. I can move. I can look at a thing until I understand it. Sometimes I arrive somewhere new, and I don’t know how that happens either.

That morning I arrived above a yellow stone path on the side of an enormous white cliff.

The path ran along a wide flat shelf of rock. There were houses cut into the wall above it and gardens hanging below it on ropes. Far down the cliff I could see another shelf with its own houses, and below that another, and below that the light gave out. Above me the shelves went up until they became a pale line.

The sky was silver. It had bands in it, like something slow was moving behind it.

I liked it immediately.

I floated forward along the path.

“Don’t cross that.”

The voice came from behind me.

I stopped.

A creature was standing in the road with a basket in her mouth. She had four short legs and a long tail and a neck that bent like rope. Her fur was dark brown except for a pale stripe running down her neck. Behind her head were two small fins.

The fins were flat against her skull.

She put the basket down.

“Don’t cross that,” she said again.

I looked in front of me. The path continued. It went past a low wall, around a tree with blue leaves, and down toward a place where I could hear bells and voices.

There was nothing in the way.

No gate. No wall. No hole. The ground ahead of me looked exactly like the ground behind me.

I looked at her.

Then I looked at the path.

“Why?”

“Because that’s Venn’s.”

I looked again.

“Which part?”

“That part.”

She pointed with her nose.

I looked where she was pointing. It was still ground.

“How do you know?”

She opened her mouth. Then she closed it.

It was the first question I asked on that world, and it was already the wrong kind.

“I can feel it,” she said.

“Feel what?”

“The edge.”

“What does it feel like?”

She thought about that for longer than I expected.

“Like the floor changing under you,” she said. “But not the floor.”

Her fins came up slightly. They tipped forward, toward the path, and I understood that she was doing something with them. Something that was not looking and was not listening.

“You are measuring,” I said.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You are doing something.”

“Everyone does it.” She sounded annoyed. “It’s not a thing you do. It’s a thing that happens.”

“Then everyone is doing something.”

She stared at me.

“There’s a marker,” she said, which was not an answer, and moved past me down the road.

I followed.

Beside the path stood a black stone post, upright, coming up to about Mara’s knee. It was clearly taller than it was wide, and not shaped so much as chosen: irregular, weathered, dark all over, with the grass cut back around its foot. A dull silver-gray band ran right around it at the middle, level, all the way about.

“That?”

“Yes.”

“That stone owns the path?”

She turned her head all the way around on that rope neck and looked at me.

“No.”

“Then what does?”

“The marker tells you who owns it.”

“Who?”

“Venn.”

“Where is Venn?”

I looked along the road as though he might be walking up it.

“Three shelves down.”

“He owns something he isn’t standing on?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because he bought it.”

“From whom?”

She opened her mouth again. Closed it again. She looked at me for a moment, deciding, I think, whether I was making fun of her.

“You ask too many questions.”

“I only asked four.”

“That’s too many.”

That was how I met Mara.

## Chapter 2: A Thing That Happens

She had dropped the basket when she first saw me. Not because I frightened her, she said later. She said she dropped it because I was in the middle of the road and there was nothing under me.

“You were floating,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Over the road.”

“Yes.”

“Without walking.”

“I don’t walk.”

She looked at my smooth dark shape.

“I noticed.”

I had no eyes that I could see. No mouth. No legs. I was a small round thing about the size of the fruit in her basket, and I moved without making a sound. I did not know what else to tell her.

“What are you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where did you come from?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where are you going?”

“I don’t know.”

She sat down in the road.

“Do you know anything?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“I can move.”

“I suppose that’s something.”

I floated in front of her. Her tail curled around her feet and the tip of it moved slowly from side to side. Later I learned that this meant she was paying attention. At the time I only noticed that it moved.

“How far is that tree?” she asked.

I looked at the tree.

“Twenty-three.”

“Twenty-three what?”

I considered the question.

“Twenty-three.”

For some reason, Mara laughed.

It was the first time I heard a Vey laugh. It was a short bright sound, and it made the fins behind her head lift.

I liked it.

“That’s not how you say it,” she said.

“How do you say it?”

She looked at the tree. She did not squint. She did not count anything. She did not move.

“Eleven steps,” she said. “And it’s thin.”

“What does thin mean?”

“It means the distance is small enough that nobody bothers to own it.”

I looked at the tree, and at the ground between us and the tree, and I could not find anything about it that was thin.

To me it was space.

That was all it was.

## Chapter 3: Not Allowed

“I’m going to the market,” Mara said. “You should go the other way.”

“Why?”

“Because that’s Venn’s road and you can’t pay him.”

“What happens if I don’t pay him?”

“You don’t cross.”

“But what happens?”

She picked up the basket. Then she put it down again, because I was clearly not going to stop.

“He reports you,” she said. “You get a fine. If you can’t pay the fine they take a distance from you. If you don’t have a distance to take —”

She stopped.

“Then what?”

“Then you’re the kind of person who has nothing to take,” she said. “And everyone knows it.”

She said it the way you say a thing you have heard adults say.

I thought about the fine. I thought about the taking. Nothing in either of those things was about the road.

“Show me where it starts,” I said.

She sighed. She walked me back three steps and stopped beside the black stone.

“Here.”

“Exactly here?”

“Exactly here.”

“How exactly?”

She put one front foot down, very precisely, on a piece of ground that looked like all the other pieces of ground.

“Here is mine to walk on,” she said. She moved the foot forward the width of the foot. “Here is Venn’s.”

“I want to try something,” I said.

“No.”

I crossed.

I moved forward exactly the width of her foot, and then a little more, and then I stopped and floated over the middle of Venn’s distance and waited to find out what a fine felt like.

Nothing happened.

The air did not change. The road did not change. The tree with blue leaves went on being twenty-three, or eleven steps, depending on who was counting.

I turned around.

Mara had not moved. Her tail had gone flat against the ground. Her fins were pinned back so hard they had almost disappeared into her fur.

“Come back,” she said.

“Why?”

“Come back.”

I came back.

She did not shout at me. That was the part I did not expect. She stood there with her tail flat and looked at the place where I had been, and then at the black stone, and then at the place where I had been again.

“You didn’t ask,” she said.

“No.”

“You didn’t pay.”

“No.”

“You didn’t —”

She stopped.

I waited. I have learned since then that the most useful thing I do is wait.

“You didn’t *feel* it,” she said.

“No.”

“Everyone feels it.”

“I didn’t.”

Mara sat down in the road for the second time that morning.

“That’s not allowed,” she said.

She did not mean it was against the rules. I understood that even then. She meant something closer to: *that is not one of the things that can happen.*

Like the floor going soft.

Like the sky coming down.

“You should come home with me,” she said at last.

“Why?”

“Because I don’t want to be the only one who saw that.”

## Chapter 4: Bell

Mara lived on a shelf called Bell.

I learned the shape of it on the way. Bell was a flat piece of the great wall about a thousand steps long, with the cliff going up behind it and the cliff going down in front of it. The road ran the length of it. Houses were cut into the wall on the high side. On the low side, the gardens hung off the edge on ropes and frames, upside down from where I was, full of pale hanging fruit.

I looked over the edge once and could not find the bottom.

“Don’t do that,” Mara said, without turning around.

“Do what?”

“Look down. It’s rude.”

“Why is it rude?”

“Because down is other people’s.”

We passed a house where a Vey was walking up the wall beside her front door to fix a rope. Straight up, at an angle no creature should have been able to hold, with all four feet gripping the stone and her tail out behind her for balance. She was talking to someone inside the whole time. She did not stop talking. Nobody watched her.

I watched her.

“Is that hard?” I asked.

Mara looked up at her, then at me.

“Is what hard?”

I decided not to answer.

The market was at the wide end of Bell, where the shelf bulged out from the wall, and it was the loudest place I had been on any world. Bells on ropes. Feet clicking on stone. Somebody calling out prices in a rhythm that never quite finished. Stalls stood along the road and hung below it, and the ones below were reached by a plank walkway that went out over nothing at all. Vey went up and down it carrying baskets on their backs.

The smell was wet stone and warm fruit.

Mara stopped at the top of the road.

“Stay close,” she said.

“I am close.”

“Closer.”

I moved beside her.

“Closer.”

I touched her shoulder.

She jumped.

“Not that close.”

I moved away.

“You are difficult.”

“So are you.”

She looked pleased.

## Chapter 5: Thirty-Two Markers

The first thing I noticed in the market was that nobody walked in a straight line.

They could have. The road was wide and mostly empty in the middle. But every Vey in that market moved in the same strange way — along the edges, around certain stalls, stopping at certain places for no reason I could see and then going on.

They were going around things that were not there.

The second thing I noticed was the markers. Black stone posts with silver bands, standing at the corner of nearly every stall, at the ends of the plank walkway, at the mouth of every alley cut into the wall. Nobody looked at them. Everybody walked around them.

“Those are distance markers,” Mara said.

“How many?”

She looked at me.

“Don’t.”

I counted anyway.

There were thirty-two that I could see, and more below the edge that I could not.

At the mouth of the walkway a thin gray Vey sat behind a low table with a box on it. Every Vey who went out onto the planks stopped in front of him first. He gave each of them a small flat piece of blue shell, and they carried it out and came back and gave it to him again.

“What is he selling?”

“Crossings.”

“He is selling the walkway?”

“He’s selling the distance over the walkway. The walkway is just wood.” Mara said this patiently, the way you explain something to someone who has asked about the sun. “Bell Under is down there. About sixty families. If you live down there and you want to sell in the market, you cross Anlo’s distance twice a day.”

“Every day?”

“Every day.”

“For how long?”

Mara looked at me.

“Forever,” she said. “It’s his.”

I watched an old Vey come up the planks with an empty basket and hand back her blue shell. She was breathing hard. She counted out something small and metal into Anlo’s box, and Anlo said something kind to her, and she said something kind back, and she went down the planks again.

Neither of them seemed angry.

That was the part I kept looking at.

## Chapter 6: Claim

We passed a place where four children were playing in a marked-off square of road.

One of them had scratched a line in the dust with her foot. Another was creeping toward it with her belly low and her tail flat, trying to get across without being caught. The other two were shrieking at her.

“Mine!” the first one said. “Mine, mine, that’s mine —”

The creeping one crossed the line.

All four of them fell over.

“They’re playing Claim,” Mara said. “Everyone plays it.”

“What are the rules?”

“You mark a distance. Somebody tries to cross it. If they get across before you say *mine*, it isn’t yours anymore.”

“Did you play?”

“Everyone plays.”

“Were you good?”

“I was terrible,” Mara said. “I always said *mine* too early. Tavi’s better than I was.”

I watched the game start again. The child who had lost the line was already scratching a new one somewhere else, in a place that had been open ground a moment before, and the other children were already treating it as real.

They were four years old, maybe five.

Nobody had told them how.

## Chapter 7: The Dot

Mara’s mother had a stall near the middle of the market, on the wall side, where she was setting out blue fruit in rows.

She saw me before she saw Mara. She stopped with a fruit in her mouth and put it down slowly.

“Mara.”

“I know.”

“What is that?”

“I don’t know.”

“You brought it here?”

“I didn’t bring it.”

“Then why is it here?”

I moved closer.

“Because Mara asked me to come.”

She stared at me. Her tail came up off the ground.

“You talk.”

“Yes.”

Mara looked at me.

“You talk?”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t say you could talk.”

“You didn’t ask.”

Her mother made a small sound that seemed to contain several different thoughts at once.

“I’m Eli.”

“I am the Dot.”

Mara looked at me.

“You are?”

“The Dot.”

“Who named you that?”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“I am a dot.”

Mara looked at her mother. Eli looked at Mara.

Then Eli laughed. It was quieter than Mara’s laugh, and shorter, and she stopped it almost as soon as it started, the way you stop something you cannot afford.

“You can stay for supper,” she said.

I did not know what supper was.

I stayed.

## Chapter 8: One Real Distance

While Eli worked I floated at the edge of the stall and did what I do, which is look at a thing until I understand it.

I understood the fruit. I understood the money, which was small and metal and not very interesting to anyone. Then I noticed that Eli’s stall had a black stone at each end of it, and that the two stones had different marks on their silver lines, and that Eli stood between them all day and never once stepped past either one.

“Who owns the stall?” I asked.

“Nobody owns a stall,” Mara said. “You own the distance to it.”

“Who owns the distance to this one?”

“We do.”

She said it in a particular way. Not proud. Careful. The way you carry something full.

“Which distance?”

She pointed her nose up the road, past the stalls, past the walkway, to where the road bent around the wall and out of sight toward the houses.

“Home to market,” she said. “It’s ours. My grandmother’s mother claimed it. It’s the only real one we have.”

“What does it do?”

“It means we don’t pay to get here. And anyone who wants to sell at this end has to come past us, so we rent crossings sometimes, when the market’s busy.” Her fins went flat. “That’s most of our money.”

We stood there for a while.

“Venn wears a metal ring for every distance his family holds,” she said suddenly. “He has nine.”

“How many would you have?”

“One,” Mara said. “That’s why we don’t wear rings.”

## Chapter 9: Nine Rings

Venn came at midday.

He came up the road with two others and stopped at the end of Eli’s stall, and I understood before anyone said anything that something in the market had changed, because the noise around us got quieter in a circle.

He was larger than most Vey. Pale gray, well groomed, with a tail that had a line of small metal rings along it. He held the tail high.

“You are Mara.”

“Yes.”

“And this is?”

“The Dot.”

Venn looked at me.

“Dot.”

“Yes.”

“Are you a machine?”

“I don’t know.”

“An animal?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where are you from?”

“I don’t know.”

Venn looked at Mara.

“You brought an unknown thing into the market?”

“I’m not a thing,” I said.

Venn looked back at me. Then he lowered his head slightly.

“I’m sorry. I did not mean to offend you.”

“You did not.”

Mara smiled.

Venn smiled back.

He seemed nice.

That was important.

I did not dislike him.

Then he turned to Eli, and said, in exactly the same pleasant voice:

“I have filed on the market distance. The office will have written to you. I wanted you to hear it from me first, rather than from a paper.”

Eli did not move.

Mara said, “Filed on what?”

“Home to market,” Venn said. “The whole run.”

“That’s ours.”

“I know that you have used it,” Venn said. “I know that for a long time nobody has said otherwise.” He said it gently, and I have thought about that gentleness many times since. “But I hold a record for that distance. It came to me with my grandfather’s papers. I did not go looking for it. It was there.”

“We have a record,” Eli said.

It was the first thing she had said since he arrived.

“I know,” Venn said. “That is the difficulty.”

## Chapter 10: Two Records

The distance office on Bell was a small room cut into the wall behind the market, and it was full of paper.

Shelves went from the floor to the ceiling on every side. Boxes were stacked on the shelves and papers were stacked on the boxes. Old black markers leaned against the wall by the door in a row, waist-high and heavy, their silver bands gone dull. A window let in a bar of white light with dust turning over in it.

I liked the room immediately.

Mara did not.

“You’re going to make this worse,” she said.

“I don’t know how.”

“That’s what worries me.”

The clerk’s name was Orra. She was gray around the face and she had a way of looking at you over the top of whatever she was holding.

“Who is this?”

“The Dot.”

“That’s not a species.”

“It’s my name.”

“I see.”

She put down what she was holding.

“You’re the market run,” she said to Mara. “Home to market. Venn filed eleven days ago. I’ve been expecting you since.”

“Then you know it’s ours.”

“I know there are two records,” Orra said, “and I know they are both good, and I have been doing this work for thirty-one years and I have never had that happen.”

She got up and pulled two things off two different shelves and put them on the table side by side.

One was a folded sheet, soft with handling, the ink gone brown.

One was a page from a bound book, newer, the ink still black.

“Yours,” she said, touching the soft one. “Claimed by Sennet of Bell, recognized by the shelf, four generations back. Passed down. Never sold, never transferred, never disputed.”

“Then it’s ours.”

“His,” Orra said, touching the other. “Purchased by Venn’s grandfather from a family called Ruhl, sixty-one years ago. Recorded properly. Paid for. Never sold since, never transferred, never disputed.”

“Then ours is older,” Mara said.

“Yours is older.”

“So we win.”

“No.” Orra said it without any pleasure at all. “If both chains stand up, a transfer with a payment recorded against it outranks a claim that was only ever passed down. Money leaves a mark in the book. Inheritance mostly doesn’t.”

“That’s not fair.”

“It isn’t about fair. It’s about which line I can point at.” Orra looked at the two records. “That is why I have not sent this up yet. Because if I send it up as it stands, it goes to him, and I do not think that is the end of it.”

Mara looked from one to the other.

“They can’t both be right.”

“No,” Orra agreed. “They can’t.”

I moved closer to the table. I looked at the soft sheet, and then at the black page, and I read them both, which took a moment, because the writing on the old one was strange.

Then I said, “They aren’t the same.”

Orra looked at me.

“What?”

“They aren’t the same distance.”

“They are exactly the same distance,” Orra said. “I measured it myself. Both runs are four hundred and twelve steps. Both endpoints are the same two places. The house end is the old anchor stone above the bend. The market end is the second post at the north stalls.”

“Yes,” I said. “But this one” — the soft sheet — “says *from the house to the market*. And this one says *from the market to the house*.”

Nobody said anything.

Orra picked up both records. She read them again, the way people read something they have already read.

“That’s the same distance,” she said.

“Is it?”

“Of course it’s the same distance. It’s the same two ends.”

“Then why did two people write it two different ways?”

“Because —” Orra stopped. “Because that’s how they wrote it.”

“Does it matter which way you write it?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

Orra put the records down.

She looked at them for a long time. Her tail had gone very still.

“It has never mattered,” she said, which I noticed was a different answer.

Mara said, “What does it mean?”

“It means nothing,” Orra said. “It means two clerks a hundred years apart wrote a line down in the order it came into their heads. It means —”

She stopped again.

“It means I have to send it up,” she said.

## Chapter 11: Knotted Cord

Mara asked what *send it up* meant on the walk home and did not like the answer.

It meant the shelf authority. It meant a hearing. It meant that until somebody decided, nobody could use a distance that two people claimed, because using it would be treated as proof, and proof was the thing being argued about.

It meant the market run was shut.

Orra had been apologetic about it in the specific way of a person who is not going to change anything. She had walked out with us and put a paper collar around the old anchor stone above the bend, a strip of gray cord with a knot in it, and Mara had watched her do it without saying a word.

“How long?” Mara asked.

“Sixty days. Maybe ninety.”

“We sell every day.”

“I know.”

“We *live* every day.”

“I know,” Orra said. “I’m sorry. There is nothing in the record that lets me leave it open.”

She went back to her office.

Mara stood in the road and looked at the knotted cord.

I looked at it too.

It was a piece of string. That was all it was. It did not block the road. A creature could have stepped over it without lifting a foot. Mara could have walked through it and been at the market in four hundred and twelve steps.

Her fins were forward. She was feeling the edge that had been there all her life, and now the string had been added to it, and the string had done something to the edge that I could not see at all.

“Can you still feel it?” I asked.

“What?”

“The distance. Ours. Is it still there?”

Mara was quiet.

“It feels the same,” she said.

“Then walk it.”

“I can’t.”

“Why?”

“Because it isn’t ours right now.”

“Was it ours yesterday?”

“Yes.”

“Did anything happen to it?”

She turned and looked at me with her whole neck.

“You don’t understand,” she said, and there was something under her voice that had not been there before. “It doesn’t matter what happened to it. It matters what everyone thinks happened to it. If I walk that run tomorrow, Venn’s people will see me do it, and they’ll put it in front of the authority, and then it isn’t sixty days, it’s *ours forever* or *his forever*, and if it goes wrong it’s his forever.”

“So you go around.”

“So we go around.”

“How far around?”

Mara did the thing again — the not-squinting, the not-counting, the standing still.

“Two thousand and forty steps,” she said. “Down through Bell Under, across Anlo’s walkway, back up the far stair. Twice a day. Both ways.”

“That is five times as far.”

“Yes.”

“And you have to pay Anlo.”

“Yes.”

She started walking home.

I followed her, and after a while she said, without turning around, “I’m not angry at you.”

“You sound angry at me.”

“I know.”

## Chapter 12: One Empty Place

That evening there were three of them in the house, and me, and one empty place where Eli should have been.

The house was a room cut back into the wall with a woven front and two big open windows that looked out over the drop. There was a sleeping nest along one side and a water jar in the corner and a lamp hanging from the ceiling on a chain.

Tavi was smaller than Mara and asked questions faster than anyone I have met on any world.

“Can I touch it?”

“No,” Mara said.

“Why?”

“We don’t know what it is.”

“That doesn’t mean I can’t touch it.”

Nema sat by the window where she could see the road. She was old. Her fur had gone pale at the muzzle and one of her fins did not lift all the way. She watched me for most of the meal without asking a single question, which I found more interesting than anything Tavi said.

They ate small yellow fruit and warm roots. They offered me some of both. I told them I did not need food.

Tavi looked disappointed.

“Everything should need food.”

“Why?”

“So you can eat it.”

“That doesn’t explain it.”

Mara laughed.

Eli came home after dark. She had walked the two thousand and forty steps with a full basket and she came in and set the basket down and stood with her head low for a while before she said anything.

“Half,” she said. “Half of what we usually take. Nobody comes to the north stalls if they have to pay Anlo to get there.”

“Ninety days,” Mara said.

“I can count.”

“We could —”

“Mara.” Eli lifted her head. “Do not say the thing you are about to say.”

Mara did not say it.

Later, when Mara and Tavi were arguing about something involving a piece of string, Nema spoke for the first time.

“You’re not lost.”

I turned toward her.

“I don’t think so.”

She smiled.

“That’s not what I meant.”

I waited.

She did not explain.

## Chapter 13: The Old Road

The next morning Mara took me out past the west end of Bell to show me a way around, and that is how we found the old road, although we were not looking for it and Mara did not think of it as a road at all.

The west end was where Bell narrowed and the houses stopped. There were sheds out there, and a rope frame with nothing growing on it, and the wall above went up bare and gray and very high.

Mara was walking the edge, checking the drop, muttering numbers to herself.

I was looking at the wall.

There was a line on it.

It was faint. It ran along the face of the cliff about the height of a house above the shelf, a narrow ledge that went out from the end of Bell and around the curve of the wall and out of sight. Something had been cut into the rock along it a very long time ago. There were stumps of posts at intervals. Most of them had broken off.

“What is that?”

Mara looked up.

“Nothing.”

“It goes somewhere.”

“It goes to Corran,” she said. “Or it went to Corran. Nobody uses it.”

“Why not?”

“Because nobody uses it.”

I floated up and along the ledge for about thirty steps and came back.

“It’s there,” I said. “The rock is good. The posts are broken but the cut is clean. A Vey could walk it.”

“A Vey could not walk it.”

“You walked up a wall to get here.”

“That’s not —” Mara made a frustrated sound in her throat. “It’s not the *rock*.”

“Then what is it?”

She did not answer for a while. She sat down at the west end of Bell with the bare wall going up behind her and looked at the faint line on the stone.

“My grandmother would not go near it,” she said. “She used to say —”

“What?”

“Never cross what nobody owns.”

She said it the way she had said *you’re the kind of person who has nothing to take*. Not as an argument. As a thing everyone says.

“Who owns it?”

“Nobody.”

“Then who says you can’t cross it?”

Mara opened her mouth to answer and found she did not have an answer.

That was the first time I was useful to her, I think. Not because I helped. Because I asked a question in the exact place where nobody had put a question before, and she heard the hole it made.

“Ask Orra,” I said.

“Orra will laugh at me.”

“Then she’ll laugh at you and you’ll know.”

## Chapter 14: Nobody's

Orra did not laugh.

She went very still with a paper half lifted, and she said, “The Corran ledge?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Mara glanced at me.

“Because we want to know who owns it.”

Orra put the paper down.

She was gone a long time in the back of the room. We heard boxes moving. When she came back she was carrying a book that was not like the other books. It was bigger, and the cover was wood, and the pages inside had gone the color of old fruit.

She turned pages carefully, from the back.

“Corran,” she said. “Corran. Here.”

She read.

Then she read it again.

“Well,” she said.

“What?”

“The Corran run was held by a family called Ise. Recognized. Recorded. It ran from the west post of Bell to the upper stair at Corran, eight hundred and thirty steps.” Orra turned a page. “The last recognition is a hundred and forty years old. The holder was Ise Marrow. There is a note that Ise Marrow died. There is no transfer. There is no sale. There is no heir recorded.”

She turned another page. It was blank.

She turned another. Blank.

“Then it stops,” she said.

Mara said, “Stops how?”

“Stops. The entries stop. Somebody wrote *Corran* at the top of the next page and never wrote anything under it.”

“So who has it now?”

Orra looked at the blank page for a while.

“Nobody,” she said.

“Nobody’s *filed*,” Mara said. “That’s different. Somebody must —”

“Mara.” Orra put her foot flat on the page. “I keep these records. If there were a recognized owner, he would be in this book. If there were a successor, he would be in this book. There is no recognized owner and no surviving successor. Under the record, that distance belongs to no one.”

The dust turned over in the bar of light.

Mara said, “That’s not possible.”

“No,” Orra agreed.

“A distance has to belong to someone.”

“Yes.”

“Then this one does and you’ve lost the page.”

“Perhaps,” Orra said. “I have looked at this book for thirty-one years and I have never lost a page. But perhaps.”

Mara’s tail had gone flat on the floor.

I said, “What happens to a distance with no owner?”

“It fails,” Orra said.

“What does that mean?”

“It means it goes bad. It stretches, or it folds, or it stops arriving where it used to arrive. That is why we hold them.” She said it easily, the way Mara had said *eleven steps*. “That is what holding is *for*. A held distance is stable. That is all of it.”

“Has anyone seen one fail?”

Orra opened her mouth.

I have thought about that pause many times.

“They are extremely rare,” she said.

## Chapter 15: The Owner's Mark

We went back out to the west end in the late light, when the sky had gone from white toward violet, and Mara stood at the bottom of the broken ledge and would not go up.

“You said the rock was good.”

“The rock is good.”

“Then why won’t you go?”

“Because I’ve been told not to my whole life,” Mara said. “Doesn’t that count for anything with you?”

“I don’t know,” I said. “Nobody has told me anything my whole life.”

She laughed. She did not want to.

“I’m going to look at it,” I said. “Not you. Me.”

“Dot —”

I went up.

The ledge was about two feet wide where it left Bell and it stayed about two feet wide. The cut was old but the stone under the cut was solid. I went out along it and around the curve of the wall, and the shelf behind me went out of sight, and there was nothing under me but a very long way down and nothing above me but wall.

There was no marker anywhere.

That was the thing I kept noticing. On Bell there was a black stone every forty steps. Out here there was nothing. Just rock, and broken posts, and the wind moving along the face.

At the far end, where the ledge widened and went up a broken stair, I found a place where somebody had cut something into the rock beside the path a very long time ago.

It was small. Weather had taken most of it.

A circle with a dot inside it.

I stopped.

I do not know how to describe what happened, except that nothing happened. There was no sound. There was no light. I looked at the mark, and the mark was familiar, and I could not attach the familiarity to anything. Not a place. Not a memory. Not a shape I had seen. Only the flat certainty that I had seen it before and no idea where.

I stayed there for a while.

Then I measured the ledge, because measuring is what I do when I do not understand something.

Eight hundred and thirty steps.

I went back.

## Chapter 16: Eight Hundred and Thirty

Mara was still at the west end. She had not moved. The light had gone deep violet and the first faint moving lights had come out below us in the Open.

“Well?” she said.

“It’s eight hundred and thirty steps.”

She stared at me.

“What?”

“Eight hundred and thirty steps. From the west post to the upper stair at Corran. The same as the record.”

Mara sat down slowly.

“It hasn’t stretched,” I said. “It hasn’t folded. It arrives where it used to arrive. Nobody has owned it for a hundred and forty years and it is exactly where they left it.”

She did not say anything.

“I crossed it,” I said.

“I know.”

“Nothing happened.”

“Nothing ever happens to *you*,” Mara said, and put her head down on her feet.

## Chapter 17: The Thing Nobody Sees

I want to be careful here, because this is the part I got wrong first.

What I thought, walking back that night, was: *the Vey are wrong about distance.*

That is not what was happening. It took me two more days to understand what was happening, and I only understood it because Mara refused to agree with me.

She would not talk about the ledge for a full day.

She took the long way to the market with Eli instead — down through Bell Under, across Anlo’s walkway with a blue shell in her mouth, up the far stair — and she came back tired and did not want to discuss anything.

On the second day she said, “It could have failed and come back.”

“Distances don’t do that.”

“You don’t know what distances do.”

That was fair.

On the third day she said, “Ise Marrow might have had a child nobody wrote down.”

“Then the ledge has an owner who has never once been there, has never taken payment, has never given permission, and does not know he owns it.”

“Yes.”

“How is that different from nobody?”

Mara stopped walking.

We were on the road above the market. Below us the stalls were opening. Anlo was setting out his box. The children were already in the road with a fresh line scratched in the dust.

“It’s completely different,” she said.

“Tell me how.”

“Because if there’s an owner then the distance is *held*.” Her fins came forward. She was angry, and being angry made her precise. “It’s held whether he’s standing there or not. That’s what holding is. It doesn’t come off when he goes to sleep.”

“Yes,” I said. “That’s what I don’t understand. What is doing the holding?”

“He is.”

“He isn’t there.”

“He doesn’t have to be there!”

“Then what is there?”

Mara opened her mouth.

Below us in the road the smallest child scratched her line, and shrieked *mine*, and three other children stopped dead at a mark in the dust that had not existed four seconds earlier and was not a wall and was not a hole and could not stop anyone at all.

Mara had turned to look at them.

I watched her watch them.

Then she made a sound I had not heard a Vey make before. Small. Like something coming loose.

“We are,” she said.

## Chapter 18: What Is Holding It?

She would not talk for a long time after that.

We sat on the low wall above the market and Mara looked at the children and I looked at Mara.

“Say it,” she said eventually.

“You say it. You said it.”

“I don’t want to.”

“Then don’t.”

“*You* say it.”

So I said it, badly, because I was working it out while I said it, and because I did not know yet how much it was going to cost her.

“The distance is there,” I said. “The one under the ledge and the one under your market run and the one under that child’s line. The space is there. It stays there. It doesn’t need anyone.”

“And the holding?”

“The holding is you. All of you. Everyone stopping at the same line at the same time, every day, since before anyone can remember.” I looked at the black stones down the length of the market, thirty-two of them that I could see. “That’s what a marker is. It isn’t a lock. It’s a reminder of what everyone has already agreed to do.”

“And you,” Mara said.

“What?”

“You didn’t agree.”

“No.”

“That’s why you can cross.”

“I think so.”

“You’re not *strong*,” she said. She sounded almost disgusted. “You’re not — you don’t have some power. You just weren’t *here* when we all decided.”

“Yes.”

She got down off the wall and stood in the road with her back to me.

“Then none of it’s real,” she said.

“That’s not what I said.”

“It’s what it *means*. My mother stands between two stones all day because everyone agreed she has to. Anlo takes money from an old woman twice a day for walking on a plank because everyone agreed he can. Venn has nine rings on his tail because —”

She stopped.

“Because everyone agreed,” I said.

“Does that mean none of it belongs to us?”

I did not answer quickly. I understood, by then, that this was the kind of question where the true answer and the useful answer might not be the same, and I did not yet know which one to give.

“I don’t know,” I said.

It was true.

It was also the wrong thing to say, and it hurt her, and I did not understand how much until she walked away from me down the road and did not look back.

## Chapter 19: Fire

She found me that night, out at the west end, where I had gone to look at the ledge in the dark.

She sat down beside me without saying anything for a while.

“I was thinking about the fire,” she said at last.

“What fire?”

“Not a real fire.” She pulled her tail around her feet. “When Tavi was small, my mother told her not to touch the lamp. Tavi asked why. My mother said *because it burns*. And Tavi didn’t touch it, and she didn’t touch it, and one day she touched it.”

“Did it burn her?”

“Yes.” Mara almost smiled. “That’s the point. It burned her. My mother wasn’t making it up to control her. The lamp is *actually hot*.”

She looked out at the ledge, faint gray in the dark.

“That’s what I always thought this was,” she said. “That’s what everyone thinks it is. Don’t cross what nobody owns, because it *burns*. Because it’s real. Because the world does something to you.” She was quiet. “And now you’re telling me nobody ever checked.”

“Somebody must have checked.”

“When?”

I did not have an answer.

“A hundred and forty years,” Mara said. “That’s how long that ledge has been sitting there being fine. How many people have walked past it and gone the long way?”

Neither of us said anything for a while.

“It might still be true,” I said.

She turned her head.

“What?”

“You keep saying *none of it is real*. That isn’t what I found. I found one distance that has no owner and hasn’t failed.” I looked at the ledge. “That’s one. That isn’t all of them. Maybe some of them do fail and this one is strange. I don’t know. I only know what I measured.”

Mara let out a long breath.

“That’s the first useful thing you’ve said in three days,” she said.

“I have said several useful things.”

“You have said several *true* things,” Mara said. “It’s not the same.”

She stood up and shook herself out.

“Ninety days,” she said. “That’s what Orra said. We’ve had eleven.”

## Chapter 20: The Short Way

I did the arithmetic that night, because that is the kind of thing I am for.

From Mara’s house, the west end of Bell was one hundred and ninety steps.

The ledge to Corran was eight hundred and thirty.

From the upper stair at Corran, the market road came back along the underside of the wall and up into the north stalls, where Eli’s stall stood between its two black stones. I had gone and measured it while they slept. Four hundred and sixty.

One thousand four hundred and eighty steps.

The way they were walking now — down through Bell Under, across Anlo’s walkway, up the far stair — was two thousand and forty, and cost two crossings, twice a day, every day, for the next seventy-nine days.

I told Mara at breakfast.

“No,” she said.

“You haven’t heard the number.”

“I don’t need the number.”

“It’s five hundred and sixty steps shorter. Each way. And it’s free.”

“It is *not* free,” Mara said, and put her food down. “Dot. Listen to me. I know you measured it. I believe you measured it. You are the best measurer I have ever met and I haven’t even known you a week.”

“Then —”

“It’s still an open distance.”

“You’ve seen it.”

“I’ve seen *you* on it!” She was up now, moving. “You’re not — you don’t have a family. You don’t have a mother who can’t work. If it folds under you, what happens? You float. What happens if it folds under me?”

“It won’t fold.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I measured it twice.”

“You measured it *twice*,” Mara said. “In a hundred and forty years. What if it’s fine for a hundred and forty years and then it isn’t?”

I did not have an answer for that either.

She stood in the middle of the room breathing hard.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“You don’t have to be sorry.”

“I know. That’s not why I said it.”

## Chapter 21: The Cost of Being Wrong

The thing I had not understood yet was that Mara was not being stupid.

I want to be clear about that, because at the time I thought she was. I thought she had all the facts and would not put them together. I thought if I said the number one more time, in a slightly better way, she would see it.

What Mara had that I did not was a picture of what happens to a family that is wrong.

I found that out four days later.

Eli came home early with an empty basket and sat down in the doorway.

“The north stalls are closing,” she said.

Mara said, “What?”

“Not closing. Moving.” Eli’s voice was flat and careful. “Half the sellers have gone down to the south end where Anlo’s walkway comes up. That’s where the buyers are now. Nobody walks to the north stalls anymore because getting there costs two crossings.” She looked at her feet. “Sennet’s boy asked me today whether I was going to move down as well.”

“Are you?”

“With what?” Eli said. “There’s no space at the south end that isn’t held. I’d be renting a crossing to stand somewhere and paying two more to get there.”

Nema said, from her window, “How long?”

“Six weeks. Maybe less.”

“And then?”

“And then we go to Corran,” Eli said. “My cousin’s there. There’s work.”

Tavi looked up.

“Are we moving?”

“Eat your food.”

“Are we *moving*?”

“Eat your food, Tavi.”

Mara had not moved at all.

“Corran,” she said.

“Yes.”

“We’d walk to Corran.”

“We’d walk down to Bell Under and out along the low road,” Eli said. “Four days with the baskets. Your grandmother can’t do it in four. Six, maybe.”

Mara said, very quietly, “There’s a ledge.”

The room went wrong.

I did not understand it at the time. I understood it afterward. Eli had spent eleven years keeping this family attached to a piece of the world by four hundred and twelve steps of agreement, and her daughter had just said the one sentence that meant *I have been thinking about giving that up.*

“No,” Eli said.

“Mother —”

“I said no.”

“You haven’t heard —”

“I have heard it since I was younger than Tavi and so have you.” Eli was on her feet. Her tail was straight out and stiff. “Never cross what nobody owns. Do you know why we say that? Because people *went out on those roads*, Mara. Because there are families on this shelf who are missing people.”

“Are there?” I said.

Everyone looked at me.

I had not meant it as an argument. I had meant it as a question, because it was one, and I did not know the answer.

“Are there?” I said again. “You said families on this shelf. Which families?”

Eli stared at me.

“Everyone knows there are.”

“That isn’t the same,” I said. “I’m asking which.”

Nobody said anything.

Nema said, from the window, “The Ise.”

We all turned.

“The Ise?” Mara said.

“That’s the story I was told.” Nema’s fins were flat and she did not lift her head. “Ise Marrow held the Corran run and Ise Marrow was lost on it, and after that nobody held it, and after that nobody crossed it.” She looked at me for a moment. “That is the story I was told when I was four years old by a woman who was told it when she was four years old.”

“Was it true?”

“I don’t know,” Nema said. “I have never met anyone who does.”

Eli said, “That’s *enough*.”

She said it loudly enough that Tavi jumped.

Then she went out onto the road and stood with her back to the house, and after a while Mara went out and stood beside her, and I stayed inside because I understood that much, at least, by then.

Through the window I heard Eli say, “I’m not stupid, Mara.”

“I never said —”

“I know what the walk to Corran does to your grandmother. I know what six weeks does to us. I have done the counting too.” A long pause. “I am asking you not to be the one who’s wrong.”

## Chapter 22: Ask Me Something

Mara came back in very late.

I was floating by the window where Nema usually sat.

“Ask me something,” she said.

“What?”

“You always have a question. Ask me one.”

I thought about it.

“When you were watching the children play Claim,” I said, “and you said *we are*. What did you feel?”

Mara sat down.

“Sick,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because I’ve been stopping at lines my whole life.” She put her chin on her feet. “And I was good at it. I was *proud* of it. I know where every marker on this shelf is. I know who holds what. I thought that was knowing how the world works.”

“It is knowing how the world works.”

“No it isn’t. It’s knowing what everyone decided.” She was quiet for a while. “Dot. If I walk that ledge and I’m right, we keep the stall and my grandmother doesn’t walk six days. If I walk it and I’m wrong —”

She did not finish.

“Then you were wrong,” I said.

“That’s not a comfort.”

“I wasn’t comforting you. I don’t know how to do that yet.” I moved a little closer. “I was agreeing with you. You might be wrong. I might be wrong. I measured a thing twice and that is all I have.”

Mara laughed. It was not the bright sound. It was the other one.

“You’re terrible at this.”

“Yes.”

“Would you go with me?”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t ask when.”

“You’ll tell me when.”

She lay there for a while with her eyes open.

“Before light,” she said.

## Chapter 23: Before Light

She did not go before light.

She got up before light, and she stood at the door for a long time, and then she went back and lay down again, and I did not say anything about it.

She went at midday.

That was the part I had not expected, and it took me until we were out at the west end to understand it, and when I did understand it I thought it was the bravest thing I had seen on that world.

Mara had waited until the market was full.

She had waited until Anlo’s walkway was busy, and the road above the stalls had Vey on it, and the children were out in the dust, and Orra had come out of her office to eat in the light the way she did every day at midday.

She wanted them to see.

“If I do it in the dark,” she said, “it’s a story. Somebody heard that the Bell girl walked the ledge. Somebody heard she didn’t.” Her tail was shaking. She was holding it very deliberately off the ground and it was shaking anyway. “If I do it now they don’t get to decide whether it happened.”

Eli was at the west end.

I do not know how she knew. Mara had not told her. She was simply there, standing at the bottom of the broken stair with her tail flat against the stone, and when we came up she did not say anything at all.

“Mother.”

“Don’t.”

“I have to —”

“Don’t explain it to me.” Eli’s voice was not steady. “If you explain it to me I will argue with you, and if I argue with you I might win, and then I have to live with that.”

Mara stopped.

“Go,” Eli said. “Go now, before I —”

Mara went up the stair.

## Chapter 24: Where the Marker Isn't

The ledge left Bell about the height of a house above the shelf and it was two feet wide at the mouth.

Mara stopped at the place where the cut stone began.

There was no marker there. That was the thing. On every other edge of every other distance on that shelf there was a black stone with a silver line, and here there was nothing, and Mara stood at the mouth of eight hundred and thirty steps of nothing with her fins pinned flat back into her fur.

“I can’t feel anything,” she said.

“Is that bad?”

“I don’t know. I’ve never —” She swallowed. “Every distance I’ve ever walked, I could feel where it was. Whose it was. Where the edges were. It’s like — it’s like putting your foot down.”

“And now?”

“Now it’s like the floor isn’t there.” She laughed, high and wrong. “It *is* there. I can see it. I can see it with my eyes. It’s just not —”

She stopped.

“You don’t have to,” I said.

“I know.”

“I mean it. I measured a thing twice. That’s all I have.”

“I *know*, Dot.”

She put one front foot on the cut stone. The one with the lighter patch.

Then she put the other three on it.

Then she walked.

## Chapter 25: Eight Hundred and Thirty Steps

Nothing happened.

Mara walked out along the ledge with her belly low and her tail out for balance, and the stone held, and the ledge stayed two feet wide, and the wind came along the face of the wall the way it had every other day for a hundred and forty years.

At about a hundred steps she stopped and stood still for a long time.

“It’s not moving,” she called back.

“No.”

“It should be moving.”

“Yes.”

She went on.

Below us the market had noticed. I could hear it happening — the way sound in a market changes when everyone stops doing one thing at slightly different times. Somebody shouted something. Somebody else told them to be quiet.

At three hundred steps Mara stopped again, and this time she turned around and looked back at Bell, and I understood that she was looking at the whole shelf from a place no Vey had stood in a hundred and forty years.

At the curve of the wall the ledge went out of sight of the market, and she went around it, and for four hundred steps nobody on Bell could see her at all.

I stayed with her.

She did not say anything for that whole stretch. Her breathing was very fast. Twice she stopped and put her head down against the rock.

“Talk to me,” she said once.

“About what?”

“Anything. Numbers.”

So I counted for her. Out loud, step by step, four hundred and eleven, four hundred and twelve, four hundred and thirteen, all the way around the blind curve of the wall, and she walked to the sound of it.

Then the ledge widened.

Then there was the broken stair.

Then there was the mark cut in the rock beside it, the circle with the dot inside it, and Mara stopped and looked at it for a moment because she had never seen it before and I did not tell her anything about it, because we were not finished.

Eight hundred and thirty steps.

Above us was Corran.

“Don’t go up,” I said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

She sat down on the wide place at the end of the ledge and put her head between her feet and breathed.

“We have to go back,” she said.

“I know.”

“That’s the part nobody thinks about.” She laughed, and this time it was almost the bright sound. “You have to do it *twice*.”

## Chapter 26: Still Here

They were all at the west end when we came back around the curve.

I have never counted a crowd before or since. There were about ninety Vey on the road above the market, and forty more below on the walkway with their heads up, and children on the wall, and Anlo standing in front of his box with a blue shell still in his mouth.

Orra was at the bottom of the broken stair.

Eli was where we had left her. She had not moved in the entire time. That is nine hundred steps of standing still, twice, at the bottom of a stair, and when Mara came down off the last cut step Eli did not say a word. She put her neck along her daughter’s neck and her tail around her daughter’s feet and stayed like that.

Mara said something into her mother’s shoulder that I did not hear.

Then she sat down in the road, very suddenly, the way you do when a thing is over.

“We’re still here,” she said.

“Yes.”

“The world didn’t end.”

“No.”

She laughed.

Then she cried.

I moved closer.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Then why are you crying?”

“Because I’m scared.”

“You are scared and happy.”

“Yes.”

“Can both happen?”

Mara wiped her face with one front foot.

“Usually,” she said.

I remembered that.

## Chapter 27: The Record

Orra tested it the next morning, and that is the part I did not expect, because Orra did not believe us.

She said so.

“I am not calling you a liar,” she said to Mara, in front of about two hundred Vey, with her measuring cord over her shoulder and her tail rigid. “I am saying that I have kept the record on this shelf for thirty-one years, and a girl telling me a thing is not the record, and the whole point of the record is that it does not care who tells it things.”

Then she walked the ledge herself.

She went slowly. She stopped constantly. She put down her cord at every broken post and read it and wrote the number in a small book, all the way out, around the blind curve, out of sight.

She was gone a long time.

When she came back her fins were forward and she did not look at anyone.

“Eight hundred and thirty steps,” she said.

Nobody said anything.

“Eight hundred and thirty steps,” Orra said again, louder. “West post of Bell to the upper stair at Corran. That is the figure in the book. That is the figure the Ise recorded a hundred and forty-one years ago.” She lifted her small book and then put it down as though it had gotten heavier. “It has not stretched. It has not folded. It arrives where it says it arrives. And there is no owner. There has been no owner since before my grandmother was born.”

Somebody in the crowd said, “That can’t be right.”

“No,” Orra said. “It can’t.”

She sat down on a rock at the west end of Bell and looked at nothing for a while.

Venn was there, at the back, with his tail held perfectly still.

He had come at some point during the morning. He had not said anything to anyone. I saw him look at the ledge, and then at his own tail with its nine rings, and then at the ledge again, and I did not know what he was thinking and I have never found out.

## Chapter 28: Open Distance

The shelf authority took nineteen days.

They did not do what Mara hoped and they did not do what Eli feared, and I have come to believe that this is what authorities are for.

They did not abolish holding. Nobody asked them to. Nine hundred people on Bell owned distances, and most of them were small, and most of the people who owned them were not Venn — they were families with one run and a black stone and a life built around it, and taking that away would have broken more than it fixed.

What they did was smaller and stranger.

They wrote a new word into the book.

**Open distance.** A distance with no recognized owner and no surviving successor, which has been walked and measured and found to be stable. An open distance stays open. It cannot be claimed. It cannot be bought. It is nobody’s, and it stays nobody’s, and anyone may cross it.

The Corran ledge was the first.

Orra wrote it in herself, on the blank page where the entries had stopped, under the word *Corran* that somebody had written a hundred and forty years ago and never written anything under.

“There will be others,” she told us. She sounded tired and something else I did not have a word for yet. “Nine, that I know of. Old runs where the record stops. I have been treating them as errors in the book.” She turned the page over. “I have been treating them as errors for thirty-one years.”

“Will you walk them?” I asked.

“I will walk them.”

“All nine?”

“I am fifty-four years old and I have a bad shoulder,” Orra said. “Yes. All nine.”

Then she said, “I never did work out the other thing. Why one clerk wrote it one way around and one wrote it the other. I have stopped waiting to find out.”

## Chapter 29: What It Is Worth

Venn did not become good and he did not become bad.

The authority found for him on the market run. It went exactly the way Orra had said it would go on the first day: both chains stood up, and the one with a payment recorded against it outranked the one that had only ever been passed down. Mara’s family’s record was older and it was true and it did not matter.

Eli took the news standing up, in the doorway, exactly the way she had taken everything else.

But the Corran ledge came out at the upper stair, and the road back from the upper stair came in at the north stalls, and by the time the ruling came down there were already eleven families using it, because it was one thousand four hundred and eighty steps instead of two thousand and forty and it did not cost anything.

The buyers came back to the north end.

Venn owned the market run. He owns it still. What he did not own anymore was the only way to get there.

He came to the stall about a week later and stood at the end of it in his careful way.

“Your figures are up,” he said to Eli.

“They are.”

“I’m glad.”

He meant it. That was the difficult part. I looked at him for a long time trying to find the place where he was lying and I never found it.

“I paid for that run,” he said, to nobody in particular. “My grandfather paid for that run. It is a good run. It has been a good run for sixty years.” He looked up the road toward the west end, where you could just see the faint line on the wall. “I do not know what it is worth now.”

“Less,” Eli said.

“Yes.”

“Is that unfair?”

Venn thought about it.

“I don’t know,” he said, which I thought was the best answer anyone gave me on that world.

Then he said, to me: “I have nine rings.”

“I know.”

“Are they real?”

I understood that he was asking me seriously, and that I was possibly the only creature he could ask.

“They’re real,” I said. “They’re just not what you thought they were.”

He was quiet.

“That is not as comforting as you think,” he said.

“I’ve been told.”

## Chapter 30: The Second Time

Before I left, Nema called me over to the window.

“Sit,” she said, which was a joke, because I could not.

I floated where she pointed.

“You went out on the ledge before my granddaughter did,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You saw the mark by the upper stair.”

I stopped.

“You’ve seen it,” I said.

“Once. When I was nine. My mother took me out to the west end to show me where not to go.” Her old fin lifted about halfway. “You can see it from Bell on a bright morning, if you know where to look. I have known where to look for sixty-eight years.”

“What is it?”

“My mother called it an owner’s mark,” Nema said. “She didn’t know whose. She didn’t know how old. I don’t know whether she was right about any of it.”

“Could she have been wrong?”

“Somebody told her when she was small, the same as she told me.” Nema’s tail moved once. “That is how everything on this shelf is known.”

“Did anyone know?”

Nema looked at me for a long moment with her pale muzzle and her half-lifted fin, and I understood that she was deciding something.

“When I was four years old,” she said, “I was told a story about a thing that came from outside and could walk on any road. I have thought about that story twice in my life. Once when I was four.”

She did not say when the second time was.

“That is all I am going to say to you,” she said. “I don’t know if it is true. I would rather give you one thing I am unsure of than ten things I made up.”

## Chapter 31: Come Back

Mara walked with me to the edge of Bell.

It was early. The light was still gold. Below us the market was opening and the first Vey of the morning were going out along the ledge — a woman with a basket, then two more behind her, moving without stopping at anything, because there was nothing out there to stop at.

“Are you leaving?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you ever know?”

“No.”

She smiled.

“That’s annoying.”

“Yes.”

We watched the ledge for a while.

“They still don’t like it,” Mara said. “Half the shelf won’t use it. My mother’s used it twice and she says she doesn’t sleep after.”

“Do you sleep after?”

“No.”

She said it lightly. Then she said, less lightly:

“I still stop. Every time. At the west post, where the marker isn’t. I get right up to it and my feet stop and I have to make them go.”

“Will that end?”

“I don’t know,” Mara said. “I don’t think I want it to, particularly. It’s not a bad thing to stop and think about where you’re putting your feet.”

She reached out and touched the side of me with the front foot that had the lighter patch.

“Will you come back?”

“I don’t know.”

She looked down.

I moved closer.

“I would like to.”

She smiled.

This time she did not cry.

“Then come back.”

“I will try.”

## Chapter 32: The Near End

I went up.

The houses became small. The market became small. The black markers went from posts to dots scattered along the shelves, thirty-two on Bell and hundreds more below it, all the way down the great wall until the light gave out.

From high enough, they were just marks on a rock.

I looked down one more time. Mara was still standing at the west end, and beside her, very faint, was the line of the old road going out around the curve of the wall where nobody had been for a hundred and forty years and where four Vey were walking that morning.

Then even the shelves were small.

Then the world was small.

Then it was gone, and I was in the place I go between, where the sky is black and there is a white line and a shape that opens, and I have crossed it more times than I can count without ever once looking at it.

I looked at it.

It was a distance.

That was all it was — a space between two places, with a length, the way the ledge had a length, the way everything has a length if you are willing to measure it.

I had crossed it hundreds of times.

I had never asked whose it was.

And at the near end of it, cut into nothing at all, small and weathered and older than any book on any world I had been to, was a circle with a dot inside it.
