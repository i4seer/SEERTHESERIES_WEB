## Chapter 1: A Great Deal of Noise

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to six worlds. On the last one I found a road somebody had built and somebody else had cut off, and the cut had an edge on it that stone cannot make. It looked like the kind of edge I might be going through every time I arrive somewhere. So on the way here I went slowly, and looked at the thing I was going through instead of at what was on the other side of it, and I still could not tell. It is hard to look at a boundary from inside it. I have never once been outside one.

Then I came out into a great deal of noise and somebody nearly dropped a crate on me.

---

It was a harbor.

Blue water in a curve, boats in rows, and the whole town going up the slope behind it in steps: stone and pale plaster, terrace above terrace, washing out on lines, windows open, the smell of salt and fish and hot oil and rope.

After a world with no ground at all, an ordinary town with an ordinary bottom to it was one of the best things I have ever floated into.

The docks were the loudest part. Boats were coming in and going out and being unloaded by people shouting numbers at each other, and there were crates and nets and bales of cloth and stacked pots and a man arguing with another man about a rope that had apparently been the wrong rope for eleven years.

Everybody had two arms and two legs and a face, which I mention because on the last world nobody had a face at all and I had got used to being looked at from both ends. Here they looked at me and then went back to work. A floating thing the size of a fist is interesting for about four seconds in a place where four boats are trying to dock at once.

I went up the hill to see what was up the hill, and about halfway I stopped, because two people were having an argument that made no sense to me at all.

## Chapter 2: The Shape of a World

They were in the street outside a rope-seller’s. One of them was old, sitting on a step. The other one was younger and had the face of somebody who had already lost.

“It’s not worth eight,” the young one said.

“It’s worth eight.”

“It’s an afternoon.”

“It’s a good afternoon.”

“It’s an afternoon *fishing*.”

“It’s a good afternoon fishing,” said the old one, “and you owe me eight, and I am not going to be paid in coin because you haven’t got any.”

The young one thought about it and then made a sound like a man agreeing to be robbed.

And then they sat down together on the step, and the old one said something quietly, and the young one said something back, and it took about as long as it takes to tell somebody the way to a place, and then the old one stood up and went back into the rope-seller’s without saying goodbye.

The young one sat on the step a moment longer, looking pleased and slightly cheated, which is a difficult combination to get onto one face.

“What was that?”

“What?”

“You paid him something.”

“That’s the eight settled,” he said. “He wouldn’t wait for the boat money, so.”

“You didn’t give him anything.”

The young one looked at me properly for the first time, and I watched him decide that I was foreign in some large way he could not be bothered to work out.

“An afternoon,” he said. “Fishing. Off the point. Years back. My father was still going out then. Good day. Nothing happened.”

“You told it to him?”

“I *gave* it to him.”

“And now he has it.”

“Now he has it,” he agreed.

“And you...”

“And now I haven’t,” he said, and stood up, and brushed off the back of his legs, and looked out at the water for a second. “Eight’s eight.”

Then he went off down the hill and I stayed where I was, because I had just been given the shape of a world in about nine seconds and I needed a moment with it.

---

Here it is.

You can give somebody a memory. Not tell them about it. Give it. Afterward they have it the way you have yours, with the light in it and the smell of it and how it felt at the time.

And you do not have it anymore.

That is the part I had wrong all morning. I assumed, because I am a dot and I think the way a dot thinks, that there would now be two of it. One each. That is what happens when I tell you something.

There is one. It moves. And the place it came from is empty.

## Chapter 3: Somewhere Safe

I spent the rest of the morning checking whether this was ordinary.

It was. Nobody could be got to be interested in it. I asked a woman hanging washing whether she had ever given one away and she said of course, several, and asked whether I wanted to know about her sister’s wedding, because she had two separate memories from that day and only needed one.

The town delighted me. It was the strangest thing I had ever seen and everybody was treating it like a market for onions.

Then I went up to the top terrace, where the streets get narrow and the washing goes across from house to house, and I met Yarrow.

---

She was sitting outside her door in the sun with a bowl in her lap, doing something to a quantity of small green things, and she looked at me the way you look at a bird that has come too close.

“You’ll be the floating thing.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve been up and down my street four times.”

“Three.”

“Four,” said Yarrow. “You went past when I was getting the water.”

She was old. Not frail. She had arms on her like somebody who had carried things her whole life. But old, and the banding at her wrists had gone dark the way it does.

I asked her what was in the bowl and she told me, at length, and it was not interesting, and she was funny about it being not interesting.

Then I asked her whether she had ever given one away.

“One big one,” she said.

“What was it?”

And she told me. Straight out, no ceremony, still working at the bowl.

“The day my sister and I put it right. We didn’t speak for nine years. Then one afternoon we did, and it was mended, and it’s stayed mended since.” She tipped the bowl to get the light on it. “That one.”

“You gave that away?”

“I gave it to a keeper. Last spring.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m losing them,” said Yarrow.

---

She said it the way you would say *because it was raining*.

There is a thing that happens to some people here as they get old. Memories start going. It has a name in that town and they say it flatly, and Yarrow said it flatly, and went back to the bowl.

“So I put that one somewhere safe.”

“With a keeper.”

“With a keeper.”

“But you don’t have it.”

“No,” said Yarrow. “That’s the arrangement. That’s what it costs.”

I stayed there outside her door and did what I do, which is take a thing apart to see how it works, and I did not like the shape of any of the pieces.

“Can you get it back?”

“I could ask.”

“Then...”

“I don’t want it back,” said Yarrow, and put down the bowl, and picked up a different bowl.

## Chapter 4: Right. Well. I Can Fix That.

I did not believe it.

No. I did not think she was lying. I thought she meant something else. I thought she meant *it would be awkward to ask*, or *the keeper’s got it now and it isn’t fair to make him give it up*, or *I’ve decided not to be the sort of person who takes things back*. Those are all reasonable things for somebody to mean, and I put them in her mouth one after another without noticing I was doing it, because they were the only shapes I had.

The shape I did not have was: **I am choosing, today, to keep not having it.**

So I asked who had it, and she told me, and I went and found him, because I cannot leave a thing like that alone.

---

His name was Ess and he had one room off a courtyard above the trade quarter, and the courtyard had a bench and a small dead tree in a pot and nothing else in it at all.

He was about halfway through his life and entirely plain and he did not get up.

“You’re the floating thing.”

Everybody in that town opened with that.

“Yarrow gave you a memory.”

“She did.”

“The day she and her sister made it up.”

“Yes.”

I waited for the rest of it. There is always a rest of it. People explain themselves without being asked. It is the most reliable thing about people on any world I have been to.

Ess drank something out of a cup and looked at the dead tree.

“Will you give it back?”

“No,” said Ess.

That was the whole answer. He did not say *no, because*. He did not say *no, I’m sorry*. He put the cup down and waited to see whether I had anything else, and when it became clear that I did not, he went back into his room and shut the door, politely, without any particular feeling, the way you would close a door.

I did not like him.

---

So that was my first day on Arrit.

An old woman with a bowl in her lap who had given away the best day of her life on purpose, and was going to spend the rest of it not having it.

A man in a bare courtyard who was holding it, who would not give it back, and who did not think he owed anybody a reason.

Every one of those facts is correct.

I floated back down through the terraces in the evening with the light going gold off the water and the boats coming in, and I thought: *right. Well. I can fix that.*

## Chapter 5: The Matcher

To fix it I needed to know what it was worth, and to know what it was worth I needed to understand the market, so I went down to the trade quarter the next morning and attached myself to the first person who would talk to me.

Her name was Tam. She was a matcher.

“You want to know what things go for.”

“Yes.”

“Everybody wants to know what things go for,” said Tam, walking, not slowing down for me. “Nobody’s ever satisfied with the answer. Come on.”

---

A matcher finds where a memory ought to go.

That is the whole trade, and it is more of a trade than it sounds. Somebody has a thing they want to be rid of, or a thing they want kept, and somebody else somewhere in Arrit wants exactly that and does not know it exists. Tam knew both of them. Tam knew everybody. Tam was paid when the two ends met and not before, which is why Tam walked everywhere at speed and talked the entire time.

By the middle of the morning I had seen:

A woman buy the memory of a particular street in a town four days up the coast, because she had grown up there and could not go back and wanted to walk down it again. The seller was a sailor who had been there once and thought nothing of it.

A man give away being ill. Not the illness. He still had that. The three weeks of it. He said he had come out the other side and did not need to keep the middle, and the person taking it was a physician who said it was useful, and both of them treated this as unremarkable. Afterward the man knew he had been ill for three weeks the way you know a thing somebody told you about, and said it was a great improvement.

A boy of about ten hand over a fight he had lost, and be very pleased about it, and be told by his mother in the street afterward that he would only go and have another one.

“That’s the trade,” said Tam. “That’s all of it. Everything else is haggling.”

## Chapter 6: No Fourth Attempt

I asked what the prices were.

“For which?”

“For any of them. There must be a...” I did not have their word for it. “A way of working it out.”

Tam stopped in the middle of the street, which she had not done all morning, and looked at me with real interest for the first time.

“Say that again.”

“A way of working out what a memory is worth.”

“Go on then,” said Tam. “You do it. I’ll watch.”

---

I tried.

I tried properly, because I am good at this. I have measured a river running at two speeds and a road that ran off the end of a world. This is the thing I know how to do.

**First attempt: how long it lasts.** An afternoon is worth more than a minute. This collapsed within the hour, when I watched a woman pay a great deal for four seconds of somebody’s dead husband saying something ordinary in a doorway.

**Second attempt: how good it was.** Happy costs more. This collapsed immediately, because people bought difficult things on purpose: a physician wanting three weeks of illness, a woman buying an argument she had missed so she would understand what her son had meant.

**Third attempt: how rare it is.** This lasted nearly a day. Then Tam sold four dull walks along the same beach, four different afternoons, near enough identical, and every one went for a different price. One went for more than anything else I had seen.

“Why?” I said.

“He grew up on that beach.”

“But the other three...”

“Didn’t,” said Tam.

**Fourth attempt.** There is no fourth attempt. I stopped.

## Chapter 7: Whatever the Two of Them Say

Here is what a memory is worth on Arrit.

**Whatever the two of them say it is.**

That is not a rule. I have tried it from several directions and it will not become one. The same memory is worth a fortune to one person and nothing at all to the person standing next to them, and both of those are correct at once, and the number that gets agreed in the middle is not a fact about the memory. It is a fact about the two people.

I found this genuinely unbearable for about two days.

---

Then I watched an argument.

It took place across a table outside a cloth-seller’s and lasted most of an afternoon, and it was between a woman who had a memory of a boat race and a man who wanted it and neither of them would move.

“Six.”

“Nine.”

“It’s a boat race.”

“You were at the race,” said the woman. “You’ve got the race. What you haven’t got is being on the winning boat, and that’s what I’ve got, and that’s nine.”

“Seven.”

“Nine.”

“You’ll not get nine from anybody else.”

“Then I’ll keep it,” she said comfortably, “and be on the winning boat.”

They went round for another hour. Tam sat down nearby to watch, the way you would watch a game. Other people arrived. Somebody brought out chairs. At one point the man went away entirely and came back twenty minutes later and said “eight,” and the woman said “nine,” and everybody made a noise.

She got nine. He was delighted. She was delighted. They had both plainly enjoyed the afternoon more than either of them would enjoy the boat race, and I said so to Tam, and Tam said that was the first sensible thing I had said all week.

## Chapter 8: Three True Things

It was Tam who noticed I had nothing.

We were outside at the end of a long day. Tam was sitting; I was where I usually am. of watching people trade parts of their lives for other parts of other people’s, and Tam said, out of nowhere:

“You’ve got none of this, have you.”

“None of what?”

“Any of it. You’ve not given me one thing all week that anybody could take.” She was not being unkind. She was working, the way she was always working. “Where were you before this?”

“Somewhere else.”

“Doing what?”

“I don’t know how to describe it.”

Tam thought about that for a moment.

“You’d be no use to me at all,” she said, and finished her drink, and got up, and went off to find somebody who was.

---

It stung slightly. Then a boat went past with somebody shouting on it and I stopped thinking about it.

---

By the end of that week I understood the trade completely.

I understood what memories were used for. People gave them away for money, for debts, for kindness, for medicine, and because they were dying and wanted something kept.

For teaching, sometimes, and that one is expensive and does less than you would think. A rope-maker’s apprentice bought one afternoon of the hardest splice done correctly. Not the knowing how, which nobody can hand over, but the once from inside, so she would know what it was supposed to feel like in the hands. She still could not do it. She said it helped. She said it would take her another year.

And I understood that keepers held things for other people, and that this was a respectable job with a reputation attached to it.

And I understood that there was no rule for what any of it was worth, and that I was going to have to work out what Yarrow’s was worth without one.

Because that was still what I was doing. I spent the week delighted by the market, and underneath it I was working on the same problem I had started with on the first afternoon.

**She has a hole in her.**

**He has the thing that fits it.**

**Memories here move from one person to another.**

Three true things.

## Chapter 9: A Number in My Head

I went back to the courtyard with a number in my head.

That was the plan. I had spent a week learning that everything on Arrit has a price, and Ess was a professional, and professionals can be paid. I did not have anything to pay him with, but I had Tam, and Tam could find anything, and if I could establish what the memory was worth then I could work out what would have to be assembled.

It was a good plan.

---

The courtyard was the same. Bench, dead tree in a pot, one door. Ess was sitting outside with a cup, doing nothing, in the way of somebody whose work does not look like anything.

“What’s it worth?”

“What’s what worth?”

“Yarrow’s.”

Ess looked at me for a moment.

“Who’s asking?”

“I am.”

“Then nothing,” he said. “You haven’t got anything.”

Which was true, and which Tam had told me four days earlier in a friendlier voice, and which I did not enjoy hearing twice.

---

I tried it a different way.

I asked him what a keeper does, and he told me, and it took about a minute, because there is not much to it if you describe it properly.

Somebody comes to you with a thing they want kept. You agree what it is and what it will cost and how long you are keeping it, and then they give it to you, and then you have it. That is all. There is no vault and no ledger and nothing to look at. The room is a room. The thing is in your head, with everything else that is in your head, and the whole of the trade is that people believe you will still have it in thirty years and will not have told anybody about it in the meantime.

“So what do people pay for?”

“Not losing it.”

“That’s not a service. That’s you doing nothing.”

“Yes,” said Ess.

## Chapter 10: Half of Them

Two people came to the courtyard while I was there, and both of them told me more about the trade than Ess did.

The first was a woman who wanted to know whether he still had something. That is a thing keepers get and it is not an insult. She was old and she had put something with him eleven years ago and she wanted to hear him say it was there. He said it was there. She asked him to say the name of it. He said the name of it, which was a person’s name and a place, four words, nothing else.

She said thank you and went away and did not stay four minutes.

The second was a man who wanted to leave something and could not decide, and who talked for a long while about the thing, all round the outside of it, without once saying what it was. Ess let him. Ess did not help him get to it and did not hurry him and did not look at anything else while it was happening, and eventually the man said he would come back next week, and Ess nodded as if next week were no different from today.

“Does that happen a lot?”

“Half of them.”

“Then why sit through it?”

“Because the other half do it,” said Ess.

---

He let me into the room eventually, and it was not what I expected.

One room. A bed, a table, two chairs, a shelf with about nine things on it, a window that looked at another wall. It was not bare in a sad way. It was bare in the way of somebody who has thought about how much furniture a person needs and arrived at an answer.

“You’ve got other people’s memories in here.”

“I’ve got them in me. There’s nothing in here.”

“How many?”

Ess put the cup down.

“You’re new,” he said, “so I’ll tell you once. You don’t ask that.”

## Chapter 11: It Isn’t Yours

I learned the rest of the courtesy over the following hour, all of it the same way, which is by walking into it.

“What sort of things, though? Generally.”

Ess looked at the window.

“Are they mostly old people? I’d have thought...”

He picked a bit of something off the table.

“Do you know who they all are, or do some of them not...”

And there it was again: not a refusal, not a *no*, just a small silence with a change of subject arriving directly behind it, and then Ess asking whether I had been down to see the boats come in.

Four separate times. Everybody in Arrit knows what that silence means. It took me until the fourth one.

Then I asked whether I could at least know how many there were, and he said, “You’ve done that one,” which is the closest Ess ever comes to a joke.

---

What I worked out, eventually and without help, is that this is not manners. It is the whole trade.

If keepers talked, nobody would use one. And then a great many things that people wanted kept would go into the ground with them.

---

“Why won’t you tell me what’s in it?”

“It isn’t yours.”

“I’m not going to *do* anything with it. I want to know what it is so I know what it’s worth.”

“It isn’t yours,” said Ess.

---

Then I made the argument I had come to make, which I had been assembling all week and which I thought was rather good.

I said that Yarrow had given him the best day of her life. I said she was going without it. I said she went about with a hole in her where it had been, and that I had watched her put a hand flat on her own doorframe one afternoon and stand there for a moment as if she were checking the wall was still there, and that I did not think a person should have to do that.

I said: she seems unhappy without it.

And Ess said:

“I know.”

## Chapter 12: That’s Not Mine

That is the whole of it. He did not look away when he said it. He was not embarrassed and he was not defending himself, and there was nothing in his face that I could use.

He knew. He had known before I told him, and he had presumably known for a year, and he was sitting in a bare courtyard with the thing that would fix it, and he was going to go on sitting there.

It was the coldest thing anybody had ever said to me.

---

I went at him properly then.

I asked whether he would still refuse if she asked. He said that was between him and her. I asked whether she had asked. He said that was between him and her. I asked what he would do if she died without it and he said, in exactly the same voice as everything else:

“Keep it.”

“Forever?”

“Until somebody else has to.”

I said that was a terrible answer and he agreed that it probably was, and finished his cup, and did not move.

---

Somebody in the next courtyard was cooking. There was smoke and oil and something green in it, and the smell came over the wall the way it does in a town built in steps.

And Ess stopped.

Not dramatically. He was halfway through standing up and he stopped, with one hand still on the table, and his face did something I could not read at all. Not sad, not pleased. More like somebody listening hard to a sound in another room. It lasted about two seconds.

Then he finished standing up and said, “That’s not mine,” to nobody, in the tone of a man correcting a small clerical error, and went inside.

I did not know what he meant. I assumed he was talking about the food.

---

I went back down the hill in the dark.

I had the two things I had come for, in a sense. I knew what a keeper does, and I knew he would not give it back on my say-so.

And I knew one more thing, which was that he understood exactly what it was costing her and had decided that this did not signify, and I turned that over the whole way down and it did not get better.

**I was right not to like him.**

Every single thing I had was correct.

## Chapter 13: The Shape of It

I went back up to Yarrow’s the next morning with the intention of finding out what she had actually lost, so that I could price it.

That is what I went up there for. I had a professional keeper who would not name the thing, so I would get the shape of it from the other end, from the person it had come out of. It did not strike me as an unkind thing to do, because it did not strike me at all.

---

She was on her step with the same bowl or a different one.

“You’ve been at Ess.”

“Who told you?”

“You went into his courtyard four times in one afternoon,” said Yarrow. “There are eleven people in this town who saw you do it and nine of them have been past here since.”

“He wouldn’t tell me anything.”

“No,” she agreed. “That’s what he’s for.”

---

Then she told me about the thing, without my having to be clever about it, because I had entirely failed to understand that she was not hiding anything from anybody and never had been.

It goes like this.

She has a sister called Del. There was a quarrel. Yarrow said what the quarrel was about and it was almost nothing. It was about a house, and about which of them had been asked to do more for their mother at the end, and it was the sort of thing that is not really about the thing it is about.

They did not speak for nine years.

Then one afternoon Del came up the hill, and they sat outside on that step, and by the evening it was mended. Properly mended. Not politely. They have been all right ever since, and that is twenty-odd years now.

“And that’s the one you gave away.”

“That’s the one.”

## Chapter 14: I’d Do It Tomorrow

And this is the part she had not gone into on the first day.

She did not give it away in a hurry, or at the end, or in a panic. She had known for two years by then. She had been losing them steadily: an afternoon here, a whole spring there, a man she worked beside for six years whose face went and did not come back. She had watched the pattern of it for long enough to know that it does not negotiate and it does not skip anything because you are fond of it.

So she sat down and did an inventory of her own life.

That is what she called it. She went through everything she had and asked which of it she would mind losing most, and she came to an answer fairly quickly, and then she spent a season being sure of the answer, and then she went down the hill and made an arrangement with a keeper with a good name.

She was sixty-eight. She was entirely herself. She understood, because everybody on Arrit understands, that afterward she would not have it.

“And you’d do it again.”

“I’d do it tomorrow,” said Yarrow.

---

Nobody in that town found any of this remarkable.

And they were not gentle with her, which I had expected them to be. That is the thing. On the way down for the basket I passed four people who knew, and all four of them treated her exactly as they had the week before. Two of them owed her something and said so, and one wanted a pot back, and the fourth shouted a piece of gossip up the steps at her from the street below and Yarrow shouted a correction back down.

There is a word for what she has and they say it the way you would say a bad knee. Half the terrace has arrangements of one kind or another. The woman three doors along had put four things with a keeper over the years and would tell you which, at length, whether or not you had asked.

What they do not do is get quiet when she comes past.

It took me longer than it should have to understand that this was the kindness, and that the version I had been expecting, the soft voices and careful looks, would have been the opposite of it.

## Chapter 15: The One That Did Something

I asked why that one.

I expected something. I do not know what. Something about it being the happiest. It was not the happiest. She was very clear about that; she said there were four or five days she had enjoyed more, and she named one of them, which was a day at the water with somebody she did not otherwise explain.

“Then why?”

“Because it’s the one that did something,” she said. “The rest are just nice.”

---

I met Del that afternoon, at the bottom of the hill, because Yarrow sent me down with a basket and instructions and no particular explanation.

Del is younger and softer around the edges and talks more, and she looked at me with open curiosity for a good deal longer than anybody in the trade quarter had bothered to.

“You’re the one that’s been bothering Ess.”

“Everybody knows that.”

“Everybody knows everything,” said Del. “It’s a town.”

She took the basket. She asked after Yarrow in a particular way. Not *how is she*, but a list, four or five specific questions about specific things, the way you ask when you have been keeping an account of somebody for a while.

Then, when I said I had been to see the keeper, Del’s face did something quick that she put away again immediately.

“Did he say anything about it?”

“No.”

“No,” said Del. “He wouldn’t.”

And that was all she said about it, and she was cheerful for the rest of the conversation, and I noticed that she had not asked me a single question about what was in it.

---

I went back up the hill in the afternoon and Yarrow was still on the step and I asked her the question I had been carrying since the first day.

“Do you ever think about getting it back?”

“No.”

“But if...”

“I don’t want it back,” said Yarrow. “I’ve told you that.”

“You have.”

“So stop asking me in different shapes,” she said, “because I can hear you doing it.”

## Chapter 16: What Ess Wanted

And I decided she was being brave.

That is the whole of it. That is what I decided. I decided that she had made a hard choice and was living with it in the way that people who have made hard choices do, by saying firmly that they are fine, and that underneath the firmness was a woman on a step with a hole in her, and that the kind thing, the *correct* thing, the thing anybody looking at this from outside would do, was to find a way to give it back to her regardless of what she said about it.

She had told me twice, in plain words, in her own voice, that she did not want it.

I had learned by then that *I don’t want it* ought to be an answer. I told myself this was different, because there was a thing missing from her and I knew exactly where it was.

And I had heard, both times, *she has decided to want nothing.*

There is a difference between those two things. I could not see it.

---

What I did next was make a plan, and the plan was good.

I could not pay Ess. I had established that. But Ess was a professional with a reputation and a price, and Arrit ran on people wanting things that other people had, and I had spent a week with the best matcher in the trade quarter learning exactly how that worked.

I did not need to have anything.

**I needed to find out what Ess wanted.**

Everybody wants something. That is the whole of the trade quarter. Find the thing, find who has it, put the two ends together. Tam did that eleven times a week for a living and would tell me how for nothing, because Tam liked the sound of her own voice better than she liked being paid.

I went to sleep that night, insofar as I do that, extremely pleased with myself.

## Chapter 17: Nobody Can Stand for It to Stop

I found Tam in the middle of a family argument, which is where Tam mostly was.

There were six of them out in the street below the cloth-seller’s. Three generations were shouting, with the smallest one sitting on a step watching the way you watch weather. The argument was about a great-grandfather.

Not about him, exactly. About a memory of him. It had been in the family since before any of them, handed down the way a bad chair gets handed down, and it was two hours of the old man walking somewhere along a road and talking about drainage.

“It’s *history*,” said the eldest.

“It’s drainage,” said her son.

“He was your great-grandfather.”

“He was *walking*. For two hours. About *ditches*.”

“Somebody has to have him.”

“Then let Merrit have him. Merrit’s got the room.”

“Merrit’s had him,” said Merrit, from the doorway, with enormous feeling. “Merrit had him for nine years.”

---

It went on another half hour and got worse. At one point the middle daughter offered to take the great-grandfather if somebody else would take a winter she had had, which everybody agreed was not the same thing at all, and the small one on the step said into a gap, “Nobody has to have him,” and was told that this was not how families work.

Nobody took him. Everybody agreed to discuss it again at some unspecified point, and the great-grandfather stayed where he was, in the eldest, going on about ditches.

“That’s the fourth time this year,” said Tam.

“Will anybody ever take it?”

“Somebody will when she’s dying,” said Tam, cheerfully. “That’s how it always goes. Nobody wants it and then nobody can stand for it to stop.”

---

Then I asked Tam my question, which I had been building for two days, and Tam demolished it in about ten words.

I asked what somebody would have to give Ess to make him hand back a memory he was keeping.

“Nothing,” said Tam. “He’s not selling it. It’s not his to sell.”

“Then how?”

“She asks for it back and he gives it to her.” Tam was looking at something over my head, already half onto the next thing. “It’s a transfer. It’s the same as any other transfer. She says the words and he says the words and then she’s got it.”

“That’s all?”

“What did you think it was?”

## Chapter 18: A Wall You’ve Been Told Is There

That was the best news I had ever had.

There was no vault. There was no price. I had spent a week and a half constructing a problem out of a keeper who would not talk to me, and the actual mechanism was two people agreeing out loud in a courtyard. It would take about as long as it takes to tell somebody the way to a place.

**She asks. He gives it back. She has it.**

It was, in fact, easier than buying a boat race.

---

So then there was only one thing left to do, which was to find out why she would not ask.

I went up the hill.

---

It came out sideways, the way the true things do.

Del had been up that morning. There had been some business about a chair that needed carrying, and it had gone badly, and by the time I arrived Yarrow was working through a bowl with more force than the bowl required.

“She thinks I’m angry with her.”

“Are you?”

“No.” A pause of about four seconds. “I don’t think so. No.”

“You don’t sound certain.”

“I’m certain,” said Yarrow. “That’s the trouble.”

---

I did not understand that, and I said so, and she put the bowl down.

“I know we made it up,” she said. “I know it the way I know my mother’s name. I know we sat on that step and it took the whole afternoon and when she went down the hill it was mended. I can tell you all of it. I’ve told you all of it.”

“Yes.”

“I can’t remember *changing my mind*.”

---

She watched me not get it, and then came at it from another side, because she is patient in a way I have not earned.

“There were nine years,” she said. “Nine years of being right about Del. I had it all worked out: what she’d done, what she hadn’t, what she was like. I carried that about every day and it was solid as a step.”

“And then?”

“And then one afternoon it wasn’t,” said Yarrow. “And I’ve no idea how.”

---

She has the fact, and nothing has taken that away from her.

What she has not got is **the afternoon that did it**. Whatever was said on that step, whatever moved in her while it was happening. That is in a bare room above the trade quarter with a dead tree outside it.

And underneath the fact, still in excellent condition, is the nine years.

## Chapter 19: The Last Piece

“It comes up,” she said, when I asked what that was actually like. “Not often. She’ll do something. She’ll be late, or she’ll not be late, or she’ll look at me a certain way, and the old thing gets up on its feet.”

“And then?”

“And then I tell it to sit down, because I know we settled this.”

“But...”

“I know we settled it,” said Yarrow. “I just can’t remember it being settled. There’s a difference. It’s not a big difference. It’s the difference between a wall and a wall you’ve been told is there.”

---

Nobody could have known that, including her, because you cannot look inside a thing before you hand it over. You know its name. You know what it is *of*. Nothing tells you what else was quietly sitting on top of it.

She had picked it because it was the one that *did something*.

She had been more right about that than she knew.

---

I went down the hill that evening to find Del, because I had worked out what I needed and Del was the last piece of it.

She was easy to find. She was cross about the chair.

“It’s a perfectly good chair.”

“I’m sure it is.”

“She doesn’t want it because I brought it,” said Del, and then heard herself, and stopped, and laughed at herself in a tired way. “That’s not true. She hasn’t got room for it. I know she hasn’t got room for it.”

---

I told her what I had learned. All of it: the fact without the afternoon under it, the nine years still sitting there in good condition, the wall Yarrow has been told is there.

Del listened to the whole thing without interrupting once, which I had not expected, and when I finished she was quiet for long enough that I thought she had not understood.

Then she said: “I know.”

“You know?”

“I’ve known for about eight months.” Del was looking down the street at nothing. “She’s careful with me. She never used to be careful with me. She’s careful the way you’re careful with somebody you’ve had a row with, and we haven’t had a row, and I’ve been trying to work out what I did.”

“You didn’t do anything.”

“No,” said Del. “She just hasn’t got the afternoon.”

## Chapter 20: Somebody Was Going to Have To

So I told her the good part. I told her that it was mechanically simple and that there was no price and no vault and no arrangement. Yarrow had only to walk into that courtyard and ask, and Ess would give it back, and the whole thing would be over by supper.

And Del said no.

---

She said it immediately. She did not think about it. She said it the way you put your hand out to stop something being knocked off a table.

“No. Don’t.”

“Del.”

“You think I haven’t wanted that?” She turned round and looked at me properly. “I’ve wanted it since the day she told me she’d done it. I went down there myself. I got as far as his door.”

“And?”

“And I didn’t knock.”

“Why not?”

“Because I stood there and worked out what I was asking for,” said Del.

“Which was what?”

Del looked at me for a moment. Then she looked away down the street, at the boats, and folded her arms, and did not answer.

“Ask Yarrow,” she said.

---

“That’s not an answer.”

“No,” Del agreed.

“You’ve both got a reason and neither of you will say it.”

“She’ll say it,” said Del. “She’s better at it than I am. I get about four words in and then I have to stop.”

Then she picked up her end of the chair and asked whether I was going to make myself useful, which I could not, on account of having no arms.

---

I went back up the hill in the dark. Here is where I had got to.

I knew what she had lost. I knew it was worse than anybody had told me. Not a nice day gone, but the thing that had been holding up the way she felt about her only sister.

I knew that getting it back was simple. Not difficult. Not expensive. Simple.

I knew that Yarrow would not ask, and that Del would not ask her to, and that both of them had a reason for it that neither would give me.

**And I concluded, from all of that, that somebody was going to have to.**

## Chapter 21: The Wedding

On the morning I went up to do it, the whole trade quarter was blocked by a wedding memory.

Not a wedding. A memory of one. An old woman named Sabb was giving her granddaughter the day she had married, sixty-odd years ago, and for reasons nobody could satisfactorily explain to me this had become an event that required forty people, three tables in the street, a quantity of food that would have fed twice that, and a small boy going up and down with a jug.

“They only have to say it,” I said to Tam. “It takes a minute.”

“It takes a minute.”

“Then why?” I looked at the tables. Somebody was arguing about where to put a bench. “Why all this?”

“Because she’s giving her a *wedding*,” said Tam, as though to a slow child, and went to get some of the food.

---

It did take a minute.

The two of them sat down in the middle of it all and the noise went out of the street the way it does, in patches, and they said what they had to say to each other, and the old woman’s face did something complicated, and then it was done.

And then everybody made a great deal of noise and the food started going round and somebody’s uncle sang.

The granddaughter sat in the middle of it with her hands flat on the table looking at nothing at all, the way you do when you have just been handed something enormous and are still finding out the shape of it.

I went and asked her what she had got, which was badly timed of me.

“A church,” she said. “It was raining. He’d got his father’s coat on and it was too big at the shoulders and I hadn’t noticed that before. I noticed it now.”

“You noticed it now?”

“She noticed it then,” said the granddaughter. “I’ve got it now.”

Then she put her hands over her face and laughed, and somebody put a plate in front of her, and I got out of the way.

## Chapter 22: That’s All True

Nothing about that was sad.

Sabb was delighted. She had wanted the girl to have it since she was born and had waited until she was old enough to hold onto it properly, and she spent the rest of the afternoon telling people what she had done, which she could still do, because she still knew she had been married in a church in the rain. She simply did not have the coat anymore.

She said it was the best thing she had ever spent, and everybody in that street agreed with her, and it went on until dark.

That is the trade working. That is what it is for.

I went up the hill afterward feeling that I had the wind behind me.

---

Yarrow was on the step. Of course she was.

I had it all arranged. I had been arranging it for four days, and as a piece of work it was entirely correct.

I said: it is simple. It is not a trade and nobody has to be paid. You walk down to the courtyard and you say the words and Ess says the words and then it is yours again, and it takes about a minute, and I have watched it done twice this week.

I said: Ess will do it. He will not do it for me and he should not, but he will do it for you, because it is yours and he has never once said otherwise.

I said: Del is not stopping you. Del has wanted it since the day you told her.

And I said the last part, which I had saved, because I thought it was the good part.

I said: **you have been carrying a wall you were told about for a year, and you do not have to.**

---

Yarrow listened to all of it.

She let me get the whole way through. She did not interrupt once, and when I finished she sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, and then she said:

“That’s all true.”

“Then...”

“Sit down,” she said, and then, “You can’t. Well. Stop moving about.”

I stopped moving about.

---

“How long have I got it?” she said.

“Got what?”

“Anything. How long does anything stay?”

I said I did not know.

“No,” said Yarrow. “Neither do I. That’s the whole of it.”

And then she said the thing, in the flat voice of somebody who has been over it many times alone and has got it down to the fewest words it will go into.

“If I take it back, I might lose it for both of us.”

## Chapter 23: The Receipt

I did not understand it for about two seconds and then I understood all of it at once.

It goes back into her, and then it is in a house that is being emptied. She might have it eleven years. She might have it a season. There is no way to know and no way to slow it and no arrangement anybody can make.

And when it goes from her it does not go anywhere. It is not misplaced. **It stops.**

Whereas at present it is in a bare room above the trade quarter, in a man of fifty with a professional reputation resting on the fact that things put in him stay in him, and who has agreed to have it for the rest of his life.

That afternoon exists. It exists once, in one place, and that place is not Yarrow.

**She did not lose it because she gave it away. She gave it away so that it would not be lost.**

---

I had spent every day since I arrived looking at a hole in somebody and working out how to fill it in.

It was not a hole. It was the receipt.

---

“You see it,” said Yarrow.

“Yes.”

“Took you long enough.”

“Yes.”

“I did tell you,” she said, without any particular heat, “about four times.”

She had. She had told me on the first afternoon, before she knew my name, and I had heard *she has decided to want nothing*, because that was a shape I had and the other one was not.

---

There was one more piece and she gave me that too, because she had decided to be thorough about it.

I asked whether Ess knew all this.

“Ess knows all of it. Ess helped me work out the words.” She was back at the bowl. “And I asked him for one thing, when I gave it to him, and it wasn’t the thing you think.”

“What was it?”

“I said: there’ll be days when I’m sad about this.” Yarrow turned something over in her fingers and put it in the bowl. “And I said: don’t you go mistaking me being sad about it for me asking for it back.”

## Chapter 24: The Wrong Question

I stayed where I was for a while.

That is what he promised. Not to keep it from her. Not to overrule her. If she went down that hill tomorrow and said *I have thought about it and I want it*, that is between the two of them and nothing I have seen suggests he would refuse her.

What he promised was not to hand it back on a bad afternoon, to a woman who was grieving the absence, in a way that would undo the one thing she had bought with it.

And a stranger with no arms had spent his whole time on that world going up and down the hill telling him she seemed unhappy without it.

**”I know.”**

He had not been cold. He had been answering the question I asked, which was the wrong question.

---

“You’ve gone quiet,” said Yarrow.

“I’ve been wrong about your keeper since the day I got here.”

“You have, rather.”

“I told him you were unhappy. I told him that to his face, as though he did not...” I stopped. “He has known that longer than I have been alive, probably.”

“About a year,” said Yarrow. “But yes.”

Then she asked me whether I was going to be any use with the bowl, and I said I was not, and she said that was becoming a theme.

---

I did not ask the next question.

There was one. There is always one. I could feel it sitting there: *but what if Ess were to*, or *has anybody ever tried*, or *is there some way of*. It had a shape and I could have started assembling it before she had finished with the bowl.

And I did not.

I stayed on that step until the light went orange, and Yarrow told me about somebody two doors down who had bought a wedding and gone on about it all afternoon, and I said that I had been there, and she said she knew, everybody knew, there had been forty people.

And the wall she has been told about stayed exactly where it was.

## Chapter 25: Most People Do

I went back to the courtyard two days later because I had got something wrong about the man in it and I did not know how to say so.

I am not good at that. I have had to do it a number of times now, on a number of worlds, and I have not improved. What I usually do is turn up and hover about until the person notices and then say something adjacent to the thing I mean.

That is what I did. I turned up. Ess was outside with a cup, doing nothing, in the way of somebody whose work does not look like anything.

He saw me and did not say *you again*, which he would have been entitled to.

---

“I’ve been up at Yarrow’s.”

“I know.”

“She told me about the arrangement. The whole of it.”

“I know that too,” said Ess. “She came down and told me she’d told you.”

Then he drank some of whatever was in the cup and looked at the dead tree, and let me get there on my own, which is a thing he does and which I had previously understood as him being unhelpful.

“I’ve been coming here all this time telling you she was unhappy.”

“You have.”

“You already knew.”

“Yes.”

“I thought...” I stopped, because I was about to explain to a man what I had thought of him, which is a gift nobody wants. “I had it the wrong way round.”

Ess considered that.

“Most people do,” he said. “It’s not an interesting job from outside.”

## Chapter 26: Housekeeping Nobody Warns You About

So I asked him what it was like from inside, which is a question I had not once thought to ask in all the times I had come to that courtyard.

He did not answer for a while.

“Yarrow’s is not the difficult sort,” he said eventually. “It’s a good afternoon. It sits quietly. Some of them do.”

“And the others?”

“The others are work.”

---

He told me in the flat way he tells things, with a great deal left out.

Other people’s memories do not sit in a separate place, because there is no separate place. They are in with everything else. So there are smells he knows that he has never smelled, and a stretch of road up north he could walk in the dark and has never been on, and a woman’s voice he would know anywhere whose name he does not have, because the name was not in the piece he was given.

Most of the time this is nothing. It is like having a lot of books.

Then something comes in from outside, cooking over a wall, a particular light, a boat being dragged up, and takes hold of the wrong thing, and for a moment he has a feeling with no reason attached to it.

“And then you work out whose it is.”

“Then I work out whose it is,” said Ess. “Takes a second. It’s not distressing. It’s housekeeping nobody warns you about.”

I said that on the day I first came here, somebody was cooking over the wall and he had stopped and said *that’s not mine.*

“That happens most days,” said Ess. “That one’s a kitchen. I’ve never been in it.”

---

I asked how many he was carrying and then apologized before he could answer, because I had finally learned that much.

He said it was all right and did not tell me.

Then he said, in the same voice: “Some of them nobody wants.”

## Chapter 27: For the Unanswered

That is how it came out. There was no moment where anybody decided anything.

I asked what he meant.

He said that a keeper’s ordinary work is a thing somebody has put with him for a reason. They want it kept, or they cannot carry it, or they are dying and it is going somewhere. There is a person at both ends. That is the job.

And then there are the old ones.

“Old how?”

“Old,” said Ess. “Passed on. Somebody had it and got past keeping it and gave it to their keeper, and that one got past it and gave it to theirs, and on and on.” He turned the cup around on the table. “There’s one I’ve had eleven years that nobody has ever wanted, and I couldn’t give it back if I tried, because whoever it belonged to has been dead longer than this town has been here.”

“Then why have it?”

“Because it was mine to take when it came round,” he said. “And because somebody has to be carrying it. When I’m done, if nobody takes it off me, it stops there. That’s all it is. It’s not valuable. It’s just the only one there is.”

---

I asked what was in it and he told me, which surprised me, until I understood that this was the one he was allowed to talk about, because there was nobody left for it to belong to.

It takes about fifteen seconds. He said it in less time than that.

Somebody is standing close to a thing that is being built.

Not a ruin. Not something old. Something under construction, with the work going on around it. A frame is set up, and inside the frame is the beginning of an opening, which he described as *a place where there stops being anything,* and then said that was not right and did not try to improve it.

There are people working. He does not know how many. Whoever the memory belonged to is not doing the work; they are standing to one side, close enough to see.

Somebody near the frame is putting a mark onto part of it. A circle with a dot inside it.

And a voice off to the side says three words, quite ordinarily, the way you would say *that’s the last of them.*

**”For the unanswered.”**

Then the memory stops.

## Chapter 28: Three Things Are One Thing

“That’s it,” said Ess. “Eleven years. There’s nothing else. It doesn’t go anywhere and it doesn’t explain itself and I have said that sentence to myself several thousand times and it means nothing at all.”

“Nothing?”

“It’s like a tune you can’t get rid of.” He drank the last of the cup. “*For the unanswered.* For the unanswered what? There isn’t one. It’s three words and half a mark and somebody’s shoulder in the way.”

---

I did not say anything.

Here is what I had, which was not very much.

I have a mark: a circle with a dot inside it, cut into stone.

I have a word: **unanswered**.

And I have a cut at the western end of a world, square across a road somebody had meant to continue, with an edge that made me start looking properly at the crossings.

Three things I had never once seen together. I learned them separately, on different worlds, and carried them separately, the way you carry three keys and do not think about it.

**They were in the same room.**

The memory gave me the part I had not been able to prove on the Reach. Somebody was building a crossing. Somebody put the circle-and-dot mark on it. And somebody near enough to be heard said, *for the unanswered.*

---

I did not know what that meant.

I did not know who was building. I did not know what an unanswered was, or whether it was a thing or a job or a kind of creature. I did not know why anybody would put that mark on a crossing, or whether any of it had anything to do with me.

What I knew was that they were not three things.

That is all I got. It is not an answer. It is finding out that three questions you have been asking separately are one question, which feels enormous and provides nothing.

---

“You’ve gone quiet again,” said Ess.

“Yes.”

“Does it mean something?”

And I said, “I don’t know what it means.”

Which was true.

“No,” said Ess. “Nor me.”

He got up and took the cup inside and came back out without it.

“That’s the trouble with the old ones,” he said. “You end up carrying somebody’s afternoon and you’ve no idea why they thought it was worth keeping.”

Then he asked whether I wanted to know about the road going north, which he could describe in the dark and had never been on, and I said yes, and he told me about it, and it took a while, and neither of us said anything else about the other thing at all.

## Chapter 29: What She Was Doing

The thing that finally got done was not my idea, which is the best that can be said for it.

It was Del’s. She came up the hill four days after all that, on an ordinary morning, and stood in the street outside her sister’s house and said, without any preamble at all:

“I want to ask him something.”

---

What she wanted was not the memory.

She had thought about it, she said, on and off for eight months and hard for the last four days, and she had got to a question she could put to a keeper without asking him to break anything.

She did not want it given back. She did not want it described to her in full, or performed, or made into a story.

She wanted **one thing out of it that she had not known at the time.**

“Because I was there,” she said. “I was there the whole afternoon and I know what I said and I know what she said. What I don’t know is what she was doing that I didn’t see.”

---

Yarrow said yes.

She said it fast enough that I understood she had thought about it too, at some point, on her own, and had put it away as the sort of thing you do not ask for.

That yes mattered. A keeper could not give Del anything from Yarrow’s memory without Yarrow allowing it.

So the three of us went down to the courtyard, and Del asked, and Ess sat with it for a while.

Then he said: “I can tell you a thing that happened. I’m not going to tell you what it meant. I don’t know what it meant. I wasn’t there.”

“That’s all I want.”

“All right,” said Ess.

---

Here is what he told her.

“When you got up to go, she asked whether you were coming back tomorrow. You said yes. She asked again at the door.”

## Chapter 30: She Asked Twice

Del did not say anything for a long moment.

“She asked twice,” she said.

“She asked twice.”

“I don’t remember that.”

“No,” said Ess. “You were leaving. People don’t.”

---

I did not understand it and I had the sense not to say so.

Del stood in that courtyard next to the dead tree with her arms folded, doing something with her face, and Yarrow watched her do it and did not help, and after a while Del said:

“Nine years.”

“I know.”

“You sat there all afternoon and got to the end of nine years and then you *still* thought I might not come back.”

“Apparently,” said Yarrow.

“You never said.”

“I wouldn’t have, would I,” said Yarrow. “I’d only just stopped being angry. I wasn’t going to start being frightened out loud on the same afternoon.”

---

That is the whole of what happened in that courtyard.

Nothing was returned. Nothing moved from anybody into anybody else. Ess told her one small thing about a door, and the memory stayed exactly where it has been for a year, in a man of fifty with a good reputation, and it will stay there.

What changed was in Del.

She had spent eight months watching her sister be careful with her, a little formal and a little deliberate, the way you are with somebody you have quarrelled with. She had been quietly working out what she had done wrong.

And what she had been looking at was a woman being careful in exactly the way she had been careful at that door, twenty-odd years ago, with the forgiving already done and the fear still there.

The fear was never inside the memory. It had been sitting outside it the whole time.

---

They did not have a conversation about that.

They walked back up the hill together arguing about the chair, which was still in Del’s house and which Yarrow still had no room for, and by the top of the terraces they had progressed to the shutter.

## Chapter 31: The Shutter

The shutter on Yarrow’s front window had been crooked since the spring.

I had noticed it and thought nothing of it. Everybody on that terrace had noticed it. Del had apparently been mentioning it, at intervals, for four months, in the way that a person mentions a thing they are not going to be allowed to fix.

“It’s the hinge.”

“It isn’t the hinge.”

“It’s plainly the hinge, Yarrow.”

“It’s the frame. The frame’s gone out. You can put a new hinge on a bent frame all day.”

They got the shutter off. This took some time and involved a knife and a certain amount of language. Then they got it back on, and stood back, and it was crooked the other way.

“That’s worse.”

“That’s not worse.”

“That is measurably worse than it was this morning.”

“That’s because *you* had the bottom.”

“I had the bottom because you can’t get down there,” said Del.

They took it off again.

---

I stayed until the light went.

They got it right on the third try, or right enough, which took most of the afternoon and produced a shutter that closes properly and does not sit quite square in the frame. Somebody coming up that street would look at it and think: *that has been mended by two people who did not entirely agree.*

While they were arguing about it, a woman two doors down came out to say that her husband had done that same shutter in nineteen minutes six years ago, and was told to go inside.

Neither of them said anything about the courtyard. Neither of them said anything about the afternoon on the step, or the nine years, or the door. Del said the frame was gone and Yarrow said she knew the frame was gone and had said so at the outset, and Del said that was not the same as knowing it, and Yarrow said it was near enough.

That is what it looked like. Two people up a terrace with a shutter, in the last of the light, getting a thing slightly wrong together.

It is a shutter, and they did it now.

## Chapter 32: The Whole Way

I left the next morning.

Yarrow was on the step, because she is always on the step, and I said I was going and she said she knew, everybody knew, Tam had been telling people for two days.

I asked whether there was anything she wanted from wherever I was going next.

“You don’t know where you’re going next.”

“No.”

“Then no,” said Yarrow, and then, after a moment: “Get on, then.”

---

I went down past the courtyard on the way to the water.

Ess was outside with a cup, doing nothing, in the way of somebody whose work does not look like anything.

“You’re going.”

“Yes.”

“All right,” said Ess.

He did not get up, and he said nothing about the old memory or the road going north or any of the rest of it. A little way down the street I looked back, and he was still at the table with the cup, exactly as he had been on the first afternoon.

---

The crossing was down past the boats, out where the water goes dark.

I went slowly.

I got level with the edge of it and looked along the length of it the way you would look along a table to see whether it is straight.

The edge was there. I looked for the impossible zero again, the place with no thickness in any direction.

I could not swear to it. Same as before.

But I no longer needed the edge to prove what I knew.

Somebody made crossings.

I have three things and they are one thing. That is all that changed on Arrit.

I do not know what the thing is. I do not know what an unanswered is, or why my... why *the* mark is on the work, or who was standing close enough to say three words while somebody built this.

I know there was a morning, somewhere, with people working, and that one of them thought the job was worth marking.

Behind me the harbor was starting up: the first boats out, somebody shouting numbers, a whole town full of people carrying pieces of each other about and haggling over what they came to.

I went through.

I looked at it the whole way.
