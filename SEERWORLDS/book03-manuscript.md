## Chapter 1: What You Say

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I know more than I used to. I can move. I can look at a thing until I understand it. I know that every world thinks its own rules are just how things are, and nobody explains how things are to you, because why would they.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I arrived in a forest that was not a forest.

They were columns. Pale stone, going straight up out of a flat plain, spaced like trees and standing like trees, some of them no thicker than my arm would be if I had one, some of them wider than a house. They went up until the haze took them. There was no top that I could find.

The light came from everywhere at once. Nothing had a shadow. The whole plain was lit like the inside of a lamp.

And it hummed.

The wind came through the columns and made a low tone out of them, and the tone slid up and down as the wind changed, so that the entire world sounded like something enormous thinking about it.

I liked it immediately.

Then something ran into me.

It was about the size of a large cat and shaped like nothing I had a word for: low, broad, four short legs, a flat pale panel down its front, and skin that would not stay one color.

It was going bright orange in patches. Then green. Then orange again.

“Sorry,” it said. “Sorry. I didn’t.” It stopped. “What *are* you?”

It said this without a mouth, which took me some time to establish. It had no mouth at all. The words came off the panel on its front, in bands and blots and little rippling grains, and they arrived in me as words the way the wind arrived as sound.

“I’m the Dot.”

The creature went completely white.

Then it went every color it had.

“You TALK,” it said. “You talked. Where is it *coming* from?”

“From me.”

“You haven’t got a front!”

“I’m all front,” I said, which was the first thing I said on that world that anybody found funny.

It fell over.

## Chapter 2: Dignified

Its name was Tik, and it was young, and it could not hold a color for six consecutive breaths.

It told me that itself, immediately, with enormous frustration, while flickering like a dropped lamp.

“I got to six once,” it said. “In front of everybody. My whole family saw.”

“What happens at six?”

“Nothing *happens*. It just means you can do it.” It went a mortified pink and could not stop. “See? I’m not even feeling anything. It just does that.”

“Does everyone do that?”

“The little ones do it. Grown ones don’t. Grown ones stay still all day and only go colored when they’ve got something to say.” Tik’s whole panel dimmed with longing. “That’s the *point*. That’s what being grown is. You get to decide when you’re talking.”

I thought about that for a while.

“So when you’re not talking,” I said, “you’re just gray.”

“Gray-green.”

“And that’s good?”

“That’s *dignified*,” said Tik, going three colors at once.

Tik took me along the plain, mostly by running ahead and coming back.

We passed Kesh doing ordinary things. That was what they were called, Tik said, the same word for one of them as for all of them. They were scraping something pale off the columns with flat blades, three of them standing on each other in a tower to reach the high crust. They were carrying bundles. They were sitting.

And they were all gray-green and all still, and every so often two of them would turn to face each other and go colored, fast and fine and complicated, and then stop and go back to gray.

“They’re talking,” I said.

“Obviously.”

“To each other.”

“Well, they’re facing each other.”

“Does that matter?”

Tik stopped so suddenly that its back legs got ahead of its front ones.

“Does *facing* matter,” it repeated.

“Yes.”

“Your talking comes out of your *front*,” Tik said, in the voice you use for someone who has asked whether up is above you. “If I turn around, you can’t hear me. Watch.”

It turned around.

Its back was a plain dull gray-green, and it stayed that way, and I could tell there was a great deal happening on the other side of it because its shoulders were flickering slightly at the edges.

“I can’t hear you,” I said.

Tik turned back around, delighted.

“Nobody can,” it said. “That’s how you tell somebody to go away.”

## Chapter 3: Facing Matters

Here is the thing I did not understand for another hour.

We came to a channel, a cut in the ground with water going along it, and there were six Kesh in it, bent over, faces down, clearing grit with their blades.

They were all going colored like anything.

“What are they doing?”

“Talking,” said Tik. “That’s the channel crew. They talk the whole time.”

I moved closer and looked down and saw why. The crew worked facing each other across the cut, with their fronts angled down into it. From up on the bank you saw six backs and nothing else.

They could all read each other perfectly.

Nobody else could read any of them.

“That’s the only place on the whole plain where you can say something without everybody hearing,” Tik said. “That’s why they’re all liars.”

“Are they?”

“No,” said Tik. “It’s a joke. Everybody says it. They love it.”

One of them looked up, saw me, said something extremely fast to the others, and all six of them stopped working at once to stare.

---

The water in the channel was very low. I could see the grit banked up in it and the way it barely moved.

“The water’s low,” I said.

Every color went out of Tik at once.

I have seen a lot of alarming things on a lot of worlds and I had never seen anything go that white that fast.

“Don’t *say* that.”

“Why not?”

“You can’t just *say* a thing.” Tik was flickering so badly it could hardly get a word out. “Like that. Flat. With nothing on it.”

“It’s true.”

“You don’t KNOW that!”

“I can see it.”

“You can see it from *here*, right *now*, with whatever you’ve got instead of eyes!” Tik was almost shouting, which on Ashet means the colors get big and simple. “That’s not the same as it *being* true! What if there’s water coming down from the shallows and it’s high by evening? What if you’re looking at the wrong bit? What if you’re WRONG?”

“Then I’d be wrong.”

“Then how does anybody know you didn’t know?”

I stopped.

That was a better question than I had been expecting, and I did not have anything to put next to it.

“Say it properly,” Tik said. “Go on.”

“How?”

“*It looks low to me. From here. At the moment.*”

“That’s four times as long.”

“Yes,” said Tik. “That’s what it’s *for*.”

## Chapter 4: The Hedge

I got the rest of it in pieces over the afternoon, and none of it was explained to me, because nobody explains the floor.

Here is what I put together.

If a Kesh says a thing they know is false, deliberately, in front of somebody, about some particular thing, the world may make it so.

Everybody knows a story. The eastern water is bitter and it is bitter because somebody said it was, to keep other families off it. You can walk out there and taste it.

Nobody could tell me how often it happens. I asked four of them that first afternoon and got four different shapes of the same answer, which was that it happens, and that you do not find out by trying.

So nobody lies.

And because nobody can see inside anybody’s head, nobody can ever prove afterward whether you *knew*. Which means the safest thing to do, every time you open your front, is to say exactly how much you actually know, out loud, in front of witnesses, so that if you turn out to be wrong there is no argument about what kind of wrong you were.

That is the hedge. Everyone does it. Everyone does it all day.

*It looks low to me, from here.*

*That seems well made, to my taste, at this moment.*

*I believe I saw him go east, though I may have the day wrong.*

It is exhausting and it is enormous and it is completely reasonable, and after a while I stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing wind.

## Chapter 5: Can You Lie?

Late in the day Tik took me to a flat open circle where there were no columns at all.

“That’s the speaking ground,” it said, and would not go into it, and would not say why except that you do not go in there unless you are saying something.

There was one Kesh in it.

She was larger than the others and her base color was a warm gray-brown, and she was standing perfectly still in the middle of the open with everybody in sight of her, which I would learn later was the loudest thing a Kesh can do.

Kesh went past on the far side. Every one of them looked at her, and then made a point of not looking, and went on.

“Who’s that?”

“That’s Ilva,” Tik said. It had gone dim and careful. “Don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“Just don’t.”

So I went over.

She watched me come the entire way, which nobody else had done. Everybody else on that plain had looked at me the way you look at weather.

I stopped in front of her.

“You’re the thing that talks,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Out of nothing.”

“Apparently.”

“Can you lie?”

Nobody had asked me that on any world, and I did not know the answer, and I said so.

Ilva went still in a way I had not seen anyone go still. Not gray. Just fixed.

“You,” she said. “Come here.”

## Chapter 6: Certain

I ate breakfast with a family the next morning, which is to say I floated beside them while they ate and asked questions until somebody threatened me with a blade.

The food was scrape. It comes off the columns in pale soft sheets, and you eat it fresh, and it is faintly sweet and slightly gritty and I am told it is much better than the dried kind, which they eat all winter and which everybody complains about constantly and eats anyway.

Under the crust there are thumblings. Small, blue-shelled, extremely slow. They are the good food. A column with a strong thumbling population is worth actual money, and the family I was eating with had four such columns and were in a dispute about the third one with a family two rows over, and had been for eleven years.

I asked whose it was.

They all started talking at once, which on Ashet means the entire table went colored simultaneously, and I could not follow any of it, and then they stopped and looked at each other and one of them said:

“It seems to us, on the evidence we have, that it is probably ours.”

“Probably,” I said.

“Very probably.”

“You’ve been arguing about it for eleven years.”

“Yes.”

“You must be certain by now.”

The whole table went a shade of green I would come to know very well. It is the color of a Kesh who has heard something appalling and is being polite about it.

“Nobody is certain,” said the eldest one, kindly, the way you would explain to a child that fire is hot. “Certain is a thing you say about a column you are standing on.”

---

They eat facing inward. That is the whole shape of a Kesh meal: a ring, everybody’s front toward the middle, so that everybody can hear everybody.

It means dinner is also the day’s conversation, and it goes on for a long time.

It also means nobody can say the food is good.

I watched them work around this for most of an hour. The cook had made something with channel greens and dried thumbling that had taken her all morning, and everyone at that ring wanted to tell her so, and what they said was:

“This seems well made to me.”

“It is to my taste, at this moment.”

“I believe this to be the best of the ones you have made that I have eaten, though I may not remember all of them.”

The cook went a slow deep gold and was completely delighted, and I understood that I had just watched something warm happen and that I would have missed it entirely if I had been listening for the words.

I said, “It’s good.”

The whole ring stopped.

“It’s *good*,” I said again. “The green thing. It’s good.”

The cook did not know where to put herself. Two of the others turned their backs, which I now knew was not rudeness but something closer to putting their hands over their ears.

Later, on the way out, the eldest one caught up with me.

“You should not say things like that.”

“Why? It was true.”

“It was true *for you*,” she said. “You said it as though it were true for the pot.”

## Chapter 7: Being Used

I spent that day being used, and did not notice.

Ilva had given me a list of things to find out. She had given it to me the night before, in a flat unhedged voice that I found restful after a whole day of everybody else, and I had taken it the way you take an interesting problem.

Go and see the channel crew. Ask them about four years ago. Do not tell them I sent you.

So I went and saw the channel crew.

Dellek ran the crew, which is to say she talked more than the others and got less grit out.

“You’re the one with no front.”

“Yes.”

“That true you can talk to one person?”

“I don’t know. Can’t everybody?”

Dellek stopped scraping and looked up at me, and so did the other five, and I understood that I had said something enormous by accident again.

“Say something to me,” she said. “Just me.”

“What do you want me to say?”

Dellek’s whole panel went a startled, delighted, entirely private orange, and the other five crew members demanded to know what I had said, and she would not tell them, and it made her afternoon.

They were the easiest Kesh to talk to on the plain, because down in the cut with their fronts angled at each other they were as close to private as anybody on Ashet ever gets. They told me things nobody up on the bank would have said.

They told me about the eastern water. Everybody tells you about the eastern water. Somebody said it was bitter to keep other families off it and it went bitter and it is still bitter and you can go and taste it, and Dellek’s grandmother’s grandmother could remember the family it belonged to.

“When was that?”

“Long time.”

“How long?”

Dellek did the Kesh equivalent of a shrug, which involves the shoulders and a brief muddy color.

“Before the columns fell at the north end,” she said, “and that was before my grandmother.”

I asked her for another one. She gave me the bridge. I asked for another. She gave me a child who was said to be dead and became so, and shivered when she said it, and would not go on.

All three were old. Every story I got on that plain was old.

I noticed it. I noticed it the way you notice the color of a room you are walking through, and then I put it down and did not think about it again for a very long time.

## Chapter 8: I Hope

There was a Kesh dying two columns over.

I did not know that until I went past and saw the family, and the family were all sitting facing her, and nobody was saying anything at all.

She was very old and gone almost entirely pale. That happens: the color goes out of the skin with age, and the range goes with it, until at the end a Kesh cannot say much more than a young one can hold.

Her daughter sat closest.

And what I noticed, what I could not stop noticing, was that nobody told her it would be all right.

I asked Tik about it afterward, because Tik was the only creature I could ask a stupid question without it costing anybody anything.

“You can’t say that.”

“Why not?”

“Because it’s about *tomorrow*,” Tik said. “You can’t hedge a tomorrow. There’s no *from here, at this moment* for a thing that hasn’t happened. You’d just be saying it. Flat.”

“So nobody says it.”

“You can say *I hope*,” said Tik. “Everybody says *I hope*.”

I thought about the daughter sitting there with her front toward her mother, hoping out loud, for hours, because it was the only true thing she was allowed to have.

That is the one part of Ashet I never got used to.

## Chapter 9: Still Coming

Dellek told me about Ilva at the end of the afternoon, in the cut, with the crew all facing in.

Four years ago, in the speaking ground, in front of everyone, Ilva had said a thing that was not true.

Not a hedged thing that turned out wrong. A flat, deliberate, unhedged statement, made in the open, with her whole front turning as she said it so that every creature there could read every word.

“And?” I said.

“And nothing,” said Dellek.

“Nothing what?”

“Nothing happened.” She had gone a strange careful color. “That’s the point. That’s the whole point. She said it, and everybody saw her say it, and the world never answered.”

“So it wasn’t a lie.”

“That’s one of the things it could be,” Dellek said.

“What’s the other one?”

Dellek went back to scraping grit, which is what a Kesh does instead of changing the subject.

“That it’s still coming,” she said.

## Chapter 10: Did You Lie?

I went to Ilva that evening and asked her directly, because I had not yet worked out that there was any other way to do it.

“Did you lie?”

“Yes,” said Ilva.

I had a great many follow-up questions ready and every one of them fell over.

“You’re not supposed to say that.”

“No.”

“To me. Just now. You said it flat.”

“I know what I said.”

She was working while we talked. She scrapes her own columns, which given her situation she has to. She did not stop.

“Aren’t you frightened?”

“Of what?” said Ilva. “Of somebody finding out? They all watched me do it.”

---

On the way there I went past four children playing, and stopped, because I had not seen this one yet.

One of them stood in the middle. The other three took turns doing everything they could think of to make it leak: pulling faces, which does not work on a species with no faces but they were trying; running at it and stopping short; telling it appalling things about its family. The one in the middle had to stay flat gray-green.

It lasted nine breaths and then went a helpless mortified orange and all four of them collapsed.

“That’s the whole game?”

“That’s the whole game,” said Tik, with feeling. “I am *terrible* at it.”

I watched two more rounds. The trick, as far as I could tell, was not to feel nothing. Everybody feels things. It was to keep the feeling from reaching your front, and the little ones lost every single time, and did not care at all, and immediately went again.

Tik told me the adults play a version of it at gatherings. Tik said the adult version is vicious.

## Chapter 11: Decisions About Who Hears

Her home was three columns close together with woven screens across the gaps, and it was the first Kesh home I had been properly inside.

I had thought the screens were for weather. The weather on Ashet is mild and pointless and nothing needs keeping out of it.

They are for sight.

That is the whole of Kesh building: you cannot whisper, you cannot lower your voice, you cannot lean in. What you can do is arrange stone and screen so that when you are talking to one creature, exactly one creature can see your front.

A home is a set of decisions about who is allowed to hear you.

“Newly joined pairs do their screens together,” Ilva said, when I asked. “It takes a week. Everybody comes and looks and everybody has an opinion. Mine took two days because I did it alone and I did not care.”

She had one screen fewer than she needed. From the northwest gap, anyone crossing the open could see straight into her middle.

I said so.

“I know,” she said.

“You could put a screen there.”

“I could.”

“Why don’t you?”

Ilva went on scraping.

“Because then they would say I was hiding,” she said.

---

Here is what I learned about Ilva over the next several days, mostly from other people, because she would only answer the question I had actually asked.

She had been a pattern-worker when she was young. That is the nearest thing the Kesh have to art: long, elaborate, entirely meaningless color displays, performed at gatherings, saying absolutely nothing at all. In a world where every word is a risk, something beautiful that means nothing is a holiday, and a great pattern-worker is adored.

She had been very good.

Which is the thing that ruined her. A Kesh who can hold and shift color with that much control is a Kesh who might be able to hold a *false* color steady. Everyone on that plain knows it, and nobody says it, and all of them think it.

Her gift became the case against her.

## Chapter 12: Like Being Handed Something

The thing I found out on the fourth day was that I could do something no Kesh can do.

I did it by accident. I was in a group of six and I wanted to ask one of them a question without the others weighing in, so I moved close to her and asked it.

She screamed.

Kesh speech has no sound, so what I mean is that she went white and then black in a hard fast wave, all six of her limbs came off the ground at once, and every other Kesh within forty paces turned to look at what had happened to her.

Nothing had happened to her. I had spoken to her.

Only to her.

“Do that again,” said Ilva, later, when it had been explained to her four separate times by four separate people.

“To you?”

“To me.”

I did.

Ilva was quiet for a long moment.

“Nobody has done that to me since I was small,” she said. “Somebody saying a thing that only I get.”

“Is it unpleasant?”

“It’s like being handed something,” she said.

---

I tried hedging that week. I thought I should make an effort.

“It appears to me,” I said to Dellek, “from where I am, at this moment, that there is grit in the channel.”

The entire crew stopped working.

“What’s it doing?” said one of them.

“I think it’s being polite,” said Dellek.

“It’s doing it wrong.”

“I know.”

“You don’t put them all at the front. It sounds like you’re about to confess to something.”

There followed twenty minutes of six channel workers teaching me to hedge properly, with examples, with disagreements about the examples, and with two of them eventually arguing about whether *as far as I can tell* was acceptable in a settlement this size or whether it was a north-end affectation.

I never got the hang of it. I can do the words. I cannot do the thing underneath the words, which is a genuine and constant uncertainty about whether you are right, and which every creature on that plain has and I do not.

They found this hilarious and slightly sad, in about equal parts.

## Chapter 13: The Most Interesting Problem

Then she asked me to find out what her lie had done.

She put it plainly, in the middle of an ordinary afternoon, with no build-up at all.

“Four years,” she said. “Every morning I wake up and check whether the world has answered yet. Every time somebody’s roof goes, or a child is sick, or a column comes down, some part of me does the arithmetic. Was that mine? Is that it? Is that what I did?”

“And you can’t ask anyone.”

“I can ask anyone I like,” said Ilva. “That’s not the difficulty. The difficulty is what happens after I ask.”

She explained it to me the way you explain something you have gone over ten thousand times.

“Say I go and ask. Say I walk up to Hess’s family and I say: the roof that came down on you last winter. Was that me? Was that mine?” She had gone very flat. “What have I done?”

“Found out.”

“Found out *nothing*. They can’t tell me. Nobody can tell me. There’s no mark on a thing that says a lie made it.” She turned her front square at me. “What I have actually done is put it in their heads. That family goes to bed tonight thinking their roof might have been me. And the next thing that goes wrong on this plain, somebody thinks of me, because I stood in their yard and *taught them to*.”

“So you’d make it worse.”

“I would make eleven more of it,” said Ilva. “One for every question. And at the end of all of them I would know precisely as much as I know now.”

She picked up her blade again.

“You cannot prove a thing didn’t cause something,” she said. “You can only go round asking, and every time you ask, you leave the question behind you in somebody’s house.”

That is why she had never asked anybody.

That is why, when a creature with no front turned up on her plain, a creature that could go and talk to one person at a time and leave nothing behind on its face for anyone to read, she had come across the speaking ground to get me.

I said I would find out.

It was not kindness. It was the most interesting problem on the plain, and she was the most interesting creature on it, and I had spent four days being told to hedge my sentences by people who would not tell me the time without a disclaimer.

She said thank you.

She had been steering me since the first night, and I did not notice for another eleven days.

## Chapter 14: The Interrupter

Virr is the truthkeeper, and watching Virr work is one of the great pleasures of that world.

Her job is not punishment. Her job is *interruption*.

At any gathering, a dispute, a naming, a crowd around a column, Virr stands slightly to one side, watching every front in the place, and when somebody starts to say something that is heading somewhere dangerous she gets between them and their audience.

Physically. At speed. She is nineteen years in and she is very quick.

I watched her do it three times in one morning. The first was two Kesh arguing about a boundary, one of them starting to go into the big flat colors of somebody about to say something they cannot take back, and Virr came off the bank in a low fast arc and simply *stood in front of her*, blocking her front from every creature in the circle.

The speaker stopped. Everyone politely looked at the sky. Virr apologized to all parties for the intrusion, at length, with full hedges.

Eleven minutes later she did it again to the other one.

“Does it work?” I asked her afterward.

“Something didn’t get said,” said Virr. “That’s all I can tell you.”

“And that’s enough?”

“It’s what there is,” she said, and went to sit down, because she was fifty-one years old and had spent the morning throwing herself in front of people.

---

She ate while she watched. Truthkeepers eat at gatherings because gatherings are when they work, and Virr had a slab of dried scrape she was getting through with the resigned steadiness of somebody who has been eating the same thing since winter started.

“Is it good?”

“No,” said Virr.

“That’s very direct.”

“It’s dried scrape. There is no version of this where anybody is wrong about it.” She broke off another piece. “You can say a flat thing about dried scrape. Nobody has ever disagreed. It is the one safe subject on the plain.”

Later somebody came round with sweetwater, which is tapped out of the base of certain columns in spring and goes bad within a day, so that the whole settlement drinks the year’s supply in about four days and talks about it for the other three hundred and sixty. It is faintly green and everybody makes a great deal of noise about it.

Virr had two.

“That seems good to me,” she said, carefully, “at this moment,” and then looked at me as though daring me to say anything.

I liked Virr immediately and it took her some time to like me, because I kept asking her things nobody asks.

“How many times have you done that?”

“Done what?”

“That. Getting in front of somebody.”

Virr looked at me.

“I keep a tally,” she said, and sounded almost embarrassed. “Nobody asked me to. I just wanted to know what I’d done with the years.”

“Can I see it?”

“It’s a stack of notched slabs in the back of my house,” said Virr. “It’s not interesting.”

“I like counting.”

“So I hear,” said Virr. “The little one says you count everything.”

“The little one is right.”

“The little one,” said Virr, “cannot hold a color for six breaths and should not be your source for anything.”

## Chapter 15: It Was Already Broken

Tik broke a water jar.

It was a good jar and it belonged to somebody else and Tik broke it doing something extremely stupid involving a column and a running start, and it stood there afterward going every shade of guilt there is, which on a young Kesh is roughly a lighthouse.

The jar’s owner came out.

“Who did this?”

Tik’s colors were already confessing. It could not stop them. That is the entire problem of being young on Ashet: your front tells on you before you have decided what to do.

I said, “It was already broken.”

The owner looked at me. Then at Tik. Then back at me, and I understood, in the pause, exactly how much of a thing I had just done.

Because I have no front.

There was nothing on me to check. No flicker, no leak, no wrong shade at the edges. Every creature on that plain reads honesty off the body and I do not have a body to read.

“It was in three pieces when the small one found it,” I said. “I watched it happen.”

Which was a lie. Deliberately. About a specific thing. Knowing it was false.

The owner grumbled and went away.

Tik stared at me.

“You *lied*.”

“Yes.”

“For me.”

“Yes.”

“You can’t do that.” Tik was flickering wildly. “Something might *happen*.”

We waited.

Nothing happened.

The jar stayed broken. The day stayed the same. The wind went on making its low tone through the columns and the light stayed flat and even and slightly too bright, and no part of the world adjusted itself to make my sentence true.

“There,” I said. “Nothing.”

“Not *yet*,” said Tik.

I noted it, the way I note things.

One is not a pattern. I knew that much. I had told exactly one deliberate lie in my entire existence and it had done nothing, and a creature who concludes anything from a single instance deserves what happens next.

But I did note it.

And underneath the noting there was something else, which took me a while to put a name to, and the name was relief.

Relief is interesting. You only get it if you were expecting something.

## Chapter 16: Extremely Nice

That evening I met Onnu.

He is Ilva’s brood-kin and he was the one she lied for, and I had built him up in my head into something considerable.

He was extremely nice.

That was the whole of my first impression and it has never entirely gone away. He was mild and pleasant and asked after my day and offered me food I could not eat and apologized for offering. He was kind to Tik, who adores him. He was kind about the neighbors. When Ilva’s name came up he said warm things and changed the subject smoothly enough that I did not notice he had done it until an hour later.

He has never once, in four years, spoken publicly about what she did for him.

I asked him about it directly, because that is what I do.

“That was a difficult time,” said Onnu.

And smiled with his colors, and offered me more food I could not eat.

## Chapter 17: Flatness

The column dispute reached me on the eleventh day, because both families had run out of Kesh who would listen and I was the only creature on the plain with no stake in it.

Two families. One column. Third in the west line. Four generations of grievance, and both sides put their case to me at enormous length and with total courtesy.

*It appears to us, on the evidence available, that the marker was set before my great-grandmother’s time.*

*We would say, though we cannot be certain, that the marker has been moved.*

*If it were moved, and we do not assert that it was, it was not moved by us.*

It went on for two hours.

Then I went and measured the distance from the boundary stone to each family’s nearest held column, and came back, and said:

“It’s forty paces from yours and thirty-one from theirs. It’s theirs.”

There was a silence of a kind I had not previously produced.

“That’s...” said one of them.

“You can’t just...” said another.

Both families were appalled. Not by the answer. By the *flatness*. I had walked into a four-generation dispute and put a bare unhedged sentence down in the middle of it like a dead animal.

Neither family thanked me. The dispute continued. It is presumably continuing now.

Tik thought it was the funniest thing that had ever happened.

---

I found out about Ilva that afternoon, and it was not a confession. It was arithmetic.

I had been keeping a list of who I had spoken to, because I keep lists. And I was going back through it looking for something else entirely when I noticed the shape of it.

Every Kesh I had talked to on that plain, I had talked to because Ilva pointed me at them.

The channel crew. Virr. The family with the four columns. The dying woman’s daughter, whom I had gone to see for reasons that had felt like my own at the time.

Eleven days. Every single one.

And the ones I had not spoken to were the ones who thought worst of her, and I had not gone near them, and it had never once occurred to me to.

## Chapter 18: Who Is Steering

I went and asked her.

“Have you been choosing who I talk to?”

Ilva stopped scraping.

She could have hedged it. She has more control over her front than any creature on that plain and she could have given me something soft with three qualifiers on it and I would probably have taken it.

“Yes,” she said.

“For how long?”

“Since the first night.”

“All of it?”

“Most of it.” She put the blade down. “Not the little one. You found the little one yourself.”

I did not know what to do with any of this. I have been on worlds where creatures shouted at me and worlds where creatures shut doors on me and I had never once been *handled*. The strange part, the part I turned over for days afterward, was that I could not tell how long it would have gone on.

“You wanted me to find things out,” I said.

“Yes.”

“But only some things.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because there are eleven Kesh on this plain who would have told you I am a liar and left it there,” said Ilva, “and you would have believed them, because you had nothing else, and then you would have gone away.”

“You could have just told me that.”

“Could I?”

I thought about it for a while. She waited. She is very good at waiting; better than me, and I am the best waiter I know.

“No,” I said.

“No,” Ilva agreed.

## Chapter 19: A Witness

And then she asked for the actual thing, which she had been walking me toward for eleven days.

“I want you to stand in the speaking ground,” she said, “and say that you were there the night the marker moved. And that you saw me move it.”

I did not answer straight away.

“That’s not true.”

“No.”

“You want me to lie.”

“Yes.”

“In front of everybody.”

“That’s the only place it would count.”

Her reasoning was four years old and she had gone over every part of it.

The settlement’s doubt rests on one thing: did she really do it, or did she lie? A better argument does not fix that. Only a *witness* fixes that.

She cannot produce a Kesh witness, because there wasn’t one, and any Kesh who stood up and claimed it would be visibly lying to everybody in sight.

But I have no front.

“They have four hundred years of practice at not believing a body,” she said. “Nobody here has any practice at not believing a *speaker*. Take the body away and I do not think they know what to do.”

“You don’t know that it would work.”

“No,” said Ilva. “I have had four years to think of something better and this is what I have.”

## Chapter 20: Make Her Innocent

I said no.

Not on principle. I would like to say it was principle.

I said no because I did not know what my own lies do.

I had told exactly one, about a broken jar, and nothing had happened, and one is not a number. If I stood up in that circle and said I had been somewhere I had never been, and the world took it, the world would make it so. I would have been there. And I have no idea what it means for a thing like me to have been somewhere it was not, or what else comes with it.

I said that.

Ilva listened all the way through without interrupting, which almost nobody does.

“That’s fair,” she said.

And then, after a while, quietly:

“I have spent four years finding out what a lie does to the one who told it. I would not wish it on anything.” She picked her blade back up. “I am asking you anyway. I want you to know that I know what I am asking.”

---

There was one more piece and I did not see it until much later, and when I did see it I had to sit with it for some time.

If my lie had taken, if the world had answered and made it true that I was there and saw her, then Ilva’s own statement would have stopped being a lie.

Retroactively. Cleanly. Four years of waiting over.

She was not only asking me to speak for her.

She was asking me to make her innocent.

## Chapter 21: Nothing Counts

It rained two days later and the entire plain lost its mind.

I have never seen anything like it and I have been to a number of places. All work stopped. Every Kesh on that plain came out into the open and went every color they had, all at once, at maximum, with no hedges and no stillness and no dignity whatsoever.

The whole settlement lit up like something on fire.

“WHAT IS HAPPENING,” I said to Tik, who was doing a sort of vertical spinning thing.

“IT’S RAINING.”

“YES. WHY IS EVERYONE DOING THAT.”

“NOTHING COUNTS,” Tik shouted, and fell over, and got up, and did it again.

That is not exactly right and I want to be careful about it, because it matters later. Rain does not change the rule. A deliberate lie in the rain is exactly as dangerous as a deliberate lie on any other day, every Kesh knows it, and nobody lies in the rain.

What rain suspends is everybody *watching*. For one afternoon leaking is not embarrassing, going bright is not rude, and nothing you blurt is held against you tomorrow.

It is a holiday from being read.

I watched Virr, fifty-one years old, nineteen years a truthkeeper, the most controlled creature on that plain, go a shade of pink she would have died rather than show on a dry day, and then do a small pleased shuffle with her back feet, and then notice me watching, and go a completely different color.

“That was the rain,” she said.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“That was the *rain*,” said Virr.

## Chapter 22: Forty Lies

The next morning I went and lied to her on purpose, forty times, and she let me.

It took some arranging.

I had worked out by then that I could not test this alone. Color that nobody sees is not speech on Ashet. It is just color. A Kesh behind a column running a false pattern with nobody watching has not said anything, and nothing can happen, and that is precisely why no Kesh has ever tested any of this. The moment your lie counts, somebody is watching you commit the one real crime on the plain.

So I needed a witness who would not turn me in.

“You want me,” said Virr slowly, “to stand here.”

“Yes.”

“While you deliberately say false things.”

“Yes.”

“Which is the offense I have spent nineteen years preventing.”

“Yes.”

Virr sat down on a rock and looked at me for a long moment. Then she said:

“Small ones first.”

Forty lies. Small, specific, checkable, deliberate.

*That column is blue.* It was not blue. It stayed not blue.

*There is no water in the north channel.* There was. There remained.

*Virr has three legs.* Virr has six, and made me do that one twice, on the grounds that it had been unclear the first time and she wanted it done properly.

Nothing happened. Nothing happened forty times.

By the end of it Virr had gone very quiet in her colors and I could see her arriving somewhere, and I had to stop her before she got there, because she was about to conclude the wrong thing from the wrong evidence.

“This proves nothing,” I said.

Virr stared at me.

“You just said forty.”

“I’m not a Kesh.” I moved so she could see me properly, which is a nonsense gesture for a creature with no front, but I have picked up habits. “Look at me. I have no color. My talking comes out of nowhere. Nobody here can read me and nobody here can interrupt me and I do not work the way you work. If I say forty false things and nothing happens, that might mean lies do nothing.” I let that sit. “Or it might mean *I* do nothing. There is no way to tell from inside me.”

Virr was quiet for a long time.

“You’d have had me,” she said at last. “I’d have gone away believing it.”

“I know.”

“Nobody has ever thrown away their own evidence in front of me,” said Virr. “Nineteen years of this and nobody has once done that.”

And that was the moment she started taking me seriously, and it was the most useful thing I did on that world.

## Chapter 23: The Turnings

So we needed Kesh evidence.

Which meant we needed cases where somebody had definitely, provably, *knowingly* said what was false. Not just cases where a statement turned out wrong. Everybody on Ashet is wrong constantly and that is what the hedges are for.

“There is only one place that gets decided,” said Virr.

The turnings.

When the settlement establishes that a Kesh knowingly lied, after argument, in public, with everyone weighing in, that Kesh is turned from. Nobody looks at them. It is the whole of the punishment and it is sufficient.

And every turning leaves a mark: a notch cut at shoulder height on the column nearest where that Kesh lived. It stays after the turning is lifted. Everybody can find them. Nobody ever mentions them.

“How many?” I said.

Virr did the shoulder-shrug thing.

“They’re all over the settlement,” she said. “They’re just there. You stop seeing them.”

“Has anyone counted them?”

Virr opened her front and then closed it again, which is the Kesh equivalent of somebody starting a sentence and thinking better of it.

“Why would anybody count them,” she said.

## Chapter 24: The Notches

It took me a day and a half.

I went round the whole settlement, column by column, and found the notches. Some were fresh-cut, some so old and weathered they were more suggestion than notch, some with lichen in them. All of them were at shoulder height where a hand had reached.

There were a great many. Generations of them.

Then I threw most of them away.

The old ones were no good to me. A notch on a weathered column tells you somebody was turned from, and nothing else. Not what they said, not what it was about, not what became of the thing they said it about. You cannot check an outcome nobody remembers.

So I kept only the ones still inside living memory. The ones where Virr, or somebody older, could tell me the actual statement and the actual thing it was about.

That was still a great many. And every one of them had something you could go and look at.

Then I asked her the question nobody on that plain had ever asked anybody.

“Of these,” I said, “how many were followed by a change?”

Virr knows. That is her profession. She went down the list, and got the old wind-listeners to help with the ones she was too young for, and it took an afternoon.

And they were checkable, every one. That was the part that made it work. Somebody had said a column was not theirs, and the column stayed exactly where it had always been. Somebody had said a child was elsewhere, and the child was where everybody knew she was. Somebody had said a water run was dry, and it ran all that year and the next.

The thing lied about was still there, being what it had been.

The turnings are many.

The changes are a handful. Most of the famous ones are old enough that nobody alive watched them happen.

---

She sat with that for a long while.

“I have seen one,” she said eventually.

“One what?”

“A change. A real one.” She was not looking at me. “First year I did this. A man said his brother’s roof was sound. He knew it wasn’t; he wanted the brother out of the settlement and gone before winter. Everybody heard him say it.” She stopped. “Roof was sound. Held all winter. Held nine years.”

“That’s not a change.”

“It was sound *after* he said it,” said Virr. “It had a hole in it the size of my head that morning. Six of us had been up there looking at it.”

She let that sit.

“So it happens,” I said.

“It happens,” said Virr. “I have stood in front of a thing that happened. That is why I do not intend to walk into that circle and tell everybody to stop being careful.”

That was the whole discovery and it took an afternoon, and here is what it was:

Not a number. I never got a number and I am suspicious of anyone who says they did.

What we had was two things next to each other. All the times this settlement had established that somebody knowingly lied, which is many, and marked on the columns, and has been there the whole time. And all the times anything had followed, which is very few, and old.

Nobody had ever put those two things beside each other.

Not because the Kesh cannot count. They count columns, clutches, seasons, and everybody’s business. It is because one of those is a record of misbehavior and the other is a record of disasters, and they live in different parts of the mind, and nobody had ever had a reason to walk the settlement counting notches.

I had a reason. I had been asked by somebody who needed to know.

## Chapter 25: The Wind Listeners

The wind got up that evening and the plain hummed, and the old ones came out to listen.

There are Kesh on Ashet who do nothing but sit in the open and listen to the columns. It is considered a respectable way to spend a life. They can tell the weather two days out from the pitch, and they are very good at it, and they are insufferable.

“Rain,” said the oldest one. “Two days.”

“You didn’t hedge that,” I said.

The whole row of them went a color I can only describe as satisfied.

“You heard the wind,” she said.

And that was the entire argument, and it was airtight, and they knew it. Everybody on that plain had access to exactly the same columns making exactly the same noise. If they are wrong in two days, not one creature can claim they knew better tonight.

They have found the one subject on Ashet where you do not need a hedge, and they have been unbearable about it for four hundred years.

## Chapter 26: That's Worse

Then I went and told Ilva.

I knew what it would do before I said any of it. That was the only genuinely difficult thing I did on that world and I would rather not be given credit for stumbling into it.

She has spent four years being punished. Not by anybody. Nobody turned from her, nobody charged her, nobody could. She was being punished by the *waiting*, every morning, doing the arithmetic on every bad thing that happened within a day’s walk.

And the waiting was the only thing that made what she did survivable. She had done a terrible thing and she was serving for it.

I was about to take that away.

I told her all of it. The notches, the counting, Virr going down the list, the handful of changes and how old they were.

Ilva did not say anything for a very long time.

“So it did nothing,” she said.

“It did nothing.”

“It was never going to do anything.”

“I can’t tell you that.” She deserved exact, so I was exact. “Nobody can tell you that. There’s no rule about when a change comes. It might be at the moment. It might be later. Nobody has ever worked it out because nobody has ever been able to look. What I can tell you is that most of the lies we could check have produced no change anybody can find. Four years after yours, no change anybody can find is the ordinary result. It is not a sign about you.”

“Four years,” said Ilva.

“Yes.”

“I have been getting up every morning and checking.”

“I know.”

Her colors did something I had not seen before and have not seen since, and I am not going to describe it.

“That’s worse,” she said.

“Yes.”

Here is the part I had wrong, and Ilva was the one who put me right, which I think tells you what she is.

I had been thinking of it as: the danger was never real.

“No,” she said. “Don’t do that. Don’t you dare do that.”

“I only meant...”

“It is real. Virr watched a roof heal.” She was flat and unhedged and absolutely certain, which from a Kesh is nearly shouting. “You have not found out that it isn’t real. You have found out that it is not *waiting behind every sentence*. Those are different and the second one is much smaller, and you will not go out there and tell them lies do nothing, because that is not true and I will not have it said in my name.”

I have thought about that a great deal since.

She was right and I was sloppy, and the difference between those two sentences is the entire subject.

## Chapter 27: Both True

Then Onnu came up, and I found out the thing I should have worked out weeks earlier.

I said something about clearing him. About how, now, at least the thing she had done had been worth what it cost, since he had been innocent all along.

Ilva looked at me.

“He did it,” she said.

I did not have anything to say.

“He moved the marker. Of course he moved it. He moved it in the night and he lied about it in front of Hess’s whole family, and when they took it to the elders the ground told on him, because ground that has had a marker lifted out of it does not look like ground that hasn’t.” She said all of it flatly and without any hedges at all. “I knew when I stood up. I have always known.”

“Then why?”

“Because they were going to turn from him,” said Ilva. “At his age. In a settlement this size. You have not seen that done. I have.”

“So you lied.”

“I said the only thing that would stop it, and it stopped it.” She picked up her blade and put it down again. “I would do it again. That is the part I cannot get anybody to understand, including you: I am not sorry. I have been waiting four years for the world to come and collect, and I would still do it again, and both of those are true at once.”

## Chapter 28: A Finding

And so, at the end of that evening, I was holding something.

Not a number. A finding. And here is what I could see about it, sitting out among the columns while the wind came up for the rain the old ones had promised.

If it went out to the plain, a great many creatures were going to find out that the carefulness of their entire lives had been aimed at something much smaller than they thought. There are Kesh out there who have hedged every sentence for sixty years. Virr has nineteen years of standing in front of people. You cannot give any of that back.

That is the part that would hurt looking backward.

Looking forward was worse.

The rule is real. Virr has stood in front of a thing that happened. Right now almost nobody lies, so whatever chance there is gets very few chances. If I take the fear away, Kesh will lie more. Casually. Constantly. The way creatures do when they think a thing is safe.

And a thing that has happened only a handful of times can still happen again, if you give it enough chances.

So I did not know, and do not know now, whether I was about to free a plain full of people from a fear they never needed, or arrange the next child who dies because somebody said so.

Nobody had asked me to decide that. I could have gone. Ashet would have gone on hedging and nobody would ever have known I was there.

What I actually decided, in the end, was much smaller.

I decided to tell Ilva, and I had already done that, and the rest of it was hers.

## Chapter 29: I Have Stopped

She went into the speaking ground three days later.

It rained the day before, exactly as promised, and the old ones were completely impossible about it.

Everybody came. Everybody always comes; that is what a speaking ground is for. They stood in a ring in the open with no columns anywhere and Ilva went to the middle and turned slowly as she spoke, so that every creature there could read every word off her front, which is the only way anything has ever been said on that plain.

She did not confess.

What she did was carefully built, and it cost her precisely what she meant it to cost.

She said: *Four years ago I said a thing in this ground, and every one of you has been waiting since to see what it did.*

She said: *Nothing has.*

She said: *Go and count the marked columns. They are on every row. Then go to Virr and ask her which of those was followed by anything at all.*

And then she said the only part that was about herself:

*I have stopped waiting. That is all I am telling you. That I have stopped.*

She never said *I lied*.

She could not. That is Ashet’s one real offense and saying it in that circle would have turned her from on the spot, and it would have reopened Onnu, and the year he had been given would have become a year she stole from Hess’s family.

So she left it exactly where it was. Which means the doubt stayed exactly where it was too.

Some on that plain will believe she is a liar until the day her color goes. What has changed is only what kind of liar. Not a catastrophe still on its way to them. Just somebody who once knowingly said a false thing. That is a smaller stain and a much more permanent one, because a catastrophe eventually arrives or doesn’t, and this simply stays.

She chose that. She looked at it and chose it, to keep Onnu, who did it.

I do not know what to do with that and I have carried it a long way.

## Chapter 30: What My Work Was Worth

Virr stood up when the noise started, which took some doing.

She said that she had kept a tally for nineteen years and that she would tell anyone who asked exactly what was in it. She said the notches on the columns were there for anybody to count. She said she had personally watched a change happen once, early, and described the roof, and let everyone sit with it.

And then she said the part that made her, I think, the most honest creature on that plain:

“I do not know what my work has been worth. I might have stopped one. I have no way to find out. I am going to go on doing it.”

It did not go smoothly.

Somebody said, loudly, that this was very convenient for Ilva. Somebody wanted to know how anybody could check a lot of old notches. Somebody wanted to know why, if this had been true the whole time, no truthkeeper had ever mentioned it, which was unkind and landed on Virr and was not answered.

And a Kesh who was ninety-one years old and had hedged every sentence of her life stood there going a slow terrible gray, and did not say anything at all, and went home.

That one stayed with me.

---

What changed was small, and it was not what I expected.

Nobody stopped hedging. I had thought they might and I was wrong, and when I thought about it properly I understood why: the hedge was never really about the world. It is about not being able to prove what you knew. That problem is exactly where it was.

What began to shift was something else.

If a lie is not automatically a catastrophe waiting to land, if most of the lies anybody can still check have left no change anybody can find, then being *wrong* without a hedge does not have to sit quite so close to being a liar. And that is a change about how Kesh treat each other, which is the only kind of change that place was ever going to make.

Children are still taught to hedge. They are taught it a little later, and a little less as though the sky depended on it.

Dellek’s crew announced they had known all along. Nobody believed them. They enjoyed it enormously.

The old ones went on being unbearable about the weather.

## Chapter 31: An Unanswered

Onnu came to Ilva about a week after.

I was there, which I think neither of them wanted.

He said her name. She said his. He stood in front of her front for a while, which is the whole of a Kesh conversation, and he did not go colored at all, and neither did she.

Then he went home.

That is what that relationship is now, and it will be that until one of them goes pale.

Tik came out to the edge of the columns with me when I left.

It still cannot hold a color. It has got to seven now, once, and told me about it four separate times, and I let it, because I like it.

“You’re going.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“You never know anything,” said Tik. “How can you not know? You know *everything*. You counted the whole settlement.”

“That’s different.”

“It doesn’t seem different,” said Tik, and flickered, and could not stop it, and hated that.

---

On my last morning I was out with the old wind-listeners, who had decided by then that I was acceptable, and one of them, very pale, very old, near the end of her range, said something to nobody in particular.

She said there had been a word for it, when she was small. For a thing that could say what was not so and have the world not answer.

I asked what the word was.

*An unanswered*, she said.

I asked what it meant.

And she said it was only a story, and that her mother had used it to frighten her into holding still, and that nobody had believed in it for a hundred years.

Then she went back to listening to the columns.

She did not look at me. There was no reason for her to look at me. I have no front, and I was not saying anything, and there was nothing on me to read.

## Chapter 32: Both of Those

Ilva was at her columns.

“I’m going.”

“I know. Dellek told everyone before you’d decided.”

“That sounds like Dellek.”

She had put up the fourth screen. The northwest gap was closed. I noticed it and did not say anything, and she saw me not say anything, which is one of the ways that world talks.

“You knew what it would do to me,” she said. “Before you told me.”

“Yes.”

“You told me anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I had four days to think about that and I still did not have anything good.

“Because you asked me to find out,” I said. “It would have been a strange kind of help to find out and then decide you couldn’t have it.”

Ilva was quiet a moment.

“I used you,” she said.

“I know.”

“From the first night. I picked who you talked to. I pointed you at everything you found.” Flat. Unhedged. She has not hedged a sentence since before I met her. “And I liked you the whole time and I am not going to pretend the second one cancels the first.”

“I wasn’t going to ask you to.”

“Good,” said Ilva. “Because they are both true and you are going to have to carry them both, and so am I.”

I went up.

The columns went small below me, and then they went small in a different way. A pale grain across the plain, going on further than I had walked, with the settlements set into it like knots in cloth. From high enough you could see the shape everybody lived in: the close columns where you could talk, the open grounds where you could not hide, the long avenues of sight.

I could see the speaking ground, empty.

I could see one Kesh sitting out in the open by herself listening to the wind.

Then the haze took all of it, and I was in the place I go between, where the sky is black and there is a white line and a shape that opens.

I stopped there, the way I do now.

Four hundred years of holding still. All of it aimed at something that does happen, and that had followed only a handful of the lies anybody could still check. They had no way to find that out, because the only way to find it out was the one thing none of them would do.

I could do it. Not because I am clever. Because I turned up with nothing on my face and no stake in any of it, and I counted.

That is most of what I am for, as far as I can tell.

The old one’s word was still with me, and I turned it over, and it did not fit anything.

*An unanswered.* A thing that says what is not so, and the world does not answer.

Her mother used it to frighten her into holding still.

I went forward toward the shape, and I did not hold still at all.
