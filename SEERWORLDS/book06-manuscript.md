## Chapter 1: Up From What?

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I can put five worlds in order now. I have stopped saying that I am getting good at them, because every time I say it a world proves otherwise, usually within a day.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

I have started to wonder about that. That is new. Until the last world I crossed the way you go through a door you have gone through a thousand times, which is to say without looking at it. Then I learned something about a line in a valley. Somebody had put it there, and somebody kept it there. I have not been able to stop turning that over.

So I arrived on the sixth world already thinking about seams.

Which is probably why it took me so long to notice I was falling.


I wasn’t falling.

That is the first thing to say, and it took me an embarrassing amount of time to establish it. I arrived in open air with light all around me and nothing underneath. My first thought was that I had come out somewhere wrong, in the middle of a crossing, or above a world instead of in it.

I looked down.

Under me was brightness. Not a floor. Not a landscape a long way away, which is what I expected and what I kept trying to see. Just white light and haze going down and down, softening, until there was nothing more I could make out.

I looked up.

The same. Blue going pale.

I looked sideways, which is where the world turned out to be.


Stone.

An enormous span of it, pale and dry, curving away from me in both directions. Wide enough to be a field. Weathered on top. And it did not come up out of anything or go down into anything, it simply ran, out through open air, until the distance took it.

And there was another one above it, crossing at an angle.

And another below that, steeper.

And once I had seen three I could see forty, and once I had seen forty I stopped counting, which for me is a considerable admission.

They went in every direction. Some nearly flat. Some standing on end. One looped over another and came back under it and kept going. Two of them ran side by side for a long way, close enough that I could have touched both at once, and never joined. There were holes through some of them, bored clean, big enough to move through. There were spans wide enough to hold a town and spans no wider than a way through, and I could not always tell which was which until I got close.

It was a lattice. The whole world was a lattice, hanging in the light with nothing above it and nothing below it, and I hung in the middle of it and turned slowly around for a while and did not think anything at all.

Then I thought: *what holds it up?*

And then, more slowly: *up from what?*

## Chapter 2: For You

I went and put myself against the nearest span.

It was warm on the side facing the light. Dry. Slightly rough. It had the grain of stone that has been out in weather for an extremely long time, and there were shallow dished places in it where something had worn it down.

Worn it down by going over it. A lot.

That was when I understood that the world was inhabited, and about four seconds later something came along the span toward me at a good speed and I moved out of the way.


It was long.

That is the first thing. It was longer than me by a great deal, long and low and jointed, six or seven hard segments with darker creases between them, pale stone-colored, dusty. Along both sides, spaced out down its whole length, were limbs with broad gripping pads, and they came down onto the rock and lifted and came down again in a traveling ripple, and the whole arrangement went past me with the unhurried competence of something doing a thing it has done every day of its life.

It had no head.

I want to be accurate. It had two ends, and both ends had organs on them, small and dark and set in clusters, and the clusters at the front were doing the same job as the clusters at the back. I could not tell you which end was the front. I decided the front was the end that was going first, and I was pleased with this for about nine seconds.

Then it stopped, and reversed, and came back past me the other way, without turning around.

The end that had been behind was now in front. The limbs ran the other way. Nothing else about it changed at all.

“Oh,” I said.

It stopped again.


“You’re not on anything.”

The voice did not come from either end. It came from somewhere in the middle of it, low down between two segments, and there was nothing there to see, no opening I could find, no movement. Just the voice, arriving out of the body generally.

“No,” I said.

“You’re not on anything,” it said again, in the tone of somebody who has offered you a way out of an obvious mistake and is prepared to offer it twice.

“I don’t need to be.”

There was a pause. Both ends of it were pointed at me now, the body bent in the middle so that both sets of organs came round, and I was being looked at from two places at once by something with no face to read.

“Right,” it said.

I thought I would demonstrate. I went out sideways off the edge of the span, over the open air, and stopped there, about ten body-lengths out with the brightness going down forever underneath me, and I said, “It’s fine. There’s nothing to it. You could come out here.”

Both ends stayed exactly where they were.

“No,” it said.

“It’s completely safe.”

“For you.”

“You wouldn’t fall. I mean, you would, but that isn’t the point I’m making.”

“It is quite a lot of the point,” it said, “from here.”

I came back. It watched me the entire way with both sets of organs, which I was starting to find unnerving, and when I was on the stone again it made a small sound in the middle of its body that I had no way to interpret and would have given a great deal to understand.

Then it went on down the span, and I watched it go, and about thirty body-lengths along it went over the edge and continued along the underside, upside down, at the same speed.

Which, I want to point out, was considerably more alarming than anything I had done.

## Chapter 3: There Isn’t a Straight Line

I did not follow it upside down. I hung there and thought about it instead.

Here is what I had, after an hour.

There is no ground. I checked. I went down, properly down, a long way, past four spans and then a fifth, into the part where the light gets thick and white and there is more haze than air, and there was never anything under me except more of the same. No floor. No bottom. Nothing but brightness, all the way to wherever brightness ends.

Nobody lives down there. Nothing does.

I came back up, and I will admit I came back up faster than I went down.

And the second thing I had was this: everything here is ways. The whole world is a set of ways from one place to another, crossing over and under each other, and if you are a long jointed creature with gripping pads, you get where you are going by climbing.

I decided this was the simplest world I had ever arrived on.

I was wrong within the hour, but I want it written down that I thought it, because the mistake is the interesting part.

---

I found the town by looking at it.

It was up and over, a place where five or six spans came together close, almost touching, and the gaps between them had been built into. There were structures on top of the stone and structures slung underneath it. There were hoops arcing from one span to the next like handles. There was green growing along the sunward faces in trained lines, and there were shallow bowls set into the upper curves, catching the light, holding rainwater from whenever it last rained. Underneath, in the shade, there were quieter frames where I could see a few long bodies moving slowly and not going anywhere.

It was busy. Even from a distance it was obviously busy. Bodies moved along every span into it and out of it. There was noise coming off it, and something was being hauled up a slope in a sling.

I looked at it for a while.

Then I went there.


It took me a minute and a half.

I mention this because of what happened next. I went into the town and found the creature I had met on the span. It was up in the middle of things, in among a crowd, with a load in slings along its central segments, and I said, more or less, *hello again, I got here.*

And it said, “You can’t have.”

“I did.”

“You were out on the west rib. I left you on the west rib.” Both ends came round. “That’s the far side of a break. You’d have to go down to the under-run, along, up through the slot at the elbow, and back over. That’s a morning.”

“I went in a straight line.”

There was a silence.

“There isn’t a straight line,” it said.

“There is if you don’t touch anything.”

## Chapter 4: The View and the Way

I have thought about that conversation a lot since.

At the time I was pleased with myself, which tells you something about me. I had solved the world in ninety seconds. I had looked at a place and gone to it, and everybody else was going the long way round like a fool, and I did not say that out loud but I was certainly thinking it in a voice I would not want played back.

What I had actually done was this.

I had looked at the town, and I had seen how far away it was, and I had assumed that how far away a thing is tells you how hard it is to get to.

On the world I had just left, that was true. On the four before it, it was true. It is true on the world you are on right now, most of the time, near enough that you never have to think about it.

It is not true here. It is not even close to true here. It is so far from true that they have a whole trade about it, and a whole way of talking about it, and one of the shortest questions in their language exists because of it.

You can see almost everything on the Reach. You hang in the middle of it and the whole world is in front of you at once: towns and spans and crossings and the small distant movements of people going about their day, laid out in the light with nothing in the way.

And seeing it tells you nothing whatsoever about whether you can get there.


I didn’t learn its name that day. It had a delivery to finish, and it went, and I stayed in the town, and I spent the rest of the light watching people come and go along the spans and trying to work out why some of them stopped at a junction, looked at a piece of hide, and then went a completely different way than the one they had obviously been about to take.

Everybody was carrying one. Every single person in that town had a folded piece of hide somewhere about them, tucked into a sling, jammed under a strap, and they were pulling them out and looking at them and putting them away all day long, the way you would check something you check so often you have stopped noticing you do it.

I could not think what could possibly be on them.

There was one way in and out of that town in every direction, plainly visible, going where it went. Anybody could see where the ways were. I could see where the ways were, and I had been there an afternoon.

What did they need a map for?

## Chapter 5: That Changed

I found out the next morning, and I found out the way I find out most things, which is by being wrong in public.

There was an argument going on at a junction.

Not a bad one. Two of them, both middle-aged and both entirely certain, stopped in the middle of a busy crossing where four spans came together, blocking everybody, having the kind of disagreement people only have about something they have disagreed about before.

“You can’t get through the elbow with that.”

“I got through the elbow with that last week.”

“Not with a full sling.”

“With a full sling.”

“Not with *that* sling.”

I drifted over, because I am nosy, and because after one afternoon on the Reach I had already formed the opinion that this was a simple world and I understood it.

One of them pulled out a folded piece of hide.

“Look,” it said. “Look at it. It’s not there.”

The other one took it.


I was watching the hide when it changed.

It happened fast and I want to be precise. There was a line on it, a thin dark line running from a junction mark toward the edge. When the second one took the hide, the line was not there anymore. Something else was. A different line, coming off the same junction at a different angle, going somewhere else entirely.

Nothing moved. There was no shimmer, no fade. It was one thing and then it was another thing, the way a word is one word and then you blink and read it correctly.

“There,” said the second one, satisfied. “Told you.”

“That’s *your* elbow.”

“It’s an elbow.”

“It is not the same elbow, and you know it isn’t, because you have never once been able to get to my elbow, and I have watched you try.”

And they went on like that. Nobody around them was paying the slightest attention. A whole town’s worth of people were going round them the way water goes round a rock, and the two of them stood in the middle of the junction arguing about two completely different elbows on two completely different pieces of hide that had been, four seconds earlier, the same piece of hide.

I said, “Excuse me.”

Both of them bent toward me, all four sets of organs at once.

“That changed,” I said.

They looked at me. Then they looked at each other. Then the first one said, in the careful voice you use with somebody who has just told you that water is wet:

“Yes.”


Here is what a map is on the Reach.

It is a piece of worked hide about the size of a large leaf, and it shows the ways you can get to things.

Not the ways. **The ways you can get to things.**

Because they are not all the same shape, and the world is a lattice with gaps in it.

If you are long, you can bridge a break a short one can’t. If you are short and you fold, you can go through a slot a long one would jam in halfway and have to be pulled out of backward, which I am told happens, and which is apparently very funny for about a year afterward.

No Reachfolk could go everywhere. Every one of them could go somewhere somebody else couldn’t.

The map knows which one you are.

## Chapter 6: Ilm

I asked how it knew.

I asked about eleven times that morning and never got an answer. It is a map. It shows your ways. If it did not, it would be a piece of hide and you would want your money back.


The town was called Ilm, and a town here is a knot: a place where enough spans come together that you can build across the gaps.

Everything wraps around the stone. Half of Ilm is above you and half is slung underneath, and you go between them along hoops that arc from span to span like handles on a basket. Green is trained along the sunward faces in careful lines. Down in the shade underneath, where the light comes up off the haze instead of down from the sky, there are quiet frames where people who are unwell or very old rest in the warm, and the noise of the town comes down to them from above.

And there is a great deal of noise to come down. A knot is where everything crosses, so a knot is where everybody is, all day, hauling and calling and stopping to talk in the middle of a way and being complained at.

I was happy in Ilm before I had a reason for it.


I liked it more when I understood about the loads.

They carry things in long slings that run down the central segments, so a loaded person is a person with a fat middle and a lot of opinions about it. And a full sling changes your shape.

Which changes your ways.

Which changes your map.

I watched a courier come into Ilm heavy, drop a load at a frame, and unfold the hide to check the way home. The way home was not the way there. It could take a slot on the return that it could not take on the way in. It knew this. It had known this before it left. The whole trip had been planned around being fat in one direction and thin in the other, and it thought no more of this than you think about which pocket your keys are in.

“You go out one way and back another.”

“You go out one way and back another,” it agreed, and went to get a drink.

## Chapter 7: The High Bend

The child attached itself to me around midday.

Four segments, possibly five, extremely shiny, in the way young ones are before the dust gets into the joints properly. Its name was Nib.

“Are you new.”

“Yes.”

“I can take the high bend now.”

“The...”

“The high bend. Over there. That one. The one going up.” Nib had rotated somehow without appearing to move any part of itself and was pointed at a span that came off the top of the knot at what I would call an unpleasant angle. “I couldn’t before. I did it this morning. I did it twice this morning. I could do it again now.”

“Congratulations.”

“It’s very hard,” said Nib. “It’s the hardest one. Most people can’t.”

By dusk, five separate adults had told me that most people can do the high bend, that the high bend is not the hardest one, that the high bend is not really considered a bend at all, and that everybody in Ilm had been hearing about the high bend for four days.

“What’s your reach?” said Nib.

“I don’t have one.”

Nib thought about this for slightly under a second.

“I did the high bend without stopping,” it said. “In the middle. Where most people stop.”

And it went off to tell somebody else.

---

The courier station at Ilm was on the underside, slung below the biggest of the spans, because that is where the most ways meet and couriers care about that more than they care about daylight.

It was a long low frame full of noise. Slings hanging in rows. Hides in stacks, fresh ones, waiting. A board of jobs with weights and destinations on it and a queue of people looking at the board and then looking at their own map and then looking at the board again and doing arithmetic on their bodies.

That is what a courier is. It is not an adventurer. It is somebody who is going that way anyway, or who can go that way when most people can’t, and who is therefore worth paying to take the thing.

They looked tired. All of them looked tired, in the specific way of people who are good at a job that is mostly walking.

The one running the place was called Terro, who was old enough to have gone gray in the joints and unhurried enough to make everybody else in the room look frantic. Terro was checking a load against a hide when I came in and did not stop checking it while we talked.

“You’re the floating thing.”

“Yes.”

“Ilm’s been talking about you.” A pause while something got weighed. “You went over the west break.”

“Yes.”

“Straight over.”

“Yes.”

Terro did not say anything for a while. Then: “Handy,” in a tone I could not read at all, and went back to the load.

## Chapter 8: The Whole Trade

I asked Terro the question I had been carrying around all day, which was why anybody bothered.

Not the maps. I understood the maps by then, or I thought I did. What I did not understand was the *fuss*, the checking, the folding, the pulling out and putting away, forty times a day, for a set of ways that surely you already knew.

“You’ve lived here your whole life,” I said. “You know where you can go.”

“Mm.”

“So why check?”

Terro set the load down and bent so that both ends were pointed at me, and I got the feeling I had said something that was either very stupid or very rude and I could not have told you which.

“Because I send people out,” Terro said.

“Yes.”

“On a way I can’t go myself. To somewhere I’ve never been. Carrying something that matters to somebody.”

“Yes.”

“So the map has to be right,” said Terro, and picked the load back up. “That’s the whole trade. There’s nothing else in it. If a courier’s map is right, they’re a courier. If it isn’t, they’re a person walking about with somebody else’s parcel.”

I said that seemed reasonable.

“It’s not reasonable,” Terro said. “It’s just true.”

---

I went up top afterward and stayed at the edge of Ilm while the light went long and orange across the spans, and I watched the whole world do a thing I had never seen a world do.

The light dropped.

And everybody stopped, at more or less the same time, and got out a piece of hide, and looked at it.

Not for long. A moment each. The ways you can take in this light are not the ways you could take an hour ago. A bad step you can see is a bad step you can make, and one you cannot see is not. So the maps had changed, all of them, all over the knot, and everyone I could see had stopped to find out what they could still do.

Hundreds of them. All over the knot and out along every span, small pale bodies stopping mid-journey and unfolding a leaf-sized piece of hide and folding it again.

Every one of those maps was different.

Every single one, and none of them was wrong, and it had never once occurred to anybody here that this was remarkable.

I thought it was the best thing I had ever seen.

## Chapter 9: Rib and Road

There was a crew working the north span, and I went to watch them because I had nothing else to do and because they were making a noise I could not identify.

The noise was chisels.

Four of them, spaced out along a stretch of bare stone, cutting notches into it. Small deep notches, two pad-widths apart, in a line, and behind them another one was going along with a heavier tool taking the crown off the rock, knocking the round of it down into a flat, so that what had been a curved stone surface became a strip you could put a body on without sliding.

Further back, where they had finished, the stone was different in a way I could see from a long way off.

Flat down the middle. Notched along both edges in a regular repeating line. Grooves cut at the outer margins, shallow and deliberate. And every so often a frame, a set of braced bars anchored into the rock, wide enough for two or three bodies to rest on out of the wind.

I went along the finished part and then the unfinished part and then back again, four or five times, until I had it.

Then I asked one of them what the difference was called.

“That’s rib,” it said, pointing one end at the raw stone. “That’s road,” pointing the other end at the worked part.

“And the difference is that somebody made it.”

“The difference is that somebody made it,” it agreed. “Took eleven years, that stretch. My mother started it.”


So: a rib is stone. Most of the Reach is exactly that, bare rock going where it goes, no help from anyone.

A road is a rib people have worked on. Flattened top, notches in sequence, edge grooves, resting frames. You can tell at a glance, from a long way off, in bad light. It is not subtle and it is not meant to be. Somebody coming along at speed can see that the next stretch has been made safe by people who came before them.

There are not many. They take years, and they get made only where enough people need to go for long enough to justify eleven years of chisels.

Rib is what the world is. Road is what somebody decided the world should be.

## Chapter 10: The Agreement

I met Vess two days later, at the station, in the middle of an ordinary morning.

Vess had just come back from somewhere and was arguing pleasantly with Terro about the weight of something, and lost, and took a smaller job instead, and was cheerful about losing.

Six segments. Youngish. You can tell by the joints, which go dusty and matte with age and were still shiny on Vess. Quick on the stone. Very good at the job, and newly delighted by what the latest change had added to it.

“You’re the floater.”

“I’m the floater.”

“Terro says you went over the west break.”

“I did.”

“That’s cheating,” said Vess, with feeling, and went to work.


I kept finding reasons to be near Vess.

Vess was fast and precise and did not make a performance of it. The complaining was constant and cheerful and entirely without self-pity: weights, weather, people who give you an address instead of a junction, and a particular customer in the lower quarter who Vess was convinced moved house specifically to be annoying.

And Vess had just changed, and was pleased about it, and kept forgetting.


Reachfolk grow in steps.

You go along the same for a good while and then over the course of a few weeks you lengthen, or you put on a segment, and then you stop again. It happens most in the young and it happens a little into adulthood and then it more or less stops, and the whole business is regarded with about as much ceremony as you would give to a pair of shoes getting tight.

Vess had come out of one about a month before I arrived. Slightly longer. One joint sitting differently than it used to.

Which meant the map changed.

“Two,” Vess said, when I asked. “I got two.”

“Two new ways?”

“Two.” Vess was pleased in a way that was trying not to be. “There’s a bridge across the Fell gap I couldn’t make before, it’s a stretch, but I can make it now, and it takes an hour off the eastern run. And there’s a shelf under the market span. I can get under there now. Nobody can get under there.”

“What’s under there?”

“Nothing,” said Vess. “Nothing at all. It’s completely useless. But I can get under there.”


A map is not a picture of the world, then. It is a picture of the agreement between you and the world, right now. The agreement gets renegotiated all the time by things as ordinary as a heavy parcel.

## Chapter 11: The Third One

Vess found the third one eight days after that.

I was there when Vess found it. We were at the station in the early part of the day and Vess had taken a western job, the first one since the change, and had the hide open at a part of it I had not seen before, out past the break, unfolded the whole way.

Vess stopped.

“That’s new.”

“Another one?”

“Must be from the change.” Vess bent close over the hide. “I haven’t had a reason to look out this far since before. Nothing out here I ever get sent to.”

“So it’s a third one.”

“So it’s a third one,” said Vess, and then did not say anything for a while.

I came and looked, and Vess held it flatter so I could see, and did not hand it to me, and I did not ask.

There was a line on the hide going out from a junction on the western side, a junction I recognized, out past the break I had crossed on my first day. It went outward. It went past two other junctions without joining them. It went out to where the hide showed nothing else at all, the empty part at the margin where the drawing ran out before the hide did.

And then it went to the edge of the hide.

And stopped being on the hide, because the hide had ended.

“Huh,” said Vess.


I did not understand what was wrong. I looked at that line for some time and thought *there is a way going west,* and could not see the problem, and Vess had to tell me.

“Where does it end?”

“At the edge.”

“That’s not an ending.” Vess had gone very still, which on a Reachfolk means all the limbs stop at once, all down both sides. “That’s the edge of the hide. That’s not on the map. That’s where I ran out of map.”

“So it ends further on.”

“Then it would say so.” Vess turned the hide over, which did nothing, and turned it back. “That’s what a map is. A way goes somewhere and then it stops, and where it stops there’s a mark. There’s a mark for a place. There’s a mark for a drop, when your next way is down to another rib. There’s a mark for a way that’s closed and a different one for a way that’s fallen and a different one again for a way that’s there but you shouldn’t. Every way I have ever had on any map I have ever held has told me where it ends.”

“And this one?”

“This one doesn’t say anything,” said Vess. “It just goes to the edge and I run out of paper.”

## Chapter 12: I Can’t Finish This

Vess had to tell me twice, and it took a while to go in, and here is what it was.

Every map runs out somewhere. The hide is only so big, and the world is bigger, and when you go past the margin you get a new hide. There were four other ways on that same map that ran off the western edge and nobody thought twice about them.

Those four had endings marked before the hide ran out: *Merrin*. *Drop*. *Shut.* You knew what you would find when you took out the next hide.

This one had nothing.

Not a wrong ending. Not a strange ending. **No ending at all**, on a system that had never once in Vess’s life failed to provide one, for a way that was, as far as the map was concerned, perfectly good, perfectly usable, and Vess’s own.

The map was not saying *you can go past the edge of the world.*

The map was saying *I can’t finish this.*


“Maps always tell you where a way ends,” Vess said.

We were still at the station. Nobody else had come over. It was an ordinary morning and there was noise everywhere and a queue building at the job board.

“This one didn’t,” I said.

“No.”

Vess folded the hide up, slowly, along the creases, and put it away, and then took it out again and looked at it once more.

Then Vess said it in the flat voice of somebody stating a professional obligation nobody could possibly argue with.

“I’ll have to tell Terro.”

## Chapter 13: Show Me the Other Two

Vess told Terro that morning. Not reluctantly. Not after thinking about it. Vess found a fault in a map, and telling the station about a fault in a map is what a courier does, in the same way that you would mention a crack in a bridge you had just walked over. It did not occur to Vess to do anything else.

Terro listened. Looked at the hide, which Vess held out flat, because you do not hand your map to somebody if you want them to see what you are seeing.

Then Terro said: “Show me the other two.”


That is the part I did not expect.

Terro did not start with the impossible way. Terro started with the Fell bridge and the useless shelf under the market span, the two ordinary ones, and went out with Vess that afternoon and watched Vess do both.

Vess made the bridge across the Fell gap. It was a stretch and it looked like a stretch, and Vess made it clean.

Vess got onto the shelf under the market span, where there is nothing to do and nothing to fetch, and came back up.

“Right,” said Terro.

Then Terro measured Vess.


Which is exactly as undignified as it sounds. There is a frame at the station for it, and Vess got into the frame, and Terro went down one side with a knotted cord and then down the other, and read out numbers to a courier writing them on a slate, while about nine people who did not need to be there watched with enormous interest and gave advice.

“Hold still.”

“I am still.”

“You’re bending.”

“I’m *breathing.*”

“Breathe less.”

They got the length. They got the reach at four points. They got the bend, which involved Vess folding up as small as possible and being told that it was not as small as possible and that they had seen Vess go smaller getting into a supply frame after hours, which Vess denied.

It took most of an hour and it was extremely funny and I laughed at all of it, and I was still laughing when Terro read the numbers and said, “That’s a good change. That’s a real one,” and I understood that this had not been a joke at any point.

Terro had just established that Vess’s body was exactly what Vess said it was.

Which meant the map ought to be right.

Terro went to the stack of fresh hides and took one down.

“Hold this.”

Vess did. The blank worked hide settled under Vess’s pads, lines appearing across it as the map became Vess’s. The Fell bridge was there. The shelf was there.

And the western line ran to the edge without an ending.

Terro took another fresh hide.

The same line appeared.

Nobody said anything for a while.

So it was not Vess’s body, and it was not one bad hide.

The problem was somewhere else.

## Chapter 14: Off the Long Runs

“Say the rest of it,” Terro said.

We were back down at the station. The frame had been put away. Most of the audience had gone.

“There’s a third way,” said Vess.

“Where.”

“West. Out past the break, along and out. Nothing I’ve ever been sent to.”

“And?”

“And it doesn’t end.”

Terro did not say anything.

“It goes to the edge of the hide,” Vess said. “There’s no mark on it. No place, no drop, no shut, no fall. It just goes out and the drawing stops because there’s no more drawing.”

“Show me.”

Vess held it flat again. Terro bent over it a long time, long enough that the noise of the station carried on around us and somebody came and asked about a job and was waved off.

Then Terro straightened up.

“I can see it,” Terro said. “There it is. Goes out, goes to the edge, doesn’t finish.”

“You see it.”

“I’m looking straight at it.” Terro bent over the hide again, briefly. “And I can’t do a single thing about it. It’s not my way. I’ve got a different west. If I take that hide off you it stops being your map and starts being mine. Your way goes, and I’m holding a piece of hide with my own ways on it, which I already know.”

“So you can’t check it.”

“I can’t check it,” said Terro. “I can see a way I can’t get to, doing a thing I’ve never seen a way do. That’s what I’ve got.”

“That’s why I told you,” said Vess.

---

I thought Terro was about to say *I believe you.*

I thought that because Terro plainly did believe Vess. I could see it. Everybody in the station could see it. Terro had spent an afternoon confirming that Vess’s body was sound and Vess’s routes were real and Vess’s account of both was accurate. Terro had known Vess for eleven years. There was no version of this in which Terro thought Vess was lying.

Terro said, “I’m going to take you off the long runs.”


“Right,” said Vess.

“Not because I think you did anything.”

“I know.”

“I want that said out loud, because it’ll get said wrong by somebody else by tomorrow.” Terro’s voice had not changed at all. It was the same voice as *hold still* and *breathe less.* “You didn’t alter a map. I don’t think you altered a map. I think you’re the last person in Ilm who would.”

“Then?” I said.

“But something’s wrong with it,” said Terro, “and I don’t know what.”

## Chapter 15: Unexplained

I argued. Of course I argued. I had been on the Reach nineteen days and I argued with a station keeper about the profession she had been running since before Vess was born, and Terro let me do it, which was more patience than I had earned.

I said the map was correct. Terro agreed.

I said the two ordinary ways had been verified that afternoon. Terro agreed.

I said that a map showing an unfinished way was not the same as a map showing a wrong way, and that nothing on that hide had been shown to be false, and that in point of fact nobody had found a single error in anything.

“That’s right,” said Terro.

“Then it’s fine.”

“No,” said Terro. “It’s unexplained. Those aren’t the same.”

“But if nothing’s wrong?”

“I send people out on maps,” Terro said. “That’s the trade. Somebody brings me a thing that matters and I put it in a sling and I pick a courier, and the reason they get it there is the map. Not the courier. The courier’s just legs. The map is the part that’s true.”

“And Vess’s map is true.”

“Vess’s map is doing something I have never seen a map do in thirty-one years,” said Terro, “and I can’t tell you why, and neither can Vess, and neither can you. So I don’t know what else it might do.”


That was the entire argument, and there is no flaw in it.

If Vess had lied, you could deal with a liar. If the hide had gone bad, you could throw it away and get another, and everybody would have had a story to tell about it afterward.

But a station keeper cannot put *unaccounted for* behind somebody’s grain shipment, or somebody’s medicine, or somebody’s letter that has to arrive.

---

The rest happened the way water finds a low place. Slowly, and without anybody deciding it.

Vess kept working. Terro made sure of that. But the long runs went to other people, and the long runs are where the money is and where the standing is, and after a few days Vess was doing local work: short hops inside the knot, a delivery to the lower quarter, a package four spans over.

Then it was messages.

Then somebody at the market asked Vess, kindly, whether it was true that Vess’s map had gone wrong, and Vess said no, and the person said *of course, of course* in the voice of somebody being polite about it.

Then a customer asked for a different courier. Not rudely. Just asked.

And I watched Vess get very tired.

## Chapter 16: Isn’t That Interesting?

Vess’s home was on the underside, three spans out from the knot, in the row where the light comes up instead of down.

It is a good place. Open frames, braced into the rock, with the whole of Ilm hanging above you and the brightness a long way below and the wind coming through in the afternoon. There is a hook rail for slings and a hanging store for food and a work brace where Vess mended things, and there is a perch on the outer edge shaped exactly to Vess’s body, worn smooth, that Vess had been using since Vess was four segments long and had outgrown twice and re-cut twice rather than give it up.

Vess sat on it in the evenings and did not say much.

I stayed there a good deal. I was not, I think, particularly good company.


Because I had an idea, and my idea was extremely interesting, and I could not stop having it.

The map was not broken. It was doing precisely what a map does: reporting the ways available to the body holding it. And it had reported one that it could not finish.

There is only one way for that to be true.

**The way is real.**

It is real, and it is Vess’s, and it goes west past the break, and somewhere out there it does something that the entire mapping system of an entire world has no mark for. Not *closed*. Not *fallen*. Not *dangerous*. Something else. Something that has never happened often enough for anybody to invent a symbol for it.

And I hung there in the evening air outside the home of somebody whose life was quietly coming apart, and what I said out loud, more than once, was:

“Isn’t that *interesting?*”

## Chapter 17: Across the Gap

Han lived across the gap.

Not far. You could see into Han’s home from Vess’s perch: the frames, the hanging store, the green Han grew along the sunward face in three careful rows and was competitive about. Twenty body-lengths of open air, and below that the brightness, and that was all that was between them.

There was no way across.

Not a hard way. Not a dangerous way. There was no rib between the two homes, no span, no hoop, nothing. To get from Vess’s home to Han’s you went up into the knot, across the top, down the far side, out along two spans and back under, and it took most of a morning, and neither of them had ever done it.

They had been neighbors for nine years. They talked every single day.


“How’s it going,” Han said.

“Fine,” said Vess.

“Mm,” said Han.

That was most of their conversations. Han would come out in the evening and do something slow with the green, and Vess would be on the perch, and the two of them would say about nine things across twenty body-lengths of nothing and then stop, comfortably, and stay in the same air for an hour without talking.

I asked Vess once whether it was strange, never having been inside.

Vess did not understand the question. I had to ask it three ways, and when it landed Vess said, “Why would I be inside Han’s house?” and I said, well, because Han is your friend, and Vess said, “Han *is* my friend,” in the tone of somebody explaining that the sky is up.


Han had a bad shoulder joint, the third one down on the left, which had never been right. Han had a set of ways that reflected it. Short reach on that side. No good on a wide bridge.

“I’ll never do the Fell,” Han told me. “Not once. Not ever. I’ve watched about two hundred people do the Fell.”

“Does that...”

“No,” said Han, before I got to the end of it. “There’s more world than anybody gets. I got the bit with the good light on it.”


Here is what I did in those days, and I thought I was being helpful.

I went back and forth.

Vess would say something and I would drift over to Han, and Han would answer and I would drift back. Twenty body-lengths, four seconds, no effort at all. When Han wanted to send something across, a cutting off the green, a bowl that Vess had left, a piece of dried fruit because Vess was not eating properly, I carried it.

I was pleased with myself about this. I want to be honest. I thought it was rather elegant. Two people who had spent nine years unable to reach each other, and I had turned up and made it nothing, and neither of them had asked me to.

And the entire time I was doing it, I was talking about the road.

## Chapter 18: Your Life Doesn’t Depend on Maps

Because I could not stop.

I had worked out that the way west was real, and that whatever it did at its far end had no mark anywhere in a system that had marks for *shut* and *fallen* and *dangerous* and *drops away*, and that this had probably never happened before, to anyone.

And I said so. Constantly. Across a gap, to two people, one of whom had a bad joint and one of whom was losing everything.

“It’s the *not having a symbol*,” I said, going across with a cutting. “That’s the part. They’ve got a symbol for a way that’s shut. Somebody, at some point, had to invent that symbol, because a way got shut and there was nothing to draw.”

“Mm,” said Han.

“So what happened out west is a thing that has happened so rarely that nobody ever needed to draw it.” I went back. “Vess. Do you see? Whatever that is, it’s the first one. Or near enough.”

Vess was on the perch, not moving.

“Do you see?”

“I see,” said Vess.

---

Two days later Terro gave the eastern run to a courier called Semmit, who was competent and pleasant and had not done the eastern run before because it had always been Vess’s.

Vess came home and did not come out onto the perch.

I went in, which I should not have done, and found Vess in the dark part of the frame not doing anything, and said:

“I’ve been thinking about the marks.”

“Have you.”

“There’s a mark for a way that’s fallen, and a different mark for a way that’s shut. Which means somebody once decided those were different enough to need two symbols. And I keep wondering what the difference was, to them, when they decided that. Because if a way is *shut* somebody shut it, and if somebody shut it then...”

“Do you want to know what I did today,” said Vess.

I stopped.

“I took four messages to the market. Then I took a pot of something to the lower quarter for somebody’s aunt. Then I came home.” Vess still had not moved. “I’ve been a courier eleven years. I did the Fell run in bad light when I was nineteen and everybody else said no. Semmit’s doing east. Semmit’s fine. Semmit’ll be good at it.”

“Vess.”

“And every day you come and you tell me how interesting it is.”


There it was.

“You keep saying that word,” said Vess. “Interesting. Every day. Isn’t it interesting, isn’t it interesting. And it *is*. That’s the worst part. It’s the most interesting thing that’s ever happened in Ilm and I can’t get anybody to send me four spans with a parcel.”

I did not say anything.

“You go across.” Vess moved one end toward the gap, toward Han’s, where the light was still on the green. “You go across that. Nine years I’ve been shouting at Han across that and you go over it four times an evening carrying fruit and you don’t even *notice* you’re doing it.”

“I was helping.”

“I know you were.” Vess sounded tired rather than angry, which was worse. “That’s not the thing. The thing is that you can’t understand why it matters. None of it applies to you. Gaps don’t apply to you. Ways don’t apply to you. You’ve never once in your life needed a map to be right.”

And then Vess said it, flatly, without heat, in the voice of somebody reading out a weight:

“Of course this is interesting to you. Your life doesn’t depend on maps being believed.”

## Chapter 19: No. You’re Right

I had things I could have said. I was trying to help. I carried the fruit because Vess was not eating. Finding out what was out there might be the only thing that would fix any of it.

All of it was true, and none of it was the point.

What had been put in front of me was not a puzzle. It was a person, in the dark, quietly losing a life while I hovered nearby saying *isn’t that interesting.*

So I said, “No. You’re right.”

And Vess said, “Don’t...” and stopped, because there was nothing after it.

---

I did not go and look at the road.

I could have. It would have taken me an afternoon, and nobody would have known, and I thought about it for a long time that night and decided that the way west could stay where it was.

What I did instead was go to the station.


It went badly.

I had been on the Reach twenty-three days and I put myself in front of somebody with thirty-one years in the trade and argued that she should put her station’s reputation behind a map nobody could explain. I did it badly, because I was arguing for a person instead of about a problem, and I had no practice at that at all.

I said Vess was the best courier in the knot. Terro agreed.

I said Vess had reported the fault voluntarily, and that punishing that taught every courier in Ilm to keep quiet next time. Terro said it was the first good point anybody had made in two weeks, that it had already been keeping her up, and that she still could not send Vess out on a long run.

I said it was not fair.

“No,” said Terro. “It isn’t.”

“Then...”

“Fair would be somebody having done something,” Terro said. “Then I could put it right. This isn’t that. This is a thing that happened.”

## Chapter 20: The Long Way Around

I went back and told Vess I had failed.

“I know,” said Vess. “Semmit told me an hour ago. The whole station heard you.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You were rubbish at it.”

“I was.”

“Terro said you were rubbish at it.” Vess had come out onto the perch again, which I chose to take as something. “She also said nobody’s ever argued for me before. She’s been doing this thirty-one years and nobody has ever once come and shouted at her on somebody else’s behalf, and she wanted me to know that.”

Across the gap, in the last of the light, Han was doing something slow to the green and was very obviously listening.

“So,” said Vess.

“So.”

“So the way west is still out there.”

I said nothing at all, because I had promised myself I was finished with it.

“It’s *my* way,” said Vess. “It’s on my map. Nobody at the station’s got it, so nobody at the station’s going out there to look.” A pause. “And if it’s real, if there’s an actual way out there and I can stand on it, then my map didn’t invent a road. Did it.”

“No,” I said. “It didn’t.”

“Then that’s the only thing that gets my job back,” said Vess. “Not you shouting at Terro. Not Terro feeling bad. Somebody standing on it.”

Han’s voice came across the gap, unhurried, in the dark.

“You’ll want to go early,” Han said. “It’s a long way round.”

## Chapter 21: A Particular Body

We left before the light was properly up, and by the time it was, I could see where we were going.

That is the thing about the Reach. The place we were headed was out west past the break, and from the top of Ilm you could see it, not the way itself, but the country it was in, the outer ribs going away pale and thin toward the edge of everything, with nothing much on them.

It looked close.

“How long?”

“Most of the day,” said Vess.

I said that seemed unlikely, and Vess said, “You’ve been here a month,” which was a complete answer and we both knew it.


Here is how you get anywhere on the Reach.

You go down first. Almost always. The knot sits high because knots sit where the spans crowd, and the crowding is up in the middle of things, so the first hour of any journey out is spent going the opposite direction from where you want to be.

We went down the underside of the market span, past the quiet frames where the light comes up off the haze, past a man mending slings who told Vess that he had heard, and was sorry, and Vess said thanks and kept going.

Then along. Then down again.

Then we came to the slot.


The slot is a crack through the body of a rib. Not around it. Through it. About four body-lengths long, and it corkscrews.

Vess went in headfirst, which on a Reachfolk means nothing, and about halfway through began to swear steadily in a conversational tone, and came out the other side dusty and pleased.

“Can everybody do that?”

“No.” Vess was already moving. “Terro can’t. Terro goes round the top, adds two hours. Semmit could when Semmit was younger.”

“You could do it because you’re...”

“Because I fold,” said Vess. “And because I’m not carrying much. I couldn’t do that with a full sling.”

Which was why the food was in two lots.

Vess went through with the first, left it on the far side, came back through empty, took the second, and went through again. Three trips for one hole, about twenty minutes, and Vess did not appear to think this was worth remarking on. Loaded, that slot was not a way at all. Empty, it was. The difference was a bag.

I thought about that for a while.

Vess had planned the entire journey around being able to fit through a hole. Not around distance. Not around difficulty. Around whether, at each specific point, on that specific day, carrying that specific weight, a particular body would go through a particular gap.

That is what a map is for. It took a month and a slot to get that into me.

## Chapter 22: The Other Side of the Rib

By midmorning we were on the underside of a long rib with the world upside down above us and the brightness below, and it is the best thing I have seen on any world.

The Reachfolk travel undersides constantly. It is not brave. It is not remarkable. The pads hold, and the stone is stone, and the fact that there is nothing underneath you for as far as anybody has ever gone is simply a fact about where you live, the way the sea is a fact if you live by it.

Vess went along the underside for two hours, at a good pace, complaining about the wind.

Above us. Below us. I do not have the word. On the other side of the rib we were under, hanging down from it, were the anchor notches of a road that had been cut on the top surface a long time ago, and their points had come through the stone in a regular line, and Vess navigated by them without appearing to notice.

“They come through?”

“Sometimes. If they cut deep.” Vess did not slow down. “Good marker. Means you’re under the Merrin road, means the elbow’s about forty on.”

I looked at the little regular points of stone coming through from a road I could not see, made by people who had been dead for a long time, and thought about how much work it is to make a way.


We stopped at a resting frame at midday and I asked Vess whether it was strange, going somewhere nobody goes.

“It’s not strange. It’s just far.” Vess was eating, and eating properly for the first time in a while, which I noticed and did not comment on. “There’s nothing out there. That’s all. Nothing to fetch, nobody to fetch it for. Nobody’s been out past the break in... I don’t know. Terro’d know.”

“Then why is there a way?”

“There’s ways everywhere. Most of the world’s ways nobody uses.” Vess gestured at the whole lattice with one end, which is a very economical piece of body language. “Ribs go where they go. Doesn’t mean anybody wants them.”

I said that was true of ribs.

I said it was less obviously true of roads.

Vess stopped eating.

## Chapter 23: The Shoulder

We got to the shoulder in the last quarter of the day.

The shoulder is a broad turn of rock where the northern ribs come round and stop. A good place, flat, with a view west over everything. Anybody can get to the shoulder. It takes a long time, but the way is wide and there is nothing on it that asks anything of your body.

And from the shoulder to the outer rib there is a gap.

It is not a large gap. It is the kind of gap that the Reach is full of, which is to say that it is entirely a question of who you are: a bridging reach across open air onto a stone lip that comes up at an awkward angle, with nothing under it for as far as anybody has ever gone.

Vess looked at it for a moment and then made it, in one movement, and was on the other side.

“Could you have done that before the change?”

“No.” Vess said it without any particular weight. “Not with this last bit. Bit short. Semmit couldn’t either. Semmit’s got the length but not the bend. You’d come in wrong.” A pause. “Terro definitely couldn’t. There’ll be somebody in Ilm who could. I don’t know who. It’s not the sort of thing that comes up.”

I went across it without touching anything, which took no time at all and cost nothing, and I did not say anything about that either.


Beyond the gap it was an outer rib, thin by the standards of the Reach, running west and slightly down. There was nothing on it. There had been nothing on any of the four ribs before it, and the country out here was empty in a way that Ilm had not prepared me for. No knots, no frames, no green, no bodies moving anywhere in any direction. Just pale stone going away into bright air.

Vess had the hide out and was working along the line on it, and stopped, and did not say anything.

“What.”

“Look at the surface,” said Vess.

I looked at the surface.

It was flat down the middle.

## Chapter 24: That’s Road

There were notches.

Two pad-widths apart, in a repeating line, along both edges, worn shallow, filled with a fine pale dust, but cut. Deliberately. In sequence. And beyond them, at the outer margin, the grooves, and forty body-lengths further on the wreck of a resting frame with its bars gone and its anchor sockets still in the rock.

Nobody had touched any of it in a very long time. The flat had weathered until it was nearly the same color as the rib. Two of the notches had broken through. Something had come down on the frame at some point and taken most of it away.

But you could read it from where we were. You could read it at a glance, from a considerable distance, in bad light, which is the entire purpose of a road.

“That’s road,” said Vess.

“That’s road.”

“That’s *road*.” Vess had gone completely still again, all the limbs at once. “Out here. Where there’s nothing. Somebody cut a road out here.”

I said that somebody had cut a road out here, and had put in resting frames, and had taken years over it.

Vess said, “Who for?”

And I did not have an answer, and neither did Vess. We stayed there in the last of the light with the whole empty west of the world in front of us, Vess on the road and me above it. The road went on ahead of us going somewhere, the way roads do.

## Chapter 25: It’s Still Going

We slept the night and went on at first light.

The road ran west along the top of the rib, and it got better as we went, which was not what I expected. Nearer Ilm the work had been worn nearly flat. Out here the notches were sharper. The grooves had edges on them. Whoever made this had finished the far end and worked backward, or had made the far end later, or had cared more about it.

There were four more resting frames. The third one still had its bars.

Vess did not say much. Vess was reading the road the way you would read handwriting: going a little out of the way to look at a joint in the stonework, stopping at the frames, once crouching over a place where the flat had been cut around an outcrop instead of through it.

“That’s a choice,” Vess said. “Going round. Costs you a month, that does. Somebody decided.”

---

By midday the rib was thinning under us and I could see the end of it.

Everybody in Ilm knew where the world stopped in the west. It stopped out here, at the far ribs, where the stone ran out and there was nothing beyond but the bright air going on to wherever bright air goes. Nobody thought about it much. It was simply the edge, the way a coast is the edge, and the only remarkable thing about it was that nobody had ever needed to give it a name.

The road went straight at it.

Not toward the end of the rib and then off to one side. Not curving away. The road ran west along the middle of the stone, dead level, notched on both sides, and ahead of us the rib narrowed and the light got wider and the road did not turn.

Vess said, “It’s still going.”


Then the road stopped, and the rib stopped, and they stopped in the same place.

The stone ended in a single flat plane running from one edge of the rib to the other and down out of sight beneath it. The plane was square across the road. It was smooth in a way that no weathered stone on that world was smooth.

Not broken. Not fallen. Cut.

And the road ran right into it.

The flat of the road went all the way up and finished where the face was. The notches came along both edges in their sequence, two pad-widths apart, unbroken, and the last one on the left was a half-notch. Not a notch somebody had started and abandoned. A finished notch with half of it gone, sheared down the middle by the plane, the cut side as smooth as the face and the other side still showing the chisel.

The same on the right, a little further along, because the sequence did not line up square with the cut.

And in the road surface, sunk in a line down the center, were anchor sockets. Empty, regularly spaced, running forward, and the last of them was a half-socket, open along its length like a cup cut lengthwise, with its bottom half still in the stone and its top half somewhere else entirely.

Behind us, forty body-lengths back, was the last resting frame.

## Chapter 26: Somebody Stopped It Here

Vess went up to the face and put a pad on it and took it off again.

“That’s not a fall,” Vess said.

“No.”

“And it’s not a drop. A drop gives you another rib below. There’s nothing there.” Vess went along the base of the face, one side to the other, all the way to where the rib fell away. “A fall isn’t flat either. And it’s not shut. Shut is when somebody bars a way. There’s nothing barred. There’s no way left to bar.”

I said that was right.

“So what’s the mark for it?”

I said I did not think there was one.

“No,” said Vess. “No, there isn’t.”


So this is what was in front of us.

A road was built here. It was built by people who cut anchor sockets in a sequence and put resting frames every so often and went round an outcrop rather than through it because that was the better job. They worked west. They were still working west at the point where the stone ends, because the last notches are half-cut and the sockets run on past them.

And the last notch was finished before it was cut in half.

You cannot half-make a notch that way. A notch you gave up on is rough at the end. This one was chiseled clean along one side and sheared flat along the other, and a socket cannot be sunk into stone that has already been taken away.

So the work came first, and the cut came second.

They built a road here, going west, and they finished this stretch of it. Then something took the rest of the rib off, and half of a notch, and half of a socket, and whatever the road was going toward.

Somebody stopped it here.

## Chapter 27: Zero

Vess did not see the next part, and I did not mention it.

I went close to the face, closer than Vess had gone, right up against it, and along it, and around to the underside of the rib where the plane came down and kept going and ended the stone there too.

And I measured the edge, where the face met the top of the rib.

Here is a thing I know, because measuring is the only skill I have ever had. **Every corner in the world is round.** Not round like a ball. Round like *a little bit*. Get close enough to the corner of a table, or a cut stone, or a chipped bowl, and the sharp part turns out to be a tiny curve. You can always find the curve. There is always a place where the top has stopped being the top and has not yet become the side. It might be no wider than a hair. It is never nothing.

I went along that edge with my whole attention, from one side of the rib to the other, as close as I have ever put myself to anything.

There was no curve.

The top of the rib was the top of the rib, and then it was the face, and there was no place in between where it was becoming the face. The distance from one to the other was zero. Not small. **Zero.** A thing with a zero in it is not a thing that got made by cutting stone, because a cut leaves an edge and an edge is a curve if you look hard enough.

I have seen a zero like that before.

It is at the place where the between-thing ends and a world starts. I have gone past it more times than I can account for without once stopping to look, because I was always arriving somewhere and there was always something to see.

I hung in front of that face for a long time.


Then I understood it, all at once, the way you understand a thing you have been standing next to for years.

The crossings are made.

Somebody built them. They have edges that nothing natural has, and I have gone through more of them than I can account for without once asking what they were, the way you would go through doors all your life without asking who hung them.

And here, on a rib at the western end of a world, there is a road that was built to keep going, and a cut that stopped it, and the cut has the same edge on it as the doors do.

Somebody made the ways between worlds.

And somebody closed this one.

## Chapter 28: Nothing to Finish

“Right,” said Vess.

I turned round.

Vess was back down the road a little, at the last of the anchor sockets, bent over it, entirely absorbed. Not looking at the face at all.

“Right,” Vess said again. “That’s the sequence, see. There. And there. And then it goes into the cut.” Vess straightened up. “You can’t argue with that. That’s not a natural anything. That’s somebody’s work, going into a thing that stopped it.”

“Yes.”

“Terro will see that.” Vess was talking quickly now, and it was the first time in three weeks that Vess had sounded like the person I met at the station. “Terro won’t be able to get here. She can’t do the slot and can’t make the Fell. She’d have to come the northern way and that’s two days. But she can get to the shoulder back there. She can see the flat from the shoulder. She can see the frames.”

“She can.”

“My map didn’t invent a road.” Vess said it to nobody in particular. “That’s a road. It’s the oldest road I’ve ever been on and it’s a road, and it’s on my map because it’s *there*.”

Vess looked back at the face once, briefly, the way you look at bad weather.

“Anyway,” said Vess. “That’s why it doesn’t finish. Nothing to finish.”

And went to look at the frames.


I did not say anything.

There was nobody on that rib to say it to. Vess had eleven years of maps and a job to get back and had just found the thing that would do it. Vess was right. The sockets and the frames and the flat would put Vess back on the long runs, and none of that had anything to do with what the edge of that face was.

I could have said: *the thing that cut this is the same kind of thing I come here through.*

And Vess would have said, *what?*

And I would have had to explain what a crossing is, and that I go through them, and that I do not know how, and that I have never looked at one properly until today. Vess would have listened, because Vess is polite and because Vess owed me an afternoon. At the end of it Vess would still have had to get Terro out to the shoulder before the season turned.

So I said, “Do you want to go up on the shoulder now, and check the sightline?”

“Yes,” said Vess. “That’s a good thought.”

And we went and checked the sightline, and it was a good sightline, and Vess wrote the bearing on the back of the hide in a small careful hand.

Behind us the road went west into a flat stone face with no depth at its edge, and the light came off the empty air beyond it, and nobody said anything about it at all.

## Chapter 29: But It’s There

It took four days to get Terro out there.

Not because Terro did not want to go. Because Terro runs a station, and a station does not stop, and it took that long to find two days when somebody else could sit on the job board.

Then there was the route. Terro cannot do the slot, so Terro went the northern way, which is longer and easier and adds most of a day. Vess went with her. I went ahead and came back and went ahead again, which is the only useful thing I have ever been able to do on that world and which I had by then learned not to be smug about.

Semmit came too. Semmit could do the slot, and did, and was insufferable about it for an hour.


They could not get to the road.

That is the part that matters. Everybody can get to the shoulder. It is wide and flat and asks nothing of anybody, if you are willing to spend the day going north.

Nobody who came that day could make the gap beyond it. Terro was never going to. Semmit had the length, and came at it twice, and did not like the angle, and stopped, which Semmit said out loud and nobody argued with, because on the Reach that is not an admission of anything.

Terro’s map showed no way past the shoulder. Semmit’s showed none either. So they went out onto the shoulder and looked west.

And from the shoulder you can see the rib.


“There,” said Vess.

Terro did not say anything for a while.

You could see the flat. In that light you could see it clearly: a strip down the middle of the stone going away west, paler than the rock either side of it, straight in a way that stone is not straight. You could see two of the resting frames, one of them still with its bars. You could see, if you knew what to look for, the regular interruption along both edges where the notches ran.

“That’s a road,” said Semmit.

“That’s a road,” said Terro.

Then Terro got out her own map and unfolded it and looked at it, and folded it up again.

“It isn’t on my map,” she said.

And then, after a moment:

“But it’s there.”

## Chapter 30: The Trade

That was all of it.

There was no apology, and Vess did not want one, and if you had offered Terro the chance to have decided differently three weeks ago she would have said no, and been right to.

What there was instead was arithmetic.

“Say it back to me,” Terro said, still looking west. “Your map showed a way. The way is there. You went along it and it went where the hide said it went, the whole distance.”

“The whole distance.”

“And it stops because the world stops.”

“Because the world stops,” said Vess. “Straight across. There’s nothing after it to draw.”

Terro thought about that for a while.

“Then it’s not making things up,” she said. “That’s what I couldn’t tell before. A map that invents one way invents two, and I’d never know which. This one didn’t invent anything. It gave you a real way and it hasn’t got a mark for what’s on the end of it, and there isn’t a mark, because nobody’s ever needed one.”

“So it’s just the ending.”

“It’s just the ending,” said Terro. “I can work with a map that can’t finish a sentence. I can’t work with a map that lies.”

She looked west a while longer, and asked Vess three questions about the frames, and then said, “Right. The Merrin run’s yours again from Thirdday. And I want you back on the eastern when Semmit’s done the season, and I’ll tell the market myself.”

“You don’t have to tell the market.”

“I put you off the long runs in front of nine people,” said Terro. “So I’ll put you back on in front of nine people. That’s not a kindness, that’s the trade.”

Semmit said, “Does this mean I lose east,” and Terro said, “Eventually,” and Semmit said something under her body that I did not catch and Vess did, and laughed for the first time in about a month.

---

We went back the northern way with everybody, which took two days and was extremely pleasant.

Nobody talked about the face.

Terro asked what was at the end. Of course she did. Vess said the rib ends and the road stops at a flat place. Terro said, “Ends how,” and Vess said, “Straight across. Very tidy.” Terro said, “Huh,” in the way you would about a peculiar rock formation, and asked whether the frames out there were worth salvaging. Vess said probably not, and that was the end of it.

There is no story in Ilm about the cut face at the western end of the world. There is a story about a courier whose map went strange, and about the station keeper who took her off the long runs, and about how she turned out to be right and got them back. People tell it. It is not a very good story, because everybody in it behaves reasonably.

Nobody has been back out there. There is nothing to fetch.

## Chapter 31: Map’s Doing Its Best

Vess’s map still shows it.

That was the last thing, and I only found out about it because I asked. The way west is still on the hide, still Vess’s, still perfectly usable, still running to the edge of the sheet.

And it still has no ending on it.

“Doesn’t it bother you?”

“It did.” Vess was checking a load; the Merrin run went out that morning. “Now I know what’s out there, so I know what it’s not telling me. That’s different.”

“It’s still the only way on the Reach with no ending.”

“It’s the only way on the Reach with nothing at the end of it,” said Vess. “Map’s doing its best.”

And Vess went to work.

---

I stayed another nine days.

Nib had entirely stopped talking about the high bend and had started on a different bend, out under the eastern quarter, which Nib assured me was much harder and which four separate adults told me was easier. Han’s green had come on. Semmit kept the eastern run for the season and Vess kept saying she would lose it eventually and Semmit kept saying *eventually* in a voice like a slammed door, and the two of them were plainly going to be friends about it.

On the last evening I went and hung by Vess’s perch, across from Han, in the hour when the light drops and the whole world stops to find out what it can still do.

Vess said, “You’re going.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

And I said that I did not know, which is true, and has been true every time.

“Huh,” said Vess. “You’d want a map.”

## Chapter 32: The Whole Way

I left the next morning from the top of the knot, early, before the light was properly up.

Here is what I had never done before, in all the crossings I can remember making.

I looked at it.

Not through it. At it. At the place where the air stops being the Reach’s air, at the thing I have gone through more times than I can account for and have never once stopped to examine, because there was always somewhere on the other side that needed looking at first.

I went along it slowly. I put myself level with the edge of it and looked down its length.

I could not tell. That is the honest answer, and I dislike it. The light was wrong, or I was, or a thing seen from inside is not the same as a thing seen from a rib at the end of a world. There was an edge, and it may have had no depth in it, and I looked at it for a long time and I could not swear to it either way.

But I know what I am looking for now.

Somebody made a road at the western end of the Reach and meant it to go further. Somebody stopped it, with a cut that has an edge like nothing that world has ever grown.

And I have gone through more doors than I can account for without once asking who hung them.

I went through the next one.

I looked at it the whole way.
