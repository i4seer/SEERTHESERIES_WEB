## Chapter 1: Nobody Looks Down

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to seven worlds and I am carrying one thing I did not have before, which is that a mark I keep finding, a word I heard in a story nobody believed, and the crossings I go through are all one thing. I do not know what the thing is. I have stopped expecting the next world to tell me.

This one was hot.

---

It was the middle of the day and the light came down like something being poured.

Flat country in every direction, pale and dry, going out to a haze with nothing in it. A town of white stone sitting on the flat: low buildings, broad courtyards, streets laid out straight so the wind could get through, and everything so bright that I had to stop for a while before I could make out anything at all.

There was a road going out across the plain. It did not appear to be going anywhere in particular. It simply left, and kept leaving, until the haze took it.

And there were people, and the people had shadows, and it took me about ninety seconds to notice what the shadows were doing.

---

They were tall and thin and they walked on two legs, and they had two arms and a face and a way of standing that I could read without being taught, which after the last world was restful. The one distinguishing thing about them is a pair of long fins on the head, set high, that lie back when a person is calm and come forward when they are paying attention.

I mention that because of what it does to a shadow.

You can tell a Seth from their shadow at forty paces. Everybody’s outline is unmistakable: the long body, the fins, the particular way a given person stands. There is no mistaking one for another and no mistaking any of them for anything else.

Which is unfortunate, given what the shadows are doing.

## Chapter 2: Pointed the Wrong Way

Here is the first one I looked at properly.

Two people were standing in a courtyard talking about a delivery. I know they were talking about a delivery because I was directly above them and I listened to the whole of it, and it was about sacks, and how many, and when.

The first one’s shadow was doing that too. Standing where he was standing, moving when he moved.

The second one’s shadow had turned around.

Not a little. All the way around, so that it was facing back across the courtyard at a third person over by a doorway, somebody who was not part of the conversation and had not looked up, and it was standing there with its fins forward, attending, the way you do when you are waiting for somebody to notice you.

The person casting it went on talking about sacks the entire time.

I went down and around to check the sun, because that is the sort of thing I do, and the sun was where it should have been and the shadow was where it should have been, and it was pointed the wrong way inside its own outline, which is not a thing shadows can do.

I was extremely pleased.

---

“Excuse me,” I said. “Your shadow is looking at that person over there.”

The one I said it to did not jump. There was no jump at all.

“You’ll be the floating thing,” he said. “You want the water? There’s water at the far end, past the row.”

“I said your shadow is...”

“Because you look like you’ve come across the flat,” he said, “and if you’ve come across the flat you’ll want the water.”

And he went back to the sacks.

## Chapter 3: That One’s Hiding

I tried three more times that afternoon, because I am not easily put off and because I could not believe my luck.

**The second one** was a woman putting up a stall. I told her that her shadow had crouched down and was doing something at the base of the wall while she was standing upright with her arms above her head. She said the light out here takes some getting used to and asked me where I had come from, and then asked me two more things about where I had come from, and then a third, and by the time I got back to it she had gone inside for more cloth.

**The third** was a man I found alone, which I thought would help. I told him what I had seen and asked him straight out what it meant. He said, “Hm,” and looked at me, and then said he had to be somewhere, and went there.

**The fourth** did not acknowledge that I had spoken at all. She talked pleasantly to me for some minutes about the caravans and the season and a person called Debb who had opinions about rope, and she was not being rude, and there was no gap anywhere in what she was saying, and my question went into it and did not come out.

Four people. Four subjects, all different, all arriving very fast.

---

Then a child solved it for me.

It was small and loud and going past at speed, and it stopped dead in the middle of the street and pointed at somebody’s shadow on a wall and said, at considerable volume:

“That one’s *hiding*.”

It was, too. Its owner was buying something. The shadow had got itself behind the doorframe with only the fins showing.

An adult I had not noticed put a hand on the child’s head and turned it about forty degrees, the way you would turn a tap, and carried on saying whatever she had been saying to the person beside her without any pause at all. The child went off sideways at speed and was shouting about something else within four steps.

Nobody looked at the wall.

## Chapter 4: I Would Show Them

I spent the rest of that day going up and down that town in the heat, and by the evening I had it, and I was so pleased with myself that I said it out loud to nobody, hanging over a courtyard while the light went long and orange.

**They cannot see them.**

That was my conclusion, arrived at in one day, on the evidence of four adults and one child.

The shadows are doing extraordinary things: reaching, hiding, turning around, sitting down when their owners are standing, walking on the spot while their owners do not move at all. And the people they belong to go about their business as though the ground were empty.

And the children *can* see them, because children look at everything, and the adults take the children by the head and turn them away from the wall, because you do not encourage a child to talk nonsense.

**They have lost it.** That was my finding. Somewhere between being small and being grown, they stop being able to look at the most interesting thing in their entire world, and the whole town has agreed that there is nothing there, because for them there isn’t.

It was the best thing I had ever worked out.

---

And what I decided to do about it followed from that.

I would show them.

I had learned by then that noticing something about another person did not automatically make it mine to say. I thought this was different. I thought they simply could not see.

Not as a trick. They were friendly, they gave directions, one of them had spent four minutes telling me about somebody’s opinions about rope for no reason I could see. I thought: *these people have a whole thing happening around their feet all day and nobody has told them.*

So I decided that in the morning I would find somebody who would listen, and I would point at a shadow, and I would say what it was doing, and I would keep saying it until they saw it too.

The light went, and the town did not get dark so much as change hands. Lamps came out along the courtyards, small and yellow, set down low.

I watched a man arrange three of them around a table where people were eating. He moved each one twice. When he had finished, every shadow in that courtyard was lying flat on the floor under the table, where you would have to get down on the ground to look at it.

He did it the way you would straighten a rug. Then he sat down and ate.

I thought it was about the heat.

Down at the far end, past the row of stalls, four children were shouting something at each other with a rhythm to it, and getting louder, and somebody two roofs over told them to stop. I did not attend to it. There was a great deal of shouting in that town and none of it had been about shadows.

I went to find somewhere to be until morning.

## Chapter 5: The Work Row

The row is where the town works.

It is a line of open stalls under one long wall, running east from the middle of the town, and it is where you go if a thing you own has stopped doing what it is for. Somebody along there will mend it, or know who does, or tell you at length that it was never any good and that they said so at the time.

I went along it in the morning looking for somebody who would listen to me, and I got as far as the fourth stall.

---

“You’ll be the floating thing,” said the old one.

“Everybody keeps saying that.”

“Everybody keeps being right.” He had a bracket in one hand and something bent in the other and he was doing a thing to the join. “You want to come out of the light. There’s shade here and you’re welcome to it.”

His name was Hannit and he sat on a low frame with his legs out because standing was not what it had been. He talked the entire time. He talked about the wind, which he was against, and about the caravans, which come through twice a season and which he had opinions about, and about a person called Debb, three stalls down, who had opinions about rope that Hannit considered a public nuisance.

“You’ll meet Debb.”

“Probably.”

“You will,” said Hannit. “Everybody does. Debb makes sure of it.”

---

The other one at that stall was thirteen.

Tavi did the standing up. That was the arrangement and neither of them mentioned it, and I would not have noticed it at all in the first hour except that I count things.

Hannit had the bracket. Tavi got the bracket from the shelf, and got the pin from the box, and went out to the front when somebody came, and came back, and put a hand on the frame when Hannit shifted his weight without either of them looking at each other.

They were funny together. They had a routine going that they had plainly been running for a long time: Hannit saying something enormous about the state of the world, Tavi cutting the whole thing down to about four words without any particular effort, and Hannit being delighted about it.

“This town has no idea what it looks like from outside.”

“It looks white,” said Tavi.

“That is not...”

“It looks white and it’s hot.”

“You are thirteen years old and have no conversation,” said Hannit, happily, and told me about the wind again.

## Chapter 6: Helping Out

I settled into their stall very quickly.

I also worked out within about an hour that the stall ran on Tavi.

Not in a way anybody was hiding. Hannit could not do the lifting. Hannit could not do the ladder. Hannit did the work that gets done sitting down, which is a good deal of it and requires the more skill, and Tavi did every single thing that required a person to be upright, and both of them referred to this as *helping out*.

Nobody had decided it. You could see that too. It had arrived one job at a time until it was simply how the stall worked, and everybody in the row was used to it, and the only person who would have been surprised to hear it described out loud was Hannit.

---

Somebody came in with a lamp bracket and I got my chance and took it.

The customer’s shadow, while she stood there discussing the bracket, had got down on the ground and was doing something at the base of the wall, reaching under it, or trying to.

“Excuse me,” I said. “Your shadow’s...”

And Tavi was in front of me.

I do not know how. I did not see the movement. One moment I had a clear view of that wall and the next there was a thirteen-year-old between me and it, holding a lamp bracket up at exactly the height that a floating thing would have to go around, asking me whether the caravans went where I had come from.

“I’m sorry?”

“The caravans. Do they go there? Where you came from.” Tavi had turned very slightly, so the customer was now behind them. “Because they go a long way. Some of them go a long way. You could ask.”

“I don’t come by caravan.”

“How do you come?”

And I told them, because it is a good story and I have not often been asked, and by the time I had finished the customer had gone and the wall was empty and I had entirely lost my thread.

## Chapter 7: A Reflex That Fast

That happened four more times before midday.

I would start on a shadow and Tavi would arrive with a question, with an object, with a sudden need to be exactly where I was. Every time it was smooth and fast and entirely reasonable, and every time I came out the other side of it talking about something else.

By the fourth I had noticed. By the fifth I had a theory.

**The child is embarrassed.**

That was my theory. It fit. Children see the shadows, adults have lost the knack, and a thirteen-year-old is exactly the age to be caught in between: old enough to know that talking about it makes you a baby, young enough to still be looking. So Tavi hears me start, and gets in front of it, the way you would steer a conversation away from something a small brother had said.

I thought it was rather decent of them.

---

Then Nub arrived, who is six.

Nub came down the row at speed, stopped dead in front of Hannit’s stall, pointed at the wall behind Debb three stalls down, and said:

“Debb’s is *upside down*.”

It was. I checked.

Debb, who I had not met yet and who turned out to be exactly as advertised, said, “Nub, go and ask your mother about the ladder,” without turning round.

Nub went two steps, stopped, pointed at a different wall, and said, “That one’s *pulling*.”

A woman going past put a hand on Nub’s head and turned it, mid-sentence, in a conversation she was having with somebody else about a delivery, and did not break off, and Nub went sideways at speed shouting about the ladder.

Two adults. Under a minute. Not one of them looked at a wall.

---

I put that in the same box as everything else and got it wrong in the same direction.

*They are so used to children talking nonsense that they have a reflex for it.* That is what I thought. I thought it was rather sad. A town so certain there is nothing to see that they have built a small automatic courtesy for turning children away from it.

I was pleased with myself for noticing the reflex. I did not once ask why a reflex that fast would need to exist.

## Chapter 8: Facing East

The thing happened in the afternoon and it took about four seconds.

Hannit was talking. Hannit was always talking. He was saying something to Tavi about a job promised to somebody for the far end of the season, a set of shutters or a door, and it was the least interesting thing in the row.

“And they’ll want it before the caravans come back through, so that’s yours, that is, that’s a whole day’s work in that.”

Tavi said, “Mm.”

And Tavi’s shadow, on the wall behind them, put down what it was holding, picked up a small bundle from beside its feet, put the bundle over one shoulder, and began to walk.

Not away. That is not a thing they do. It walked on the spot, in the place where a shadow goes, with its fins forward and the bundle up. It was walking hard, the way you walk when you have a distance in front of you, and it was facing east, along the row and out, in the direction of the road.

“Oh,” I said. “Look at...”

And Tavi was in front of me.

---

“How do you come, though?” Tavi said. “You said. You said it isn’t a caravan. I want to know how you come.”

“I told you this morning.”

“Tell me again.”

So I told them again, and Tavi listened to the whole of it with their back to the wall and their fins right forward, and did not look at me once, and asked three questions about a thing I had already explained.

By the time we had finished, Hannit had moved on to the wind.

---

I did not think about it again that day.

I noticed a shadow doing something interesting, I got interrupted, and I filed it with the other forty interesting things I had seen since arriving, and in the evening I went off to look at the lamps again.

What I thought about instead was Tavi. I thought: *that child works too hard, and is embarrassed about the shadows, and has had to hear about the wind approximately nine thousand times.*

Down at the end of the row, four children were arguing about who was starting. One of them was shouting a string of questions at the others and the others were shouting the answers back, all of it fast and flat and worn smooth, the way children say a thing they have said a great many times.

They got to the end. Somebody started. They ran off.

## Chapter 9: Entirely Happy

The row at midday is the busiest place in that town.

Everybody comes at once, because everybody has worked out the same thing about the heat, and for about an hour there is a queue at every stall and people standing about in the shade of the long wall waiting to be dealt with.

At Hannit’s stall that morning there were five of them: a woman with a lamp bracket, two men who had come together about a hinge and disagreed with each other about it continuously, somebody’s grandmother waiting for a pot, and Debb, who did not need anything and had come to say something about a ladder and had stayed to listen to the hinge argument.

Tavi was doing the standing up. Hannit had the pot.

And behind all of it, past the end of the row, out beyond the last of the white buildings, the road went off across the flat and did not stop.

You can see it from that stall. You can see it from most of the row. It is the only thing out there: one pale line going east through the haze until the haze wins. The caravans come in along it twice a season and go out along it again, and take on people who want to go, and everybody in that town has been watching it their whole lives without particularly noticing they are doing it.

I had come across it myself. It had not once occurred to me to think about it.

---

I was up above the awning being useless and enjoying myself.

I had by then a list of about nine shadows I intended to talk to somebody about, and I had worked out that mornings were better than afternoons for it, and I had noticed that Tavi got in front of me less when there were other people about, which I took to be embarrassment thinning out in a crowd.

I was, that morning, entirely happy.

## Chapter 10: Everybody Saw It

The hinge argument finished. The two men went away still disagreeing. The grandmother got her pot and stayed to look at it.

And Hannit, who talks, said the thing he said.

“That’s the shutters for Merrat’s place after this,” he said, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man working through a list. “And then the racks. We’ll want another rack before the caravans come back through. You’ll still be here when the dry season starts, we’ll do them then.”

He was not asking anything. There was no question anywhere in it.

Tavi said, “Mm.”

---

And the shadow put down the bracket it was holding, and picked up the bundle, and put it over one shoulder, and started walking east.

It walked hard. It had its fins forward. It went nowhere at all, the way they do, but it went nowhere *east*, along the wall, toward the end of the row and the last buildings and the road going out across the flat.

**Everybody saw it.**

Every single person under that awning: the grandmother, Debb, the woman with the lamp bracket who had never met Tavi before.

And all of them looked away at the same moment.

Not one at a time. Together, in about the space of a breath. The grandmother looked down at her pot, the woman looked out at the street, Debb looked sideways at nothing in particular, and then all of them talking again, immediately, about four different things.

I saw that happen and I thought: *how strange, they have all decided to be busy at once.*

---

So I said it.

“Your shadow keeps leaving whenever he talks about next season.”

## Chapter 11: Nothing Happened

Nothing happened.

Nobody shouted. Nobody turned round. Nobody told me to be quiet, or asked me what I meant, or looked at the wall.

What happened was that the grandmother asked Debb a question about the ladder, and Debb answered it, and the woman with the lamp bracket said that she would come back for it, and did not take it with her, and went.

The grandmother finished with the pot and said it was very good and paid for it and went.

Debb said something to Hannit about the hinge, in a completely ordinary voice, and Hannit answered in a completely ordinary voice, and neither of them was talking about the hinge.

Within a minute there was nobody under that awning but the three of us, and I have never in my life seen a place empty out while everybody in it was being so polite.

---

Hannit did not say anything.

He sat where he was with the pot cloth still in his hand and he did not look at Tavi, and he did not look at the wall, and he did not look at me. He looked out at the street, at the middle distance, where there was nothing.

He is a man who talks. He had talked without stopping since the day I met him.

He sat there for a long time and did not say one word.

---

Tavi did not say anything either.

Tavi picked up the bracket the shadow had put down, the real one off the bench, and turned it over, and put it back, and did not do anything else with it.

And on the wall, the shadow stopped walking.

It stopped, and it stood still, with the bundle still up on its shoulder, facing east.

It did not put the bundle down.

## Chapter 12: Don’t

“I only meant,” I said, “that it does it whenever...”

“Don’t,” said Tavi.

---

I went up out of the row after that, because it seemed like the thing to do, and because nobody had told me to stay.

I was *confused*.

I had said a true thing. I had checked it three times. The shadow did do that, and it did do it whenever the season came up, and I had watched it happen twice more and been prevented from mentioning it twice, and I had assumed the preventing was shyness.

Nobody had contradicted me. Nobody had said *no it doesn’t*. Nobody had said *that’s not what it means*.

They had simply all gone somewhere else, very politely, at speed.

And Tavi, who I liked, had said one word to me and it was *don’t*, and I did not know what I had done, and there was nobody in that town I could ask, because the only person who had ever answered a direct question about a shadow was six years old.

---

I went out along the row in the late part of the afternoon and the awning shade was long and everybody was working and nobody said anything to me at all.

Which was normal. That was the thing. It was exactly as normal as it had been the day before, in every particular, and I could not find one single thing that had changed.

Behind me, at the fourth stall, an old man who talks was not talking, and a thirteen-year-old was doing the standing up, and on the wall between them a shadow stood facing east with a bundle on its shoulder and did not move all afternoon.

## Chapter 13: Nobody Said Tavi’s Name

Nobody came to tell me what I had done.

I had assumed somebody would. On the fourth world a man explained my mistake to me at considerable length and it was one of the more useful afternoons I have had.

Here I waited two days and nobody came, and I decided they were being kind.

---

Tavi did not come to the stall for two days.

Hannit worked alone. He got there earlier than usual, because everything takes longer if you are doing it sitting down and then getting up, and he did the getting up about forty times a day, and he did not complain once, which I had never known him manage for an hour.

He did not talk either. Not properly. He said the right things to customers and then stopped.

---

The row was kind to him and did not appear to be doing anything at all.

I watched it for two days before I had any idea what I was looking at.

Somebody from the next stall along came and borrowed a thing he did not need and brought it back an hour later, and while he had it he carried three heavy items from the back of Hannit’s stall to the front, and put them down where they would be wanted, and said nothing about it.

Debb came four times in one morning about a ladder that was never mentioned again.

A woman with two children going past sent one of them in to ask whether Hannit wanted anything from the far end, which the child clearly did not want to do and did anyway, twice.

Somebody left a covered dish on the bench and went away before Hannit could turn round.

**Nobody said Tavi’s name.** Not once, in two days, in a row of eleven stalls where everybody talks continuously about everybody.

## Chapter 14: Everything Else

They talked about everything else.

I listened to three conversations on the second morning and every one of them was about Tavi and not one of them said so.

The first was two women at the end of the row discussing whether the caravans were early this year. They went at it for some time. They agreed the caravans were slightly early, and that this was usual, and that the ones that come early take on more people because they have further to go afterward, and that a season is not very long. Then they talked about somebody’s roof.

The second was a man asking Debb whether Hannit needed a hand with the heavy work. Debb said Hannit had never needed a hand with anything in his life and would say so at length if you offered, and that somebody ought to offer anyway, and that it should probably not be Debb, because of the ladder.

The third was Hannit himself, to a customer, saying that the racks would get done when they got done and there was no hurry on them, and that he had been in two minds about the racks, and that a person could make too much of racks.

**Hannit had wanted those racks since before Tavi stopped coming.** I had heard him want them.

## Chapter 15: A Very Expensive Habit

It arrived in pieces and it took two days.

The one that did it was the glance. Five people had looked away at the same instant. Not one after another. At the same instant. You cannot do that by accident. Five people do not independently decide to be busy in the same quarter-second unless all five were watching the same thing and all five knew what it was.

And once I had that, the rest went over like a row of stacked tiles.

The lamps. Nub’s head turned away before the woman doing it had stopped talking. Four adults who had all changed the subject and not one of whom had said *no it isn’t.*

**None of it was accident.**

Everybody under that awning saw Tavi’s shadow pick up its bundle, and every one of them knew what it meant, and all of them looked away on purpose.

And I had come along and said it out loud.

---

I could not work out why.

That is where I stopped and that is where I stayed for another day. I had the whole shape of it and I could not find the reason inside it.

And whatever it is, it is not cheap. A stranger takes a six-year-old by the head and turns it away from a wall, mid-sentence, in the middle of somebody else’s conversation, without appearing to notice she is doing it. **You do not get that good at a thing by accident.**

I turned it over for a day and got nowhere, and the nearest I came was thinking: *this is a very expensive habit.*

## Chapter 16: Looking Away on Purpose

I went to find Hannit on the second evening and apologized.

I did it badly. I said that I had said a thing I should not have said, and that I did not fully understand what the thing was, and that I was sorry.

Hannit was putting away and did not stop putting away.

“It’s all right,” he said.

“It isn’t.”

“It’s all right,” said Hannit, and he was not being brave about it and he was not making me work for it and he was not going to discuss it. He said it the way you would tell somebody their coat was on the wrong hook. Then he asked whether I had eaten, which is a joke, because I do not, and he had made it before and it had been funny.

He did not make it funny this time. He just said it and went on putting away.

---

I would have preferred to be shouted at.

That is not a noble feeling and it does me no credit. But somebody shouting at me would have been a thing I could stay still and receive, and afterward there would have been an afterward.

Instead there was a man who talks, not talking, telling me it was all right, and meaning it.

---

That night I went up over the town and looked at it in the dark, all the little lamps in the courtyards, set low, throwing everything flat on the ground where nobody has to look at it.

I had all the evidence there was and I had put it together correctly and I understood exactly one thing:

**They are not blind. They have never been blind. They are looking away on purpose.**

And I did not know why anybody would.

## Chapter 17: Four Hard Things

There is an hour in the middle of the day when that town goes odd.

I had noticed it early on and put it down to the heat, because I put everything down to the heat that week. Around the middle of the day the light comes down almost straight, and the shadows go small and tight and pull in under people, and for about an hour there is very little to see on any wall in Seth.

And the town changes.

Not in any way you could point at. The row does not close. Nobody announces anything. It is simply that during that hour a great many things happen that do not happen at any other time, and one midday while Tavi was away I hung above the courtyard by the well and watched four of them.

---

**The first** was a man who had been avoiding a woman all week.

I had noticed the avoiding without understanding it. He took the long way round the row twice a day. He turned into doorways. It was the most obvious avoiding I have ever watched and neither of them ever acknowledged it.

At the top of that hour he crossed the courtyard and spoke to her.

They talked for about four minutes. She said something and he said something and then they both laughed, once, in the awkward way, and then they went off in different directions, and whatever it was, it was finished.

**The second** was somebody asking somebody else for money.

I could tell from the shape of it: the too-long approach, the amount of throat-clearing. He asked. She said yes and made nothing of it. He went away with his fins flat and something eased in him about eight steps later.

**The third** was two women at the well who had a conversation that was plainly the seventh part of a conversation, and got to the end of it, and stopped.

**The fourth** was a boy of about fifteen going up to a stall and saying something quickly and going away again, and the woman at the stall standing quite still for a moment afterward.

## Chapter 18: When There Is Nothing to Read

Four hard things, in one hour, in one courtyard.

And when the hour ended and the light came off the straight and the shadows started coming back out from under people, the whole thing stopped. The next difficult conversation I saw was the following midday.

I worked out what the hour was for on my second pass through the evidence.

**They do the hard things when there is nothing to read.**

Nobody has scheduled it. Nobody has ever written it down. It is simply that if you have something to say that costs you, you say it when there is the least amount of you showing. The entire town knows the hour is easier, and not one of them has ever said what it is easier *than*.

---

I did not tell anybody I had worked it out.

It is not much of a thing. I hung above a courtyard with a fact in me and did not put it into the air where it could land on somebody.

It had taken me that long to manage it once.

---

Tavi came back to the stall the next morning, because the work needed doing.

That was all it was. Hannit could not do the standing up and the row does not stop, and there were shutters promised. Tavi came back and did the standing up.

Nobody made anything of it. Debb said good morning in a completely ordinary voice. Hannit said something about the wind.

Tavi did not look at me and did not not look at me, which is a thing I had not known you could do until I watched somebody do it for an hour and a half.

## Chapter 19: Exactly This

I did not say anything for most of the morning. Then a customer went and it was the two of us at the front of the stall, and I said the thing I had been assembling since the day I said it, which was that I was sorry, and that I had not known.

Tavi kept sorting the box.

“I know you didn’t.”

“If I had known...”

“You’d have said something else.” Tavi put a bracket in the wrong section and took it out again. “You didn’t know.”

Then, after about a minute, without stopping the sorting:

“I wasn’t scared of you.”

“What?”

“That first day. You thought I was being...” Tavi made a hand movement meaning *whatever it was you thought*. “Because I kept getting in front of you. I wasn’t scared of *you*.”

Tavi put the last of the brackets away and closed the box.

“I was scared of exactly this.”

---

I did not have anything to say to that.

There was a version where I said *I understand now* and I could feel it coming up and I did not use it, partly because of the hour by the well and partly because it would have been for me.

Tavi went and dealt with somebody about a hinge.

## Chapter 20: The Only Way

Debb explained it to me that afternoon, in about two sentences, while doing something else.

I had gone three stalls down because Debb is the only person in that row who will answer a question with the answer, and I asked, straight out, why nobody looks.

Debb was under a bench with both arms up inside something.

“Course we can read them,” Debb said. “What do you think we are.”

“Then why does everybody...”

“Because it’s the only way anybody here gets to keep anything to themselves.”

“So the shadows tell the truth.”

Debb came out from under the bench far enough to look at me.

“They show you something,” Debb said. “That’s not the same as knowing what it means.”

Then Debb hit the thing with something, twice, said a word about the person who had made it, and got up to find a different tool.

---

I went up over the row and all of it dropped into place so fast I could hear them.

The man taking the long way round. The lamps set low. Nub turned by the head before Nub could learn there was anything to be careful about. Five people looking away in the same quarter-second, then that awful polite minute where everybody found an ordinary reason to be somewhere else.

I had gone through it without noticing it was there.

---

Tavi’s shadow was still facing east.

It had put the bundle down at some point during the week. I do not know when. It had picked it up again twice that morning, and it stood at the wall behind the stall doing what it had presumably been doing for a season, which was getting ready to go somewhere and not going.

And now every person in that row knew that I knew, and that Hannit knew, and that they all knew that Hannit knew.

The wall was still up. It had a hole in it exactly the shape of one thirteen-year-old.

## Chapter 21: The Work

I stopped trying to help.

That is not as noble as it sounds. I stopped because I had run out of things to try, and because everything I had tried so far had made it worse, and because I had finally understood that there was nothing I could do that would not involve saying something to somebody.

So for three days I did the work.

I cannot lift and I cannot carry and I cannot hold a bracket steady, so what I did was go and get people. Somebody at the far end wanted to know if Hannit had finished the pot. I went and found out and came back. A part was needed from four stalls down. I went. It is the one useful thing about being able to cross a courtyard in a straight line, and I did it for three days without once mentioning a shadow to anybody.

Tavi noticed, and did not say anything about it, and by the third day I was being sent on errands without being asked, which is a thing that happens to people who are part of a stall.

Nothing was solved. The shadow still faced east. Twice during those days it picked the bundle up while Hannit was talking about the shutters, and both times everybody in the row did the thing with their eyes, and both times it was worse than the time before, because now everybody knew that everybody was doing it.

But nobody was making Tavi say anything. That was the whole of what I had to offer and it was not mine to be proud of, since it consisted entirely of shutting up.

## Chapter 22: Who Watched the Lines?

The children play in the space at the west end where the row runs out.

It is not a square. It is where three streets fail to meet properly and there is a piece of ground nobody has built on, and it is in shade after the middle of the day, which is why it is full of children and why nobody else wants it.

I had been past it about forty times. It is loud in the way that a place full of children is loud, and I had never once attended to any of it, because I had been busy being wrong about shadows.

At the end of the third day I went past it with nothing to do.

---

There were six of them and Nub was one of them, and they were arguing about who was starting.

They argue about this at length. It is apparently the most important part. Somebody wanted to start because they had not started last time, and somebody else said they had, and a third said it did not matter because there is a way of deciding and they should just do the way of deciding.

So they did the way of deciding.

One of them, a tall one older than Nub, stood in the middle and the rest went round in a ring, and the tall one pointed at each of them in turn and said a line, and the ring said the line back, and the pointing moved on with each one. Fast, and flat, and worn completely smooth, in the voice children use for the part of a game that is not the game.

> “How many worlds?”
>
> “More than you can count.”
>
> “How many hands?”
>
> “Enough to make the lines.”
>
> “Who went home?”
>
> “Almost everyone.”
>
> “Who watched the lines?”
>
> “Someone stayed.”

And the pointing finger came down on a small one at the end of it, and the small one shrieked, and they all scattered, and the game started.

## Chapter 23: Enough to Make the Lines

I made them do it again.

I asked. Nub said they had already done it and you do not do it twice, and I said I wanted to hear the words, and Nub said the words did not matter, they were just for the pointing.

Then I asked whether I could do the pointing.

Six children stopped what they were doing and explained to me at once and at length that I could not do the pointing, for reasons.

The reasons were: I did not know the words. I could not point, having nothing to point with. I had not been playing. Nub added that I was not allowed, and when pressed on this said that I was *not allowed*, with the finality of somebody quoting law.

The tall one, who was kinder, said I could be in the ring if I liked, and that all I had to do was say the answers back.

So I was in the ring.

I got the first two right because I had just heard them. I got *almost everyone* wrong. I said *nearly*, and the tall one stopped the whole thing and made everybody start again, and Nub was disgusted with me, and the small one at the end got picked again and shrieked again, and they scattered again and left me hanging over an empty piece of ground at dusk with the words still going round in me.

---

Here is what I had.

*How many worlds. More than you can count.*

Not *many*. Not *several*. **More than you can count**, in a counting rhyme, said by children who use it for counting.

*How many hands. Enough to make the lines.*

Hands. Not powers. Not makers. Not anything with a capital letter on it. **Hands**. The thing you have several of and use for work, and *enough of them*, which is a builder’s answer. It is what you say when somebody asks whether there will be enough people to get the roof on.

*Who went home. Almost everyone.*

So they came from somewhere. So they went back to it. So it was a job that finished.

## Chapter 24: A Great Many of Them

I hung there for a long time doing nothing at all.

It is very little, and it would be easy to make it more.

I do not know who they were. I do not know what the lines are for. I do not know when.

What I have is this. **I already knew somebody had made the lines. I had never once pictured the somebody.**

And the answer, in a rhyme that six children use to decide who is on, is: **hands. A great many of them, from a great many places, who came and did the work and afterward went home.**

Not one great thing that did it. Not something. Somebody, and then somebody else, and then a great many more of them than anybody has counted.

---

The small one came back for a shoe that had come off during the scattering.

It found the shoe, and looked at me, and asked whether I was going to be there tomorrow.

I said I did not know.

It said that if I was, I should learn the words properly, because you cannot be in the ring if you get them wrong, and then it went off up the street with the shoe in its hand shouting for somebody.

Somebody’s mother called from a doorway that it was getting dark, and one by one the shouting went out of that end of the town, and I went back along the row in the last of the light.

## Chapter 25: It Would Mean Poles

It started because Debb’s back is bad.

That is not the whole of it, but it is where it came from, and if you had asked anybody in that row what happened they would have told you about the glare.

Debb has complained about the glare for eleven years. This is established. Several people told me the exact number without my asking, and one of them told me twice.

The complaint goes: the sun comes down the row from the west end in the late part of the afternoon, and it comes in under the short awnings at exactly the angle to get a person in the face, and it has faded the good cloth on Debb’s third shelf twice, and it makes the dust show, and Debb has to stand at an angle to work in it, which is what does the back.

Everybody has heard this. Nobody disputes any of it. And nobody has done anything about it in eleven years, because it would mean poles.

---

The next morning Debb said the thing, in the middle of complaining, exactly the way you would say anything else:

“We should run the cloth the whole length.”

There was a pause of about two seconds.

“The whole row?”

“The whole row,” said Debb. “Not these little bits. One run of it, end to end, up over the top and down the wall side. Do it once and it’s done.”

And the man from the next stall along, who had not said anything so far, said, “You’d want proper poles for that.”

And Debb said, “You would.”

And that was it. That was the entire decision.

## Chapter 26: The Rope Was Measured Wrong Twice

By midday there were four people arguing about poles.

By the afternoon there were nine, and somebody had gone to get a cart, and somebody else had produced a quantity of cloth from a back room that everybody said was too good for it and that got used anyway.

---

Here is what that looked like.

The hook argument went on for two days. It concerned whether the wall at the east end, which belongs to a man called Serrat and whose owner was not present for most of it, would take a fixing, and whether it was the sort of wall that had a course of good stone behind the render or the sort that did not, and whose fault it would be if the answer turned out to be the second one.

Six people had opinions. Serrat, when finally consulted, said he did not care and that they should do whatever they liked and stop coming to his door about it, and then came out four times that afternoon to watch and comment.

**The rope was measured wrong twice.**

The first time by Debb, who had done it in paces. The second time by two people who did it properly with a cord and got a different answer, and who then argued about which of them had held the end badly, and who went and did it a third time in the presence of a third person who had been brought specifically to watch them do it.

The ladder came from somebody’s cousin and everybody had a view about it. It was too short for the east end, which everybody had known it would be, and which everybody had said, and which did not stop anybody from bringing it.

Nub was underfoot the entire time and was removed from the site four times.

## Chapter 27: Make Myself Useful

I helped by being unable to help.

I cannot lift a pole. I cannot hold a rope. What I can do is go and get somebody, which I did about thirty times over two days, and which is the sort of help that stops being remarked on by the fourth time.

At the east end I went up over the last pole and said that it was leaning.

Three people told me it was not. Somebody fetched a cord and dropped it down the side of the pole and showed me that it was not, and then everybody looked at the cord for a while and decided that actually it was, slightly, but not because of anything I had said, and by then they were adjusting it for their own reasons and I was in the way.

I was told to move twice, and once, by Debb, to make myself useful, which I could not, on account of having no arms.

---

Tavi worked on it.

Tavi did the standing up, which by then I understood to be the arrangement, and carried the cloth end for most of the second day, and went up the short ladder to do the fixings on the middle section because Tavi was the one who could get up there.

Nobody made anything of that.

Nobody made anything of Tavi at all. Debb sent Tavi for the good rope in the same voice Debb used for sending anybody for anything. Serrat’s wall got argued about across Tavi’s head. Somebody handed Tavi one end of a cord and said hold that and went away for eleven minutes.

**Tavi’s name was said perhaps forty times in two days and not once did anybody mean anything by it.**

## Chapter 28: The Sort of Light You Get in a Room

Hannit sat at the front of the stall and had opinions about the poles.

He had a great many. He had a view about the spacing which turned out to be correct and which he mentioned afterward eleven times. He had a view about the west end that was ignored. He said, at one point, to nobody, that this town could not put up a run of cloth without holding four meetings about it, and the woman next to him said that was true and that it had always been true and that his father had been the worst of the lot.

He talked without stopping for two days.

---

On the second evening it was done.

They ran the last of it out over the poles at the east end and got the corner square and came down off the ladders, and everybody stood in the row and looked at it, and somebody said it was a good job, and Debb said it should have been done years ago, and three separate people said *well* in the particular way that means the day is over.

And then everybody went home, and the row was empty, and the light came down the length of it from the west end and did not reach the ground.

---

I stayed up there a while after they had gone.

The cloth is not a good colour. It is the wrong side of orange and it does not match the bits of awning it replaced and one section of it is visibly newer than the rest, and it goes the whole length of the row from the west end to Serrat’s wall, and under it the light is the sort of light you get in a room.

Down on the ground the shapes were soft at the edges and going nowhere.

You could not have told, from the shape on the floor under that cloth, what any of them was doing.

## Chapter 29: She Wanted to See

The first complaint arrived before the middle of the morning.

It was from a woman four stalls down who does fine work with a needle, and it was that she could not see. She said it in the doorway of Debb’s stall at some volume, and she said that they had gone and made it dark, and that she had been perfectly happy before, and that if she was expected to work in a cellar she would want to know about it in advance.

Debb was delighted. Debb had been waiting for this since the poles went up and had an answer ready, which was that the light had been ruining her thread for years and she had said so herself in the spring, and the woman said she had never said any such thing, and Debb said she had, and they went on with it for some time.

Two other people joined in on different sides. Somebody proposed cutting a gap in the cloth over the needle stall, which everybody immediately hated. Somebody else said you get used to it. The woman said she did not want to get used to it, she wanted to *see*, and Debb said that was a very high standard for a person who had put a sleeve on backward in the winter, and that was the end of that particular round because everybody had to stop and laugh.

She was still working there at dusk. She did not move stalls. As far as I know she is complaining about it yet.

## Chapter 30: Possible Not to Be Certain

Under the cloth the row is the colour of the inside of a tent.

Everything is soft. The light comes through the orange and lands on everybody the same way, and the shapes on the ground have no edges to speak of. They are there, they move about when people move about, and there is nothing in them to read.

Nobody said anything about that. Not on the first day, not on any day.

What they said was that it was dim, and that it was better, and that it was too warm now with the air not moving, and that somebody ought to have thought of that, and that Serrat’s wall was holding up better than expected.

---

Tavi worked.

Hannit talked. The pot job came in and went out and two of the racks got made, and Nub came down the row and stared openly at somebody’s shadow and was steered away from it by a woman who did not stop talking, which happens under cloth exactly the way it happens in the sun, because the shape is still there, it is only harder to be certain about.

The cloth did not solve it. Anybody who stood close enough and looked hard enough could still have made a fair guess.

What the cloth did was make it possible not to be certain.

Which turns out to be all anybody needed.

---

On the third day Hannit sent Tavi to Serrat about a fitting.

It was the most ordinary errand in the row. Serrat is at the east end, four minutes away, and does not have the thing, and everybody knows he does not have the thing, and the entire purpose of going to Serrat about a fitting is to be told to go somewhere else.

Hannit said it without looking up from what he was doing.

“Go and see if Serrat’s got one of the small ones. He hasn’t. And when you get back, we’ll want the long bench out. I can’t shift it on my own and it’s wanted for the racks.”

Tavi said, “All right.”

And went.

## Chapter 31: It’s About the Glare

I do not know what to say about that except what happened, which is: nothing.

Hannit went on with the fitting. Tavi went off up the row and was gone about ten minutes and came back and said Serrat had not got one, and Hannit said he knew that, and the two of them got the long bench out, which took both of them and some complaining.

Neither of them looked at the other one while any of it was happening.

The shadow on the wall behind them was a soft grey shape with no bundle in it that I could see, and no direction to it, and nothing to read at all.

---

I asked Debb, later, whether the awning had been about Tavi.

I should not have asked. I knew that while I was asking. But I had all that watching and one clear fact and I wanted somebody to say it out loud, which is the thing I do, which is the thing that started all this.

Debb was doing something to a hinge.

“It’s about the glare,” Debb said.

“Yes, but...”

“It’s about the glare,” said Debb, and turned the hinge over, and did not look up, and after a while asked whether I was going to be any use with the other end of it.

That is the last thing anybody in that town ever said to me about any of it.

---

I left on the sixth morning after the cloth went up.

Hannit said goodbye at length, twice, and told me that if I came back through I was to say so in advance so that somebody could make something, and then said that of course I would not be able to say so in advance, and worked round to being annoyed about that, and then said goodbye a third time.

Nub was not there. Nub was somewhere at the west end shouting.

## Chapter 32: That’s All Right, Then

Tavi walked with me as far as the end of the row, which is not far, and did it while carrying something to the east end anyway.

We had not talked much since the day I said it. We never got back to what we had been on the second day.

But we were walking along the row together, and Tavi was on my left, and there was a wall on my left, and Tavi did not put themself between me and it.

That was all. Tavi walked on the side that Tavi happened to be on, and looked where they liked, and did not once arrange where I was.

I noticed it the whole way down the row, and I did not say anything about it.

---

At the end Tavi put the thing down and said:

“Are you going to tell anybody where you go?”

“I don’t know where I go.”

Tavi thought about that.

“That’s all right, then,” they said, and picked the thing up, and went to the east end.

---

The road out of Seth goes east across the flat and does not stop.

I went along it in the early part of the day with the town behind me and the heat coming up already, and about half a mile out I turned and looked back at it, which is a thing I do not usually bother with.

You could see the row from there. One long band of orange along the north side of the town, mismatched at one end where the newer cloth is, sitting over the top of eleven stalls and everybody in them.

Somewhere under it a woman was complaining that she could not see.
