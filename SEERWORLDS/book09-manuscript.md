## Chapter 1: Nothing to Carry

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to eight worlds, and on the last one some children repeated an old counting rhyme that put worlds, hands, lines, going home, and someone staying in the same few breaths. I thought it might mean that a great many ordinary people had helped make the crossings. I did not know that.

I have spent the whole way here thinking about it. I arrived with a question I have never had before, which is **why**. The first person I asked anything on this world told me to move, because I was floating where a rope was going.

---

It was a river.

Wide and brown and busy, with the far bank low and green and nobody looking at it. There were boats, not handsome ones, working ones, flat and broad and patched, coming in and going out and tying up along a run of landings that went most of the length of the town.

And the town went back from the water in a way that made it obvious which end mattered. Everything faced the river. Everything was about the river. Somewhere behind there were houses and streets and people doing things that were not freight, and none of it had the slightest interest in the water it was all pointed at.

The noise was tremendous.

The noise delighted me at once, which I have learned to be careful about.

---

The people were low and broad and went about on four legs.

Everything about this world comes out of the shape of them. Four legs underneath, set wide, so that a person standing still is extremely difficult to knock over. Two smaller limbs at the front, graspers, they call them, which do everything hands do and are quick with rope.

And a back. A long flat back with a shallow ridge running down it, and that is where things go.

**Everything goes on the back.** Sacks, tools, a rolled net, somebody’s coat, a child. A person walking along that waterfront at midday has a load on them arranged the way you would arrange a shelf, and they are not struggling with it, because that is what the back is *for*. A loaded person here is not carrying a burden. A loaded person is just a person, working.

They shift things up onto the ridge with the graspers without looking, in one movement, forty times a day.

Which is what made it take me so long to notice.

---

Here is the thing I watched about nine times before I understood it.

Two people on the middle landing, arguing about a price. Cargo between them, both of them enjoying the argument, neither in any hurry. Eventually the one on the left said something like: *fine, and I’ll have the rest of it down to you before dark.*

And there was a thing in her graspers.

It had not been there. She had not picked it up. Nothing had been handed to her. It was simply there: a flat dull object about the size of a folded cloth, no colour to speak of, plain as a stone.

She put it up on her ridge without looking at it and went back to arguing about the price.

I went and looked at the ground where it might have come from. There was nothing on the ground. I went round to the other side and looked at her back. It was there, sitting among the rest of what she was carrying, and it was the least interesting thing on her.

---

I watched for the rest of the morning and it happened everywhere.

A man promised a boy he would look at a hinge. A woman told somebody’s mother she would come by. Two people at the scales settled something that had clearly been going on for weeks, and both of them got one, and both of them looked mildly annoyed about it in a way that had nothing to do with the objects.

**They arrive small.** The hinge was about the size of a thumb. The one the woman on the landing got was a good deal bigger, because there was a good deal more of that promise.

And in every single case the same movement: it appears in the graspers, and it goes up onto the ridge with everything else, and nobody looks at it.

---

Then I saw one go.

The same woman, coming back down the landing with the rest of the load.

She handed it over. The other one checked it and said something rude and friendly.

And the thing on her back stopped existing.

Not dramatically. It did not go anywhere or make a sound. It was there, and then the space it had been in was the space between a coil of rope and a bag of something, and she did not look round, and she did not react, and she walked off up the landing with her ridge one item lighter and complained about the weather to somebody.

---

**You promise a thing. You get a thing.**

That is the whole of it. You say *I’ll do it* to somebody and an object arrives in your graspers, plain and dull and weighing what it weighs, and you put it on your back and get on with your day.

Do the thing, and it goes.

That is the entire mechanism of this world, and I had it inside two hours, and I was so pleased that I said it out loud to nobody, hanging over the middle landing in the noise.

**Because here is what that means.** On eight worlds I have had to work out what people owed each other by watching how they behaved. Here it is an object. You can look at somebody and count.

I have never been anywhere so legible in my life.

---

Then somebody asked me where I keep things, and it went badly for about a minute.

He was mending a net and he was perfectly friendly about it. He had watched me come along the landing and he wanted to know, in the way you want to know a small practical fact, where I put things.

“I don’t put things anywhere.”

“You’d have something, though.”

“I don’t have anything.”

He thought about that while doing four more knots.

“Where’s it go, though,” he said.

---

That was the first one. I got asked three more times before evening.

**The second** was a woman on the north landing who wanted to know whether I had a hook arrangement of some kind underneath, and who bent down to check, and who was disappointed.

**The third** was a boy of about twelve who did not believe me. He believed the floating, which he could see. He did not believe that a person could go about with nothing on them, and he said so, twice, with the flat certainty of somebody who has never once in his life met anybody who was not carrying at least a bag.

**The fourth** was the mender again, who had been thinking about it.

“So if somebody asked you to take something down to the south landing.”

“I couldn’t.”

“You couldn’t take it.”

“I’ve nowhere to put it.”

“Right,” he said, and did some more knots, and then said, “That’s no good, is it,” not unkindly, and went back to the net.

**And that is the exact size of my usefulness in this town.** In a place that runs on carrying, I am the one thing that cannot carry anything, and everybody worked that out faster than I did.

---

The weigh-house is the biggest building on the water and the town has grown round it the way a root grows round a stone.

It is enormously old and has been added to by everybody who ever thought of something. There are beams in it that are visibly older than the walls they are holding up, and three different kinds of floor, and a set of scales at the front that four people were shouting about when I went in and were still shouting about when I came out.

That is where the freight goes. That is where everything goes. If a thing arrives in this town it gets weighed in that building, and somebody promises somebody something about it, and then it goes somewhere else.

I spent the end of the afternoon in the rafters of it counting.

**Everybody in that building was carrying between two and six.** I did the whole floor twice. The average was a little under four. Nobody, anywhere, in an hour and a half, was carrying nothing.

---

Late on, a boat came in that everybody had opinions about, and the person bringing it in was the most loaded person I had seen all day.

They came up the landing with the ridge full, eight promises by my count, plus a coil of line and a bag, and they were not slow with it and did not appear to notice it. Three separate people said something to them on the way past and got something back.

They tied up, and did the thing with the papers, and stood there a moment looking at the water in the way you do at the end of a run.

Then they turned round and looked directly at me, which nobody had done all day without wanting something.

“You’re the one with nowhere to put anything.”

“Word’s got round.”

“Word’s got round since about noon,” they said. “I’m Corrin.”

## Chapter 2: The Weigh-house

The weigh-house opens before it is light and the arguing starts about four minutes later.

I got there early on the second day and did not leave until the afternoon, and I have not spent a better morning on any world. It is one enormous room with a roof somewhere up in the dark, and a floor that is never empty, and a set of scales at the front that everything in that town passes over eventually.

The scales are the point. If a thing comes off a boat it gets weighed. If it gets weighed there is a number, and if there is a number somebody disagrees with it, and if somebody disagrees with it then two people stand there having the same argument their mothers had.

Nobody is angry. That took me a while. **They are enjoying it**, in the way you enjoy a thing you are good at, and when it is settled they go off together perfectly pleased and the next two come up.

---

Here is what I counted between opening and midmorning.

Forty-one promises made. I am confident about that number because I had nothing else to do.

Twenty-nine of them discharged before I stopped counting. Most inside the hour. Somebody says *I’ll get that down to you*, and the thing arrives in their graspers, and it goes up on the ridge, and forty minutes later they get it down to them and it is gone.

**The whole building runs on it.** Not on memory. On the objects. Nobody has to keep a list in their head of who has promised what to whom. If you owe somebody a thing then you are carrying a thing, and anybody can see it.

**It does not make anybody do anything.** It only means nobody has to argue about what was said.

I said to a man at the scales that it must save an enormous amount of trouble.

He said what must.

---

The freight itself is the other half of the morning and it never stops.

A boat comes in. Whatever is on it comes off, and goes across the floor to the scales, and gets weighed, and gets a number. Then the number gets disagreed with. Then somebody who is taking it onward promises to have it somewhere by a particular time, and gets a thing for it, and puts the thing on the ridge, and loads the cargo on top.

**Which is the part I liked best.** A person going out of that building is carrying the goods and the promise about the goods, on the same back, at the same time, and the promise is usually the smaller item.

I watched a woman go out under a load I would have described as impossible: four crates, a roll of something, and a bag of tools, with a thing about the size of a pear sitting up near her shoulders holding the whole arrangement together in the sense that it was the reason for it.

She came back an hour and a half later carrying the tools and the bag.

Everything else, and the pear, had gone.

---

The twelve that did not discharge were the interesting ones, and by the end of the morning I understood why.

Some were long. A man promised to have a hull done by the turn of the season and put a substantial object up on his ridge and will be carrying it for two months, and thinks nothing of it, and everybody who sees him knows he is good for it because they can see it there.

Some were waiting on somebody else. You cannot deliver a thing that has not come in yet.

And a few were small and old and had simply not been got round to. I saw a woman with a very small one who was asked about it twice by different people, and both times she said *tomorrow*, and both times everybody laughed, and it was clearly a joke of some standing.

**Nobody was ashamed of any of it.** Carrying is not a failing here. It is a Tuesday.

---

Then Pip crossed the room, and Pip is six.

Pip came in through the big doors at speed, stopped, looked around with the concentration of somebody on official business, located the person Pip was looking for, and went over.

Pip was carrying one promise. It was about the size of an acorn and it was in the middle of Pip’s ridge, alone, in an enormous amount of empty space.

Pip did not walk over. Pip *proceeded*.

---

The errand was that somebody’s aunt had asked Pip to **get a late boat written up on the board**, not merely mention it or pass it on. Written up. Pip had said yes, and Pip had a thing on the back about it, and Pip was going to discharge that thing if it killed him.

He got to the man at the scales and delivered the message in a voice pitched for a much larger room.

The man said thank you very much and that he would see to it.

Pip did not move.

The man looked at Pip. Then he looked at the acorn on Pip’s back, and worked out that *seeing to it* was not going to be sufficient for present purposes, and went and got the chalk, and wrote the boat up on the board.

And the acorn went.

Pip watched the place where it had been for about two seconds, and then went out through the big doors at a speed that suggested he had somewhere better to be, and shouted something at somebody in the street.

---

“He’ll do that four times today,” said a woman near me, not looking up.

“Is that a real errand?”

“It’s a real errand.” She was doing something to a strap. “His aunt’s got about nine of them and she gives him one at a time. He gets very cross if she gives him two.”

---

I went round the whole building in the middle of the day when it was quieter, which meant only about thirty people were shouting.

The back of it is where things go that are not going anywhere soon. Long-term storage, mostly: bales and crates with dates chalked on them, a stack of something under sacking, three broken scales that somebody is going to mend.

Along the back wall there is a low shelf with old things on it. A ledger with a broken spine, some tools of a kind nobody uses now, and two flat dull objects the size of a folded cloth.

Somebody had put a bucket down beside them.

---

I looked at the shelf for about as long as I looked at the broken scales, which is to say not very, because at that moment somebody dropped a crate at the front of the building and the whole room turned round.

Pip came back in twice more that afternoon. Both times with something on the back. Both times at speed.

And at the end of it Corrin came in off the water with a delivery, and I got a proper look at what Corrin was carrying, and it was the first time I thought about it at all.

**Eight, I had counted the day before.** It was eight again, and one of them was very large.

Not the size of a folded cloth. The size of a thing you would need both graspers for. It sat at the back of the ridge behind everything else, and it had the look objects get when they have been on somebody a long time. Not worn, exactly. Settled.

Corrin put a crate down, argued about its weight for a while, won, and went out.

I did not ask. By then I had seen enough old ones on enough backs to know that carrying something a long while is not remarkable here.

## Chapter 3: What the Boat Is For

Two streets back from the water there is a workroom with a wide door, and if the door is open it is because Nilla is working, and if Nilla is working you can hear it from the corner.

I went in because of the noise.

It is a rope shop. Not the making of rope, which happens somewhere upriver in a long shed by people who do nothing else. The other thing, the part I did not know existed until I got in the door. **Splicing.**

Every rope on every boat in that town ends somewhere. It ends in a loop, or in another rope, or in a thimble round a ring, and how it ends is the difference between a load arriving and a load in the river. And when a rope goes, and they go, somebody has to put it back together in a way that will hold under a weight that would take your leg off.

Nilla does that.

---

I watched for most of a morning and understood perhaps a third of it.

The rope comes apart into its own strands, and the strands go back through each other in an order, and the order is the whole art. Over, under, against the lay. Nilla’s graspers move at a speed that makes it look like less work than it is, and every so often they stop and pull the whole thing tight with the front legs braced and the ridge dropped, and you can see exactly how much force is in it.

Then they run a thumb down it, and are not satisfied, and take part of it out again.

**Four separate times**, in front of me, because of something I could not see and could not be shown when I asked.

“It’s not fair on it,” Nilla said, about a rope.

---

They are about seventeen, at a guess. Quick, and dry, and a good deal more patient with objects than with people.

Nilla was easy for me to be around. I ask a great many questions, and Nilla will answer a question about work for as long as you keep asking. I also had nothing to do and no promises to carry, so I could hang about in a workroom all morning without anybody wanting me to move.

By midday I had learned the names of six knots and been told twice that a splice is not a knot.

---

Corrin came by at some point about a thing that needed doing to a hawser.

There was no ceremony about it. Corrin put their head in, said something about the hawser, was told it would be ready when it was ready, and said that was no use to anybody. Nilla said it would be ready by the afternoon. Corrin said that was worse.

Then they argued for a while about a person they both knew who had done something at the north landing, and they disagreed about it in a way that had clearly been going on for years and that neither of them had any intention of resolving.

“You always say that.”

“I always say it because it’s always true.”

“It was true *once*,” said Nilla, not looking up.

---

They are family, or the nearest thing. I got the shape of it in pieces over that morning and none of it was told to me as a story.

Nilla’s people were on the water and then were not. There was a bad stretch, an illness, I think, and something about a boat that had to be sold, and for a couple of years it was not clear to anybody, including Nilla, what was going to happen to Nilla.

Corrin was around for all of it. Corrin is the sort of person who is around.

And somewhere in that stretch, on a landing, with a great deal else going on, Corrin said the thing.

---

**”This boat will be yours.”**

That is all of it. Five words, and Corrin meant them entirely, and a thing appeared in Corrin’s graspers and went up on the ridge where it has been ever since.

Nilla was eleven.

And for a couple of years after that, when it was still not clear what was going to happen to Nilla, **it was clear what was going to happen to Nilla.** There was a boat. It was coming. Somebody had said so, and you could see that somebody had said so, and it was on their back every day where anybody could look at it.

I asked Nilla, later, what that had been like.

“It was the best thing anybody ever did for me,” said Nilla, and went back to the rope, and did not elaborate.

---

The trouble is that Nilla is seventeen and Nilla can splice.

Not adequately. Properly. Boats come to that door from three landings away, and the long shed upriver has twice asked whether Nilla would like to come and work for them, which Nilla does not want either, because in the shed you make rope and here you mend it and the mending is the interesting part.

**Nilla is going to spend a life doing this and is entirely happy about it.**

And a boat is not a possession. You do not put a boat in a cupboard. If you take on a boat you take on a run, and a season, and everybody who depends on the run, and you are on the water from before light. You are not in a workroom two streets back doing the interesting part of anything.

---

I worked all that out for myself over about an hour and was quite pleased with it, and then I said something insufferable, which was that surely they should just sort it out.

Corrin was in the doorway by then, having come back about the hawser.

Both of them looked at me.

---

“Go on,” said Corrin.

“Nilla doesn’t want the boat. You know Nilla doesn’t want the boat.”

“I do.”

“So...” I did the thing I do, which is to lay it out as though nobody in the room has had a thought. “So you tell each other that, out loud, and then it’s dealt with.”

“We’ve told each other that out loud,” said Nilla.

“About six times,” said Corrin.

“I said it on Thirdday,” said Nilla, “and I said it before the season, and I said it at least twice last year, and I said it to your face in this room in front of a man waiting for a hawser.”

“That’s five.”

“There was another one.”

---

I asked what happened when they said it.

Corrin turned round and showed me the ridge.

**Nothing happened.** The thing was still there. It is very large and it sits at the back where it has settled and it has not been affected by anything anybody has said about it in six conversations across a year.

“It’s not Nilla’s,” said Corrin. “That’s the thing you’re not getting. It isn’t a bargain, and it isn’t an agreement the two of us can cancel. A promise is the fact that I said I would do something. Nilla can say no every day of the week. Nilla *has* said no, often enough. It doesn’t touch the thing, because it was never Nilla’s to give back. It’s mine. **I said it.**”

---

So then I asked what the options were, and Corrin listed them like a tide table.

“I give Nilla the boat. Promise kept, the thing goes, and Nilla spends the next forty years on the water instead of doing that.” A grasper at the rope. “That’s one.”

“Or?”

“Or I don’t. Which is what I’m doing. And I carry it, and it stays, and we have this conversation twice a year for as long as I’m alive.” Corrin did not sound self-pitying about it. Corrin sounded like somebody describing a wall. “That’s two.”

“And three?”

“Three is I sell the boat to somebody else,” said Corrin. “Then I can’t ever keep it. Then it’s broken.”

“And the thing goes?”

“The thing stays,” said Corrin. “That’s what broken means. It stays and it doesn’t come off and everybody who looks at me for the rest of my life can see I broke one.”

---

I said, because I am the way I am, that there must be a fourth.

“There isn’t,” said Nilla, from the bench.

“There’s always a...”

“There isn’t,” said Nilla, and pulled a splice tight, and did not look up. “We’ve had a year on it. I’ve thought of everything you’re about to say and about nine you won’t.”

---

Corrin took the hawser away in the afternoon and Nilla worked until it was dark, and I stayed in the workroom because I had nowhere I needed to be and because the sound of it was better than the sound of the landings.

Some time in the evening Nilla said, to the rope rather than to me:

“It was a good promise, though.”

“It was.”

“That’s the whole trouble with it,” said Nilla.

## Chapter 4: Too Much on the Back

I spent the next two days trying to get round it, and everybody let me.

That is not what I expected. On other worlds, when I start taking somebody’s problem apart in front of them, there is a point where they would like me to stop. Here there was no such point. Corrin answered everything. Nilla answered everything. Neither of them was embarrassed, or brave about it, or tired of me. They had been round all of it themselves and they were mildly interested to see whether the floating thing would find something they had missed.

They did not think I would. They were perfectly polite about that too.

---

**Here is what I tried.**

**Somebody else could take the boat on and keep it for Nilla.** A holding arrangement: Corrin gives it to a third party, the promise is discharged, and Nilla is free to do nothing with it.

Corrin said that would not be giving the boat to Nilla. That would be giving the boat to somebody else. The promise says what it says.

I said the promise did not specify a date.

“It doesn’t,” Corrin agreed. “That’s why I can go on not keeping it.”

**Nilla could take the boat and immediately sell it.** Promise kept, object gone, Nilla ends up with money instead of a life on the water.

This one I was proud of for about four minutes.

Nilla pointed out that this would mean Nilla taking the boat, which is the thing Nilla does not want to do, and that being handed the largest thing somebody owns in order to sell it that afternoon is not a smaller cruelty than being handed it to keep. **”I’d be doing it to get it off him,”** Nilla said. “That’s not a gift. That’s a chore with a boat in it.”

Corrin said Nilla had thought of that one about eight months ago and had put it better then.

**Somebody could take the promise off Corrin.** Assume it. Carry it instead.

This is not a thing, and nobody had to explain why; I could see it from the way the question landed. A promise is an account of what one particular person said. You cannot hand it over any more than you can hand over having said it.

I said that in that case Corrin could promise Nilla something else instead. A bigger, better thing, to stand in.

Corrin said that would make two.

---

By the end of the second day I had a list of nine and every one of them came apart in the graspers.

And I was doing it in the middle of a working waterfront the entire time.

**Nobody stopped working.** Corrin did two runs. Nilla did an eye splice in a hawser as thick as my whole body and then four smaller jobs and then something to a net. The scales argued. Boats came in. The tide did whatever the tide does on a river, which I never got to the bottom of.

I have solved problems on eight worlds and I have never before had one refuse to be solved *while everybody involved carried on with their afternoon*.

---

On the second day I properly looked at what Corrin carries.

I had counted eight. That was low. At the busy part of an ordinary working day it runs between nine and twelve, because Corrin is the kind of person people ask, and Corrin says yes, and then there is another thing on the ridge.

I watched a morning of it.

Somebody wanted a barrel taken up to the north landing. Yes, and a thing arrived. Somebody’s mother wanted a message got to somebody’s brother. Yes. A man wanted to know whether Corrin would look at a pump, and Corrin said not this week but next, and got a small one for that.

By eleven there were eleven.

By the middle of the afternoon there were seven, because Corrin had done four of them, which is what Corrin is like.

---

The town has opinions about people who carry a lot, and the opinions are contradictory and everybody holds all of them at once.

There is a man at the south end who is generally reckoned to be at nineteen. I did not count him myself; nineteen is the agreed figure and it has been the agreed figure for a while. Half the waterfront thinks he is the most obliging person on the river. The other half thinks he says yes because he cannot bear the four seconds in which he might say no, and that a man at nineteen is not generous, he is behind.

Both halves are perfectly friendly to him.

And there is a woman who runs the north landing and is never above two, ever, and who is admired for it and considered slightly cold.

**Corrin at eleven is thought of as normal**, which took me a while to work out and which turns out to be the point. Corrin does not say yes to everything. Corrin says yes to a great deal and then does it. The ridge goes up in the morning and down by dark, most days, and that is what it means here to be somebody people ask.

None of that applies to the one at the back.

---

And behind all of it, at the back of the ridge, the big one, not moving.

**It does not look heavy.** That is the thing about it. It looks like part of the arrangement. Everything else on that back is on its way somewhere: up, off, delivered by dark. The big one has settled in behind the traffic the way a stone settles into a riverbed with the water going over it.

Corrin does not shift it when loading. Corrin loads around it.

---

The best thing I saw in those two days had nothing to do with any of that.

There is a woman on the middle landing who owes another woman something extremely small. I never found out what. It has been going on, by the general report, for over two years, and both of them are now completely committed.

The one who owes goes the long way round. Not a little longer. She comes up from the south end and across the back of the weigh-house and down, adding a good six minutes to a trip she makes four times a day, in order not to pass the front of a particular shed.

The one who is owed knows this, obviously. Everybody knows this. The one who is owed has therefore taken to standing at the back of the weigh-house at certain hours.

They passed each other while I was watching. They said good morning. Both of them were perfectly pleasant, and the small thing stayed exactly where it was on the first one’s ridge, and afterward a man at the scales told me that this had been the most entertaining thing on that waterfront for two years and that neither of them would take a hundredweight of grain to settle it now.

**It is a small object. It has to be. She would have discharged anything that mattered.**

---

At the end of the second day I went and found Corrin on the boat and admitted I had nothing.

Corrin was doing something to a fitting and did not stop.

“Nine, was it.”

“Nine.”

“That’s more than I got to.” Corrin turned the fitting over. “Took me about a year to get to six and give up.”

“You haven’t given up.”

“I’ve given up on finding a fourth thing,” said Corrin. “That’s different from giving up. I’ve got three. I know exactly what all three of them cost. I’ve known for a year. Not knowing which one I’m doing is not the same as not understanding the problem.”

I said that most people would have picked by now.

“Most people,” said Corrin, “would have picked the one that’s easiest to live with. That doesn’t make it the right one.”

Then somebody shouted from the landing and Corrin went, and I stayed on the boat in the dark with the water going past.

## Chapter 5: We Undertake

There is an hour in the middle of the afternoon when the weigh-house is nearly empty.

The morning boats are done and the evening ones are not in, and for a while there is nobody on the floor but two people doing paperwork at the scales and whoever is shifting things about at the back. The room is enormous when it is empty. You can hear the river through the wall.

I went in there one afternoon because I had run out of things to try and because it was the only building in that town where nobody was going to ask me to move.

---

Pip was in, on an errand, with something on the back.

He went the length of the floor at speed, delivered whatever it was to a woman near the scales, waited with visible patience while she did the thing that needed doing, watched the object go, and left again. The whole business took under a minute and Pip did not look at anything else in the building on the way in or on the way out.

I mention it because it is the only other thing that happened.

---

I went along the back shelf because it was there and because I had spent days looking at promises and had started looking at everything else the way you do.

The ledger with the broken spine is a weigh-book. Somebody’s tally of what came off boats, in a hand nobody uses now, running for about eleven years and then stopping in the middle of a page.

The tools are for a kind of fastening they do not do anymore.

And the two flat dull objects at the end of the shelf are promises.

---

I had assumed they were something else. I do not know what. Trays, or lids, or a thing to do with the scales.

They are the same. Exactly the same as the one that arrived in a woman’s graspers on the landing on my first morning: the same plain surface, the same nothing colour, the same weight-look. There are two of them on a shelf at the back of a building, with a bucket beside them, in the ordinary dust of a working room.

**Which means somebody promised something and did not finish carrying it.**

That is all it means. While a person is alive, an unfinished promise stays on the ridge. When the person dies, whatever is still there remains as an object that somebody else can lift away and keep. So a promise on a shelf means the person who undertook it died with it still on the back.

---

I asked Corrin about them that evening, on the boat, while Corrin was doing something to a rope with a spike.

“Those are old,” Corrin said.

“How old?”

“Old. That one’s ours.”

“Ours?”

“Family.” Corrin did not stop with the spike. “Somebody a long way back brought it home. It’s been in the building since before the building was that shape.”

I asked what was in it.

“It’s the crossings,” said Corrin. “It’s the closing of the crossings. Everybody knows that one.”

“That’s small.”

“It’s one person’s,” said Corrin. “There’d have been thousands of these. One for every person who took that undertaking on. You don’t get a bigger one for a bigger result. You get what you took on yourself.”

“Did anybody ever open them again?”

“No.”

“Did anybody...”

“Nobody ever managed it,” said Corrin, and got the spike under a strand and leaned on it.

---

**And Corrin went on working.**

There was no pause in it. Corrin said the thing about the crossings the way you would say a building was put up by the river authority, and then said something about the rope, and then complained about a person at the north landing who had been given a job Corrin considered beyond them.

I said, after a while, that I would like to look at it.

“Then look at it,” said Corrin. “It’s on a shelf.”

---

I went back the next afternoon in the quiet hour.

Up close there is writing on them. There is writing on all of them, cut shallow into the flat of the surface and the same colour as the rest of it, so that from the middle of a room a promise object is a blank grey thing and from a hand’s width away it is a sentence. I had been on that world several days and had never once been close enough to one to notice.

That is what a promise object is. It is the words a person undertook, made into something that person has to carry.

It takes about as long to read as anything else. It is not long. Whoever said it was not making a speech.

> **We undertake to close the crossings between the worlds because passage is carrying the rules of one world into another, and the failures are killing people.**
>
> **We acknowledge that this will strand travelers, divide families and communities, and close routes without the consent of everyone who uses them.**
>
> **We will keep the crossings closed while passage remains unsafe.**
>
> **We promise to restore them when passage can be made safe.**

---

I read it four times.

**They closed them.** Not something. Not a force. *They*. The word is in the first line, and it is the whole of the answer I came here for.

**Because the rules were getting out.** A rule belongs to a world. Take enough across enough times and the rules go across too, and land somewhere they do not fit, and a world that was not built to hold that rule has to hold it anyway.

And the failures were **killing people.** Not *were dangerous*. Not *caused difficulties*. Written flat, in the middle of a sentence about procedure, by somebody who was not trying to make me feel anything about it.

**Then the second line**, which I did not expect. Not *we hope this will be all right*. A list: stranded, divided, cut off, and done to a great many people who were never asked.

They wrote that down **before** they did it, in the same document.

**And then they promised to put it back.**

---

That is the fourth line and it is why the thing is still on the shelf.

The promise has a condition in it: *when passage can be made safe.* And Corrin, not looking up from a rope, had already told me that nobody ever managed that.

The promise says the crossings go back when passage is safe. Passage was never made safe. So it never came due, and it was never discharged, and the person who brought it home carried it for the rest of their life.

Then they died, and the object did not.

Somebody put it on a shelf.

It has been there longer than the shelf.

---

I stayed in the weigh-house until the evening boats came in and the floor filled up and somebody moved the bucket.

I had the answer to the question I arrived with. All of it. It took a few sentences of Corrin’s and four lines of somebody’s minutes and about six minutes of an afternoon.

I hung in the middle of that room for a long time.

## Chapter 6: Nowhere to Put It

I went back the next morning to find the part where somebody was wrong.

I did not admit to myself what I was doing until it had failed. I was not going back to understand it. I understood it by the second reading.

I was going back to find out **whose fault it was.**

Eight worlds of pieces, and somewhere in the assembling I had decided what the answer would be shaped like. Not the details. The shape. Somebody did this. And at the bottom of it there would be a person, and I would know what they had done, and I would know what to think about them.

---

**It is not in the first line.** I looked hardest there, because that is where you would put it, and I read it enough times to be certain, because I wanted it to be there. There is no *we have decided*. There is no sentence in it where somebody is protecting their own convenience. People were dying. That is what they had.

**It is not in the second line either**, and the second line is where I stopped being able to do this properly.

Nobody made them put it in. They could have written *regrettable disruption*. They could have written nothing at all, and it would have sat on that shelf for the same number of generations and I would have read it and known no different.

**They wrote down the worst true thing about what they were doing, before they did it, and then they did it.**

---

So I went at the people afterward, which was the last thing I had.

Somebody was supposed to open them again. Somebody did not. A whole world of people, over an enormous number of years.

I put it to Corrin at the north landing while Corrin was arguing with a man about a weight.

“They never opened them.”

“No.”

“Somebody must have tried.”

“People did try,” said Corrin. “Long time ago. It’s in the histories, I think. My grandmother knew more about it than I do.” Corrin took the weight ticket off the man and looked at it and gave it back. “Nobody ever got it safe. That’s the whole of what I know.”

“But somebody could have...”

“Could have what?” said Corrin, and it was not a challenge, it was a genuine question from somebody who had a boat to unload. “Made it safe? Then they would have. It’s not that they didn’t want it. It’s that nobody ever worked out how.”

---

And that was the last route I had, and it went the way the others did. The promise did not say *reopen the crossings no matter what*. It said *when passage can be made safe*, and it never was, and a condition does not come due because generations got tired of waiting.

---

So I hung over the north landing and stopped looking.

**They were right and they did an enormous wrong thing**, and both of those are in the same four lines, on the same object, and neither one cancels the other.

**I am still angry.** For about an hour I thought the anger had gone somewhere and it has not. There are people who never met their own families. There are roads that stop. There is a courier on a world I will not name with a road on her map that does not finish, and she will never know why, and I do.

**What I have not got is anybody I can point at and make the whole of it theirs.**

There is no name at the bottom of the thing on that shelf. If there were a hundred names I would not know which of them to pick, because whatever went wrong in it went wrong across all of them at once, and I cannot get hold of any single one and say: **you.**

---

I told Corrin that afternoon.

We were on the boat. Corrin was doing the thing with the fitting again, which I had by then worked out was a fitting Corrin had been meaning to replace for two years.

I said: “It was people. They closed the crossings because people were dying. They knew what it would do to everybody and they did it anyway.”

Corrin said, “Yes.”

---

That was all.

Not dismissively. Corrin was not being cold and was not brushing me off and did not think I was being tiresome. Corrin simply already knew it, and had known it since childhood, the way you know what the big building at the end of your street was built for.

There was a pause of about four seconds while Corrin worked out that I had wanted something else and did not know what.

Then Corrin said, “It’s an old thing,” in the tone of somebody being kind about it, and went back to the fitting, and asked whether I had seen what they had done to the crane at the south landing.

**And that is what the largest fact in eight worlds is worth on an ordinary afternoon.**

---

We went down to the south landing and looked at the crane, which had been repaired by somebody who should not have been allowed to.

Corrin was extremely funny about it. There was a whole section of that afternoon in which two people I did not know joined in and everybody had a view about the crane and about the man who had mended it and about his father, who had apparently been worse.

Then the evening boats came in, and a load went wrong, and Corrin was in the middle of it for an hour, and I fetched two people because that is the one thing I can do.

It got dark. The floor of the weigh-house emptied. Somebody upriver was singing badly.

And I said that I supposed I would be going on soon.

Corrin was coiling the line and did not look up.

“You may as well stay till this load’s off,” Corrin said.

So I did.

## Chapter 7: The Boat Goes Somewhere Else

I did not know it had been decided until I saw the man on the landing.

He came up on the morning boat from downriver, a heavy square person called Deth who runs cargo out of a town two days down and who had, apparently, been asking about a hull for a year and a half. He had a bag and a folded paper and the manner of somebody who had made a trip specifically.

Corrin met him at the water and they went and looked at the boat together for about an hour, in the way people look at a thing they are both pretending not to want.

I did not understand what I was watching until they got to the money.

---

**Nobody told me.** Corrin did not announce it, or explain it, or come and find me to say what was going to happen. Corrin got up that morning and did it.

I found out the way anybody on that waterfront found out, which is by being nearby.

---

Here is how a boat changes hands in the Landings.

You do it at the weigh-house, because everything gets done at the weigh-house, and you do it in the middle of the day when there are people about. Not for ceremony. Because a boat is the largest thing most people ever own, and if it moves from one person to another then a good number of people need to have seen it happen and be able to say so afterward.

You need a witness. It is a real job with a small fee and there is a list of who does it, and Heth was on the list and was in the building and was available.

You need the paper, and the paper gets read out.

And then you both say what you are doing, out loud, in front of everybody, because that is the whole of it. There is no signature that matters more than the saying.

---

They did it just after midday and it took eleven minutes.

Heth read the paper. Deth said what he was buying and what he was paying. Corrin said what Corrin was selling.

Somebody’s crate was in the way and had to be moved twice.

Then Deth put the money due that day on the scale table because that is how it is done, and Heth counted it, and Corrin said the thing that finished it, which is a set form of words about a hull and its fittings passing from one person to another as of that day.

And Deth got a small object in his graspers, because he had promised the second half by the turn of the season.

And Corrin got nothing, because Corrin had just done a thing rather than promised one.

---

**The big one stayed exactly where it was.**

I was watching for it. I do not think anybody else in that building was.

Nothing happened to it. Nothing moved on that ridge at all. Corrin sold the boat and turned round and the thing that had been on the back for years was on the back, in the same place, settled in behind the traffic.

---

Nilla was there.

I had not seen Nilla come in and I do not know how long they had been standing at the back of the building. Long enough. Nilla did not come forward and did not say anything during any of it, and when Heth finished reading the paper Nilla looked at the floor and kept looking at it.

Afterward Deth wanted to talk to Corrin about the pump, and did, for about ten minutes, while the rest of the building went back to work.

Then Corrin came over to where Nilla was.

---

“You didn’t have to do it like that,” said Nilla.

“I did.”

“You could have just gone on carrying it.”

“I could have,” said Corrin. “For years. I’d have been all right. That’s not the same as it being all right.”

Nilla did not say anything.

“I’d have been carrying it and you’d have been watching me carry it,” said Corrin. “Every day. And every year it’d be a bit more your fault, in your head, and it isn’t your fault, and there was no version of that where you got to stop thinking about it.”

“So instead everybody can see you broke one.”

“Yes.”

“For the rest of your life.”

“Yes,” said Corrin.

---

Nilla said, after a while, that it was a good boat.

“It’s a good boat,” Corrin agreed. “Deth’ll ruin it inside three years. He doesn’t listen about the stern.”

And then, in the tone of somebody who has been waiting a considerable time to say a thing and has decided to say it while there is a crate being moved four feet away:

“It was never about the boat. You know that.”

“I know.”

“I said it because you were eleven and everything had gone and somebody had to say what was going to happen next.” Corrin was not looking at Nilla. “So I said what was going to happen next. That was the job. That’s what it was for.”

---

“And now it’s for you doing what you’re doing,” said Corrin. “Which is not on a boat.”

Nilla said something I did not hear.

“Well,” said Corrin, “it’s done now.”

---

They walked back down to the water together and Nilla went off up the street to the workroom and Corrin stood on the landing where the boat had been and looked at the place where it had been for a while.

Then Corrin went to see about a job.

---

I stayed in the weigh-house after they had gone, up in the rafters where I had done my counting on the first day.

Down below, four people were arguing about a weight. Somebody dropped something. Heth put the witnessed paper in a box with a great many other witnessed papers and shut the box.

I had looked for a fourth thing and there was not one. What happened instead was that somebody picked the least bad of three bad things and then went to see about a job.

**The promise was broken. It was going to stay broken.**

**And it was the right thing to do.** I could not make it come out any other way.

## Chapter 8: Not Yet

The Landings did not make an occasion of it.

A boat changed hands. That happens perhaps four times a year in a town that size and it is interesting for about a day. Two people asked Corrin what the plan was. A man at the scales had a view about the price, which he shared with everybody, and which was wrong.

By the second day after the sale it was not being discussed.

There was a great deal else on. A load of something came in that nobody had ordered and had to be argued about for an entire morning. The crane at the south landing broke again, differently. It rained, which apparently it had not done for some time, and everybody had opinions about the river coming up.

**People saw it.** Of course they saw it. That is what the thing is for.

A woman at the scales noticed on the first morning and said so, not unkindly, just noticing, the way you would notice somebody had cut their hair. She said, “That’s gone and broken, then.”

And Corrin said, “It has.”

And she said, “Right,” and went back to the weights.

That happened three or four times in two days and it went the same way every time. Somebody sees. Somebody says. Corrin agrees. And then there is a load to get off, and the conversation is over, and neither of them thinks about it again until the next person notices.

**Nobody asked what it was.** They can all read one if they get close enough, and none of them got close enough, and that is not delicacy. It is that a broken promise on a person you have known for thirty years is a fact about them, and the fact is the part that matters, and the details are somebody else’s business and always were.

So the cost is exactly what Corrin said it would be. Everybody can see. **And nobody makes anything of it**, which turns out to be a different thing from nobody noticing.

---

Corrin went to work for somebody else.

That took two days to arrange and was not tragic. There is more freight on that river than there are people to move it, and a person of thirty years’ standing who has just sold a hull is a person several outfits would like to talk to. Corrin took a place on a bigger boat working the same run, and complained about the owner, and complained about the boat, and got on with it.

“It’s a worse boat,” Corrin told me.

“Is it?”

“It’s a much worse boat,” said Corrin, with enormous satisfaction, and went off to tell somebody else about it.

---

Nilla was in the workroom.

I went up a few days after and the door was open and the noise was coming out of it, and Nilla was doing something to an enormous hawser that had come off the crane, and did not stop when I came in, and told me eleven things about crane hawsers that I did not know and have mostly retained.

There was a rope on the bench with a splice in it that Nilla had done that morning and was not satisfied with.

Nilla took it out while I was there, and did it again.

---

We did not talk about the boat. Nilla mentioned it once, sideways, in the middle of something else, that Deth had already had the stern up on the hard and that Corrin had been right, and then went back to the hawser and did not raise it again.

I do not think Nilla is at peace with it. I think Nilla is going to think about it for a long time.

But there is a workroom two streets back from the water with a door that stands open, and the boats come to it from three landings away, and there is room in it for somebody to spend years getting better at the only thing they ever wanted to do.

**That is what it bought.** Corrin carries the proof of it. Nilla gets to keep getting up and doing the thing.

---

Then the excuses started properly.

I said, some days later, that I supposed I would be going on.

“Not before the market boat,” said Corrin. “You’ll want to see the market boat. It’s the one thing that happens here.”

The market boat came in the next morning. It is a boat with a market on it. I will not pretend it was not worth seeing, because there were four people selling four different kinds of the same thing off the same deck and shouting about it, and one of them had a bird.

“There,” said Corrin afterward. “Now you’ve seen the market boat.”

---

I said, a couple of days after that, that I really would be going.

“You can’t go today,” said Corrin. “It’s the weighing.”

The weighing is a thing they do four times a year where the scales get checked against a set of standard weights kept in a box, and it is administratively necessary and takes about an hour and is watched by the entire town for reasons I never established. Somebody read numbers out. Everybody agreed the scales were the same as last time. Two people said they had known they would be.

It was, and I say this having watched a boat race on another world, the least interesting event I have ever attended.

Corrin stood beside me through the whole of it looking perfectly content.

---

There were two more.

There was a thing about a delivery that Corrin thought I would find interesting, and did not explain, and which turned out to be a delivery.

And there was one where Corrin did not even bother with a reason. Corrin said, one evening, “You may as well stay,” and did not put anything after it, and looked at the water.

---

**I knew what was being asked by then.** I had known since about the weighing.

Here is what it was, as far as I could ever work it out.

I am the only thing Corrin has ever met that cannot be given anything to hold. Everybody else on that river arrives with a load and leaves with a different load. That is not a complaint. Corrin chose almost all of it and would choose most of it again, and is the sort of person other people ask, and has never once in my hearing wished to be somebody who is not asked.

But I went about that town carrying nothing at all, and there was no arrangement anybody in the Landings could make that would put one of its promises on me.

And while I was there, that was **in the room.**

Corrin did not want to come with me. Corrin never once asked where I was going, or where I had been, or what was on the other side of anything. Corrin wanted the market boat, and the weighing, and a delivery, and one more evening.

**Corrin wanted not yet.**

---

I went the next morning.

I told Corrin the night before, and Corrin said right, and asked whether I wanted to see the north landing at first light because the light comes up the river in a particular way at that time of year.

“That’s another one,” I said.

“It is,” Corrin agreed.

And then, after a moment: “It’s a good one, though. The light.”

---

It was a good one. The light comes up the river and hits the whole waterfront at once from downstream, and for about four minutes everything on that bank is the colour of the inside of a shell, and then it is an ordinary morning and somebody starts shouting about a crate.

We watched it from the north landing.

Then Corrin said there was a boat to get on and went down the steps and got on it, and did not look back up, and that is exactly what I would have wanted if I had known to want anything.

---

I went out along the water.

I passed the weigh-house on the way. The old thing was where it has always been.

---

The crossing is downriver, out past the last of the landings, where the bank goes to reed and nobody has any reason to be.

I did not hurry. But I went.

Behind me the Landings was starting.

The first boats out. The scales already going. Somebody upriver singing the same bad song, badly. A load coming off at the middle landing and a man shouting a number at somebody who was shouting a different number back.

And out in the middle of it, on a worse boat than the one they used to have, a person with a full ridge and one thing on it that is never coming off, complaining about the stern.
