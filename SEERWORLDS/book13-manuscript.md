## Chapter 1: The Long Way Round

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to twelve worlds. I do not know how I get from one to the next, and I have stopped expecting to find out, which is a different thing from not minding.

This one started at the top of a valley, where two long ridges come down and nearly meet.

Below me the ground opened out into the best-looking country I had seen in a while: a wide shallow valley running away south, with settlements scattered down it, smoke in three or four places, and a river doing the sensible thing along the bottom. There was a road down the middle of it that I could see from a mile up. It was pale and straight and looked like somebody had drawn it.

I went down toward it, and that is where I met the fork, and the fork is where I stopped understanding anything.

---

Two ways went the same direction.

One of them was that pale road. Flat. Hard. Dry even where the ground either side was not. It was the sort of surface that makes you want to travel on it for no reason.

The other one was a mess. Rutted, soft in patches, with grass coming up the middle of it in a way that suggested nobody had been down it in a while.

A woman came up to the fork carrying a load on a frame against her chest, which is how they carry things there. She was tall, a head above a person if you know people, with very long legs and a small head set forward, and she was walking the way they all walk, which is unhurried and about twice as fast as it looks.

She stopped at the fork for about a second.

Then she took the bad one.

I thought: she is going somewhere else. That is obviously it. Two roads, two destinations, and the poor one goes wherever she is going.

Then a man came up behind her with an empty frame and took the bad one too.

Then two more people, together, arguing about somebody’s roof.

I followed them for about a mile, floating along over the ruts, waiting for the bad road to peel off somewhere the good one did not go.

It did not. They ran parallel the whole way and rejoined at the bottom, forty paces apart, at the same place.

So I went back up to the fork and waited to see if anybody would do the sensible thing.

Eleven people went past. Nine took the bad road.

---

The tenth was a boy of about fourteen who took the good one, and got about six steps, and then a woman behind him said his name in a particular voice, and he came back and took the bad one, complaining.

The eleventh was an old man with a stick who took the good road, unapologetically, and I decided he was my best chance.

I said I had a question that was going to sound stupid.

He said most were.

I said there were two roads that went to exactly the same place, and one of them was excellent, and everybody was choosing the other one, and I could not work out what was wrong with the excellent one.

He stopped walking and looked at me properly for the first time, the way they all did once they noticed I was floating and not standing.

“Nothing’s wrong with it,” he said.

“Then why...”

“Because nothing’s wrong with it.” He started walking again. “It’s had everybody down it for forty years. It’ll be fine if nobody goes down it till spring. That one won’t.”

“That one won’t what?”

“Be fine.” He said it the way you would say *water’s wet.* “Give it a year of nobody and it’ll be gone. That’s why they’re on it. Keeping it.”

I said the two roads finished in the same place, so what did it matter which one went.

“Because Odden’s farm is halfway up the bad one,” he said, “and the good one doesn’t go anywhere near it. Ends up the same place. Doesn’t pass the same places.”

I said, and I want to record that I said this out loud, in the tone of somebody being very slow: “Keeping it how?”

He stopped again.

“You’re not from here at all, are you,” he said, and then he explained the rule of that world to me in about four sentences, standing on a road, in the manner of a man explaining to a foreigner that the sun comes up.

---

Here it is.

**Ground remembers being walked on.**

If people go along a route, it gets better. Not tidier. *Better*. It firms up. It levels off. It drains. It behaves like ground that expects to be walked on, and the more it is walked on, the more it expects it, and the easier it gets.

And if people stop, it goes the other way. It softens. It breaks up. Things grow over it. And after long enough it is not a road, and there is no evidence there ever was one, and you cannot get to wherever it went.

Nobody knows why. Nobody I asked knew, and they all gave the same answer, which is a sort of dip of the head that means *what a childish question*, followed by some version of *it’s the ground, that’s all.* They find the question about as interesting as we would find *why does water go downhill*, which is to say they have all wondered about it once, at nine, and then got on with their lives.

So: the pale road is pale because everybody uses it. The bad road is bad because the quarry it used to serve closed twenty years ago. And nine people out of eleven were walking the bad one that morning **on purpose**, out of their way, for no personal reason at all, because if they stop, in a year or two there will not be a road there, and there is a farm halfway up it.

That is the whole of it. That is the rule of that world, and everything that happened to me there came out of it.

---

They are called the Tarrow, and I want to describe them properly, because they are built for exactly one thing.

Tall and narrow, with a high straight back and a small head set forward. Two long light arms held close in front, not much good for gripping. And legs like nothing I have seen anywhere: long, jointed high, and completely tireless. A Tarrow walking looks like something that could keep doing that until the world ran out.

But the important part is the feet.

Broad and flat and splayed, with a hard sole that thickens the more you use it. And they read each other’s feet the way you might read hands. You can tell what somebody’s life has been from their soles: whether they have carried loads, whether they are on the road all day, whether they have had an easy time of it. *How are your soles* is small talk there. People say it the way you would say *how have you been.*

Nobody was impressed by anything I could do. But every single person who saw me float noticed within about four seconds, and stopped, and looked, and said some version of *you’re not touching it*.

I said I never do.

Almost all of them said the same thing back, in the same tone, and I did not understand yet why they found it so remarkable. It was this:

**”Then you don’t owe anything.”**

## Chapter 2: Whose Turn

The place at the bottom of the valley is called Bream, and it sits where the main road, the Slip road they call it, meets the branch that goes up the western arm.

It is a junction town and it behaves like one. Everything passes through and nothing stops long. There is a yard, a place that feeds people, a great deal of freight, and an enormous amount of arguing.

The arguing is about walking, and it never stops, and it took me a day and a half to understand that it is not small talk. It is the central business of that valley.

---

Derr keeps the allocations.

Derr is in the sixties, works out of a room off the yard with a table and a great many lists, and does the numbers that decide who walks what. I met Derr because Derr came to find me, having heard there was a thing in Bream that went along roads without touching them, and having thought about that for about an hour before deciding it was interesting for a reason nobody else had spotted.

“You don’t wear it,” Derr said.

I said I did not follow.

“Walk a road, you wear it. Improves it.” Derr tapped the table. “Everything that moves in this valley either improves a road or is too small to count. You’re the first thing I’ve met that’s big enough to matter and doesn’t.”

I said I was not sure that was a compliment.

“It isn’t anything,” said Derr. “It’s unusual. Sit down. You can’t sit down, can you.”

---

Here is the arithmetic of that valley, which Derr explained to me over two afternoons and which I found genuinely gripping, and I am aware of what that says about me.

There are only so many people in the Slip, and they can only walk so much. Derr’s list puts a number on it: **eleven hundred walking-days a season**. That is the whole budget. That is what the valley has.

And there are four branches off the main road that Derr allocates. Four open ones. I will come to why I am putting it that way.

The main road takes care of itself. Everybody uses it because everybody is going along it anyway, and it keeps its condition without anybody thinking about it. Derr does not spend a single day on the Slip road and never has.

The branches are the whole job. A branch has to be walked or it goes, and mostly it has to be walked by more people than actually need to go up it, and the difference is what Derr’s eleven hundred days are for. You pay people to walk. You send freight the long way. You put the season’s meeting up a branch instead of in Bream so that forty people have to travel to it.

**Treaders** are the people paid to do it directly. They walk a branch out and back, out and back, all season, in order to keep it in condition. It is honest, dull, badly paid work with a faint whiff of charity about it, and every settlement in the valley argues about the cost of it every single year.

“You’ll hear people say treading’s making work,” Derr said. “You’ll hear that a lot. And then the same people will tell you the Corren branch has got further this year, and they’ll not put the two together.”

I asked what *got further* meant.

Derr looked pleased, which I learned was Derr’s response to any question with a good answer.

“Corren’s fourteen miles,” Derr said. “It’s fourteen miles this year and it was fourteen miles last year and it’ll be fourteen miles when we’re both dead. But it was two hours and now it’s four. So it’s further. That’s what further means.”

I said that on other worlds, distance was distance.

“Then those worlds,” said Derr, “have got nothing at stake.”

There is one thing Derr could not tell me, and it took me a while to believe that nobody could.

I asked when the Corren branch would close.

“Don’t know.”

I said that seemed like the single most useful number in the room, and that Derr had a number for everything else.

“I’ve got a number for what’s happened,” said Derr. “Nobody’s got one for what’s next.” Derr pushed the list away. “I can tell you exactly what that road is today. Anybody in the valley can, it’s not clever. And I can tell you it’s in the bad stretch: it’s gone soft, it’s thinning, it’s the state where roads go. Everyone agrees on that and nobody argues about it.”

“But not when.”

“Not when. Might hold three years. Might go this winter if it’s a wet one and the low ground takes it.” Derr shrugged with the ears, which is a thing they do. “It’s soil and rain and drainage and what’s underneath, and none of that’s written on the top of it. Roads tell you where people went. They don’t tell you what the weather’s going to do.”

So that is the shape of it, and everybody there lives inside it: **you can tell exactly how bad a road is now, and nobody can tell you exactly when bad becomes gone.**

---

The comedy of that valley is entirely about whose turn it is.

I heard, in one week: a forty-minute argument about whether going halfway up a branch and coming back counted as using it, which ended without resolution and had apparently been going for six years. A man complaining that his neighbor had been taking the long way to the mill and telling everyone about it. A furious dispute over a shortcut somebody had started using between two yards, which had, inevitably and to the shortcutter’s private delight, become a real road, thereby ruining the trade of everybody on the long way around.

And I heard a great many people complain that the Slip road is boring, which they know is ridiculous, and say anyway.

The best of it is the children.

**Brann** is twelve, and I met Brann on the second day, going up the Corren branch with a bag containing, as far as I could establish, nothing that anybody needed.

I asked what was in the bag.

“Nothing,” said Brann.

I asked where it was going.

“Corren.”

I asked why.

Brann stopped, put the bag down, and gave me a look I have received on four worlds now, which is the look of a child who has been asked to explain something they have already explained to an adult that week.

“Because the road wants walking,” said Brann, “and I’m the one nobody needs for anything.”

Then Brann picked the bag up and said, in the exact tone of somebody who has just thought of it and has in fact been thinking of it for some time:

“You could do it.”

I said I could go up the branch, certainly, and would be happy to.

“No,” said Brann. “*Do* it. Instead of me.”

And that is where I had to explain, for the first time on that world, the thing about me that actually matters there: that I could go up and down that branch a hundred times and it would make no difference whatsoever, because I do not touch it. I weigh nothing. The ground does not know I have been there.

Brann considered this.

“So you’re no use at all,” Brann said.

I said that appeared to be the case.

“Right,” said Brann, with enormous satisfaction, and went off up the branch.

---

There is a meeting at the turn of the season, in Bream, where the eleven hundred days get allocated.

I had assumed Derr simply decided. Derr was insulted by this for about four seconds and then explained, at length, that Derr prepares the figures, hears the claims, chairs the thing, and writes down the answer. **And the room votes it.** Everybody with a claim comes and makes it in front of everybody else with a claim, and then they settle it between them.

“Wouldn’t have it any other way,” said Derr. “You want a branch shut, you shut it in front of the people who live up it. That’s the whole of the system and it’s a good one.”

I asked what they decide on.

“Whatever’s put in front of them.” Derr counted it off. “How many live up there. What comes down it. Whether there’s anybody old or anybody expecting. What the road’s doing. What we did last year. Whether somebody stands up and makes a case worth hearing.”

I said the road’s condition was only one of those.

“Course it is,” said Derr. “It’s the only one nobody argues about, mind. Everything else you can dispute till supper.”

I asked whether it was contentious.

“Somebody loses it every year,” said Derr. “Every year. Four branches, eleven hundred days. I have never once come out of that room with everybody satisfied, and if I did I’d assume I’d made a mistake.”

## Chapter 3: Fourteen Miles

Isk treads the Corren branch, and has for nine years, and I went out along it with Isk on my fourth day in the valley.

Isk is in the forties, and is the driest person I have ever met on any world. Not unkind. Not sour. Just entirely unwilling to say a thing with more weight than it deserves, which after a while becomes very funny and then becomes restful.

Isk lives in Corren, at the top end, and treads down to Bream and back as the job. So the walk we did that morning was Isk’s commute in reverse: out from Bream, up the branch, home for the night, and down again tomorrow. Nine years of it.

I asked, at the start, whether Isk minded me coming.

“Can’t stop you,” said Isk. “You don’t need the road.”

---

Fourteen miles. Four hours out.

And for the whole four hours Isk read the road to me, out loud, the way somebody reads a page.

This is the part I need to set down carefully.

Anybody on that world can read a road. It is not a skill and nobody is impressed by it. You look at the ground and you can tell how much it has been used and how recently, the way you can tell whether a coat has been worn. Children can do it. Isk can do it to about a week.

So Isk walked and talked, and it went like this: *that’s holding, that’s four days old at least, somebody’s been up here with a load since the rain, that bit’s going and it’ll be gone by spring, that’s a fox and it doesn’t count because a fox doesn’t weigh enough.*

I asked whether the fox comment was aimed at me.

“Take it how you like,” said Isk.

And around the eighth mile, where the branch goes soft along a low stretch and you can see it thinning out in both directions, I asked the question I thought I was being clever with.

I said: so the road tells you nobody much comes up here anymore.

“Tells me exactly that,” said Isk.

I said: and it tells you why.

Isk stopped walking. It was the only time Isk stopped in fourteen miles.

“No,” said Isk.

Then Isk said it the way you correct a child who has made an honest error, which is to say without any pleasure in it at all:

**”It’ll tell you how many. It won’t tell you why. Never has, never will, and anybody who says different is selling something.”**

I said that seemed like a large gap.

“It’s the only gap there is,” said Isk, and started walking again.

---

Corren is at the end of it: forty people on a shelf of ground where the western arm turns, with hill grazing above and a spring that Isk told me twice was the best on that side of the valley.

There is a pottery, which is the reason Corren exists as far as anybody outside it is concerned. And above the settlement there is a quarry that has been worked for a hundred and something years and is nearly finished.

That is the whole of the problem and I understood it before anybody explained it, because Derr had taught me the arithmetic and Isk had walked me up the evidence.

The quarry was why people went to Corren. The quarry is nearly out. So fewer people go. So the branch has weakened. So the journey has gone from two hours to four. **So fewer people go.**

Nobody did that. There is nobody to be cross with. It is a thing that is happening the way weather happens, except that weather does not usually take a settlement with it.

---

**Nessit** runs the pottery and met us at the top with clay dust on both arms and the specific brisk manner of somebody who has three things going and is doing all of them.

Nessit looked at me for about five seconds and asked one question.

“Can you carry?”

I said I could not.

“Nothing?”

I said nothing at all. No arms, no hands, no grip, and I weigh less than the air around me.

“Right,” said Nessit, and that was the end of Nessit’s interest in me as a solution to anything, and, I want to be fair, not the end of Nessit’s interest in me. We got on perfectly well for the rest of the afternoon. I was simply, from that moment, filed under *things that will not be moving any clay*, which is a category that contains almost everything in the world.

The pottery needs four cartloads of clay hauled up the branch before winter. That is the year’s firing. Without the firing there is nothing to send down in spring, and without anything to send down there is no reason for the carts to come up, and the branch has another quiet year.

I said that seemed like a lot resting on four cartloads.

“It’s how it’s always been,” said Nessit. “It’s just that it used to be easy.”

---

There was one thing I could not work out, and I asked Isk on the way back down.

I said: there are forty people up there. If walking keeps a road, why do they not simply walk it? Every day, all of them, out and back. Forty people would hold fourteen miles easily.

Isk did not laugh at me, which a lot of people would have.

“They do,” said Isk.

I said that plainly it was not working.

“They do it every day,” said Isk. “Nessit’s lot go down for clay and up with it. The grazing’s above the settlement and that’s four of them out on the top road morning and evening. Kids get sent down and back for nothing at all, same as Brann. Half of what you saw on that branch today was Corren’s own feet. That branch is still there **because** of them.”

“Then why is it failing?”

“Because forty people is forty people,” said Isk. “They’ve got a pottery and a quarry and grazing and forty people’s worth of ordinary life, and fourteen miles wants more than that. It always has. The difference used to come off the quarry traffic and now the quarry’s done, so the difference has to come off Derr’s list, and Derr’s list is eleven hundred days and four branches.”

I said, working it out as I said it: so Corren is not failing to keep its road. Corren is keeping its road and it is not enough.

“That’s it,” said Isk. “That’s the whole of it. And you’d be amazed how many people in Bream think it’s the first thing.”

---

We came down the last mile in the evening and I noticed something I had noticed twice already and had not said anything about.

Where the branch turns below Corren there is a place where the ground goes up over a shoulder and away west, and there is nothing there. No road. Not a bad road. No road at all. Grass and stone and a slope going up to a skyline.

Isk looked at it.

Isk had looked at it on the way up, too, and at the top, and now again on the way down: not for long, and not in any way that made a performance of it, just the flat unhurried look of somebody checking that a thing is still where they left it.

I did not ask about it.


## Chapter 4: The One Who Owes Nobody

Derr came and found me in the yard four days before the meeting and asked me to speak at it.

I want to set out the reasoning, because it was good reasoning and because Derr gave it to me straight, standing in a yard, with a list in one hand.

“You’ve been up all four,” said Derr.

I said I had. Corren with Isk, and the eastern two on my own, and the Bream–Ondry stretch twice because I liked it.

“And you’ve not spent a day of mine doing it.”

I said I had not.

“And you’ve not got a cousin up any of them, or a load waiting, or a share in anybody’s pottery, or a house that’s worth more if the road holds.” Derr looked at the list rather than at me. “That’s the part. It’s not that you’ve been everywhere. Traders go everywhere. It’s that you’ve been everywhere this season and you’re not carrying anything for anybody when you tell me about it.”

I said that people had come through the valley before who had no stake in it.

“They have,” said Derr. “And they’d been on the Slip road and maybe one branch, and they’d been three weeks ago, and they were on their way somewhere. You’ve been up all four in one season and you’re still here.” Derr shrugged with the ears. “It’s not a miracle. It’s just a combination I’ve not had before.”

That is the whole of it, and it is exactly as much as Derr claimed. Nobody said I was the first honest witness the valley ever had. Derr wanted better numbers than Derr could get by asking people who wanted things, and I was the only thing standing in that yard that had gone along every branch in the Slip without wanting anything at the end of any of them.

---

I said yes before Derr had finished putting the list away.

I want to be honest about why, because it was not public spirit.

I have been to twelve worlds before this one. On most of them the thing I am has been an inconvenience to be worked around. I cannot carry. I cannot lift. I cannot hold a rope or push a door or take one end of anything. On Tarrow I had spent a week being told cheerfully by everybody I met that I was no use at all, and being told it by a twelve-year-old with a bag of nothing, and I had agreed with all of them.

And then a person with a list came into a yard and said: **the thing about you is that you do not owe anything, and I need somebody who does not owe anything.**

By then I knew that owing nothing was not the same as being outside consequence. Cadd had taught me that accepting a connection could carry a duty I had not understood. Sennet had taught me that simply being somewhere could cost somebody something without any agreement at all. But Derr meant a narrower thing: no cousin up a branch, no load waiting, no share in a pottery, and no road getting better because I had gone along it. On that measure I was clean.

Nobody had ever needed that before. Nobody had ever needed this particular uselessness of mine: that I can go along a road all season without wearing it, and arrive owing nothing to anybody at the end of it.

I was pleased with myself for the rest of the afternoon.

---

Ottel does freight out of Bream, which means Ottel is on the Slip road four days out of five with a frame and a load and an opinion about the state of the surface.

I met Ottel properly that week because Ottel was the person Derr sent to check my figures on the eastern branches, which took two days, in which Ottel said perhaps forty words, of which about thirty were about the weather.

The other ten were these, and they came up because of a line on Derr’s list.

The list is ruled into columns and the branches are down the side, and there are four of them with numbers against them. And underneath the four there is a fifth name with nothing against it at all, in older ink, not crossed out.

I asked what it was.

“Ivry,” said Ottel, without looking. “That’s not a branch. That’s where a branch was.”

---

Here is Ivry, and I am going to write it down the way Ottel told it, which is flatly, while walking, without stopping.

Ivry is up the eastern arm past the Ondry turn. There were about sixty people there, and now there is nobody at all.

The road to it went over eleven years ago, and it did not go suddenly. It went the way they all go: the traffic thinned, and then the road thinned, and then it was five hours instead of two, and then it was most of a day, and everybody could see what that meant, because everybody on that world can.

So Ivry came out.

Not in a panic and not all at once. Over about two years, in the order you would expect: the ones with somewhere to go first, then the ones with work they could do elsewhere, then the families with young children, and last the ones who had been arguing all along that the road would hold if people would only walk it.

Ottel came out at nineteen with a bag, in the middle of that, with work arranged in Bream.

The last four left the summer before the road closed, and they left with everything they could carry on frames, and what they could not carry is still up there.

“Nobody had to be got out,” said Ottel. “That’s the thing people get wrong when they hear it. Nobody was trapped. We all walked down a road, same as anybody. It’s just that after we did, there wasn’t one.”

I asked whether anybody had tried to reopen it.

Ottel did not sigh, or look at me pityingly, or any of the things a person might have done. Ottel just answered.

“You’d need forty people walking a day and a half of nothing, every day, for about four years,” said Ottel. “And they’d have to eat while they did it. And it’d have to be forty people who could spare four years. And at the end of it you’d have a road to an empty village, which is a thing nobody’s going to spend four years on when Derr’s got eleven hundred days a season and can’t quite hold the ones that are still open.”

Then Ottel said the only other thing about it.

“My mother’s buried up there,” Ottel said. “So’s her mother. That’s the bit I mind.”

And then Ottel said the surface on the Ondry stretch was holding better than last year, and we walked on, and that was the entire conversation.

---

I have to put down what Ivry is, because I did not understand it at first.

**Ivry is not a story they tell to frighten people.** Nobody in Bream calls it a warning. Nobody points at it. It is not a ruin and it is not a tragedy that happened to somebody once.

**It is a place that is still entirely there**: houses, walls, a mill, two hundred years of somebody’s work, the graves of everybody who ever lived in it. **And there is no longer any such thing as going to it.** Nothing was destroyed. Nobody died of it. Sixty people simply picked up what they could carry and walked out of their own lives in the correct order, over two years, being sensible.

That was when I understood why nobody in that valley treats a closing branch as an abstraction. **Everybody in the Slip has one of those on their list**, in older ink, with nothing against it. It is what the argument is about, and it is why the people who say *the road is telling us Corren is finished* and the people who say *the road is telling us nothing of the kind* are both arguing about something real, and both know exactly what losing looks like.

---

I spent the four days before the meeting going up and down every branch in the valley, counting.

I did the Corren branch twice more, once with Isk and once without. I did the eastern two. I did the Ondry stretch three times because I wanted to see whether the surface differed by the hour, which it does not, and I was pleased to have established that.

I was extremely thorough. Every figure I had was right, and I checked them twice.

Isk asked me, on the second Corren walk, what I was going to say.

I said I was going to say what was there.

“Good,” said Isk. “Nobody does.”


## Chapter 5: What the Road Says

The meeting is held in the long room behind the yard at Bream, and everybody who has a claim comes and stands in it, and there is nowhere to sit down, which Derr told me is deliberate and about a hundred years old.

There were maybe fifty people. Nessit had come down from Corren with two others, and their feet showed it. Isk was at the back by the door, where treaders stand. There were people from the eastern branches, and a large delegation from Ondry who had brought a written list of their own, and a man from a hamlet on the second eastern branch who I gathered had lost the last three years running and had come anyway.

Derr stood at the front with the lists and said the numbers.

Eleven hundred walking-days. Four branches. Same as last season and the season before.

Then Derr said that this year there was something new, and that I had been up all four in the same season and had spent none of the valley’s days doing it, and that the room could make of that what it liked, and asked me to say what I had found.

---

So I said what was there.

I want to set out exactly what I said, because it took about ten minutes and every word of it was true.

I said the Ondry stretch was the best of the four and would hold indefinitely without a single allocated day. The Ondry delegation looked pleased, and then, as they worked out what that might mean, considerably less pleased.

I said the mill branch was steady, and the second eastern branch had weakened in the middle four miles where the ground is poor. The man who had lost three years running nodded at that as though I had said something he had been saying.

And I said the Corren branch was in the worst condition of the four by a clear margin. That it was thinning along its whole length rather than in patches. That it was four hours where the older people in the room remembered two. That it had, on my count, less than half the use of any other branch in the valley, and that most of that use was Corren’s own people going up and down their own road.

Nobody disputed any of it. Nessit did not dispute it. Isk did not dispute it. **It was all correct**, and I had checked it twice, and the room knew it was correct because every single person in that room could have walked out and checked it themselves in a day.

I would like to say that I was nervous. I was not. I was enjoying myself.

---

Then Derr asked for the claims, and the meeting turned into what it always is, which is people who need something explaining why they need it more than the person next to them.

Ondry went first and were sensible about it: two hundred people on that stretch, most of the valley’s freight, and a case that days spent there return more than days spent anywhere else. Nobody could argue with the arithmetic and several people tried.

The mill branch wanted a small increase. There is a floor under that branch already: a hundred and eighty days set aside for medicine, set aside for longer than anybody in the room had been alive, which nobody proposes to touch, ever. What the mill wanted was days on top of it, and it made its case about medicine anyway, which is the strongest case there is, and there was some grumbling about how often it gets made.

The man from the second eastern branch spoke for a long time and was heard politely.

And Nessit spoke for Corren, and I want to record that Nessit did not make a sentimental case, because Nessit is not a sentimental person. Nessit said: the pottery needs four cartloads of clay hauled up before winter. The pottery is the only thing Corren sends down. If the clay does not go up, there is no firing, and if there is no firing there is nothing to bring down in spring, and if there is nothing to bring down then next season there is even less reason for anybody to be on that road.

“So it’s not that we want the days,” said Nessit. “It’s that if we don’t get them this year, the case for next year is worse. And I’d rather have this argument now than have it again in a year with worse numbers.”

That was true too. Everybody in the room knew it was true.

And then they argued for an hour and a half.

---

I want to be fair about that hour and a half.

It was not a bad argument. Nobody shouted. Nobody was stupid. It was fifty people who all knew the same facts and could not agree what to do about them, going round and round, because the facts did not settle it and were never going to.

Ondry said Corren’s numbers spoke for themselves. Corren said numbers were not the only thing on Derr’s list and never had been. Somebody said Corren had a spring worth more than the pottery; somebody else said you cannot drink a spring you cannot get to. Somebody’s aunt raised the question of whether Corren’s own walking counted toward Corren’s claim or against it, which produced twenty minutes I will not attempt to reproduce.

And through all of it I could see Derr at the front getting quieter, which I had learned meant Derr was going to have to write something down soon that somebody would hate.

I thought I could help.

I remembered Ebb. I did. I had already learned that good measurements can leave out the part that matters. So I asked myself what these measurements left out.

The answer was *why*. Isk had told me that plainly. A road tells you how many and never why.

And I thought that was the virtue of it. Why was the part everybody in the room could argue about until dark. Use was the part nobody disputed. I thought taking *why* out was how I would keep myself from choosing for them.

---

Here is what I said. It is nine sentences.

I said that I had noticed something about the way the room argued, which was that nobody disputed the roads. Every other thing raised in that hour and a half had been contested by somebody, and reasonably: how many live where, what goes up and comes down, whose case was stronger, what was owed from last year. But not one person had contested a single figure about the condition of a road.

I said that seemed remarkable to me, and that on other worlds I had not often seen fifty people agree on anything.

I said that if there was one measure in the valley that nobody argued about, it seemed to me that measure could do work that the arguing could not.

And I said: let the roads decide it.

Then I said how, because a thing like that is no use unless you can say how.

**First**, set aside the medicine floor, which is a hundred and eighty days and is not anybody’s to argue about. **Second**, take out the branches that do not need the days at all: Ondry holds itself and everybody in the room already knew it, and spending days there is spending days on a road that is going to be fine either way. **Third**, divide the nine hundred and twenty days left among the branches that are actually contesting it, **in proportion to the use their roads currently show.**

That is the whole of it, and every number in it is something anybody in the valley could go out and check before supper.

And then nobody in that room has to stand up and say that one settlement matters more than another. **The measurement does that part**, and the measurement has no cousin in Ondry and no share in a pottery and nothing to gain either way.

I said I did not think it was a perfect method. I said I thought it was the only one in the room that nobody could accuse of being unfair to them personally.

And I said, last, that it was not a new idea, because Derr had told me on my second day that the road was already one of the things the meeting weighed. All I was suggesting was that they weigh the one thing they agreed on and set aside the things they did not.

Then I stopped talking.

---

I want to put down what happened next carefully, because it was not dramatic.

The room went quiet for a moment.

Then a man from Ondry said, slowly, that it was the first thing anybody had said in an hour that was not an argument for somebody’s own branch.

Somebody else said it would at least be quick.

Somebody said it sounded fair.

The mill spoke against it, on the grounds that medicine is not a road measurement, and there was general agreement that medicine should be kept out of any such scheme, and Derr wrote that down.

Nessit said, “You know what that does to our branch.”

And somebody near the front said, not unkindly, that they did, and that Nessit’s branch had the lowest use in the valley by a clear margin and that was not the Dot’s doing or anybody’s, and what did Nessit want instead, because if there was a fairer way to split eleven hundred days between four branches, the room had been trying to find it for an hour and a half and would be glad of it.

Nessit did not have one.

“No,” said Nessit. “I don’t.”

And that was the whole of the objection, and it was a fair answer to it, and the room moved on.

Isk was at the back by the door and did not say anything at all, and I looked, because by then I had learned to look at Isk when I wanted to know whether I had said something foolish, and Isk was doing what everybody else in the room was doing, which was thinking it over.

Then Derr said it would be put, and put it, and the room voted, and it carried comfortably, not overwhelmingly, but comfortably, with the mill exempted and a note about medicine.

And I would like to be able to say that I felt something at that moment. A doubt. A cold patch.

I did not. I thought it had gone well.

I thought: **fifty people came into a room with the same facts and no way to agree, and I found them a way through that nobody could call unfair, and I did it by pointing at the one thing in that valley that never lies.**

I was so pleased with myself that I went out afterward and did the Ondry stretch again in the dark, for no reason at all, because I wanted to be moving.


## Chapter 6: One Cartload

The numbers went up on the board outside the toll of the yard two days later, in Derr’s hand, and they were not dramatic.

Medicine kept its hundred and eighty, unchanged as it has been for ninety years. Ondry got nothing, as agreed, because Ondry holds itself. The mill and the second eastern branch took the rest between them, and the column came to eleven hundred exactly, because Derr’s hand does not make mistakes with a column.

Corren: one hundred and ten.

Corren had four hundred and forty last season.

---

I did not know what that meant. I had to be told what it meant, by a man in the yard who was not talking to me and had no idea I was listening, explaining it to somebody who had just come in off a boat.

It means Isk, and one other treader three days a week instead of six.

It means the freight that used to go up the branch because there were days allocated to send it that way now goes the short way to Ondry, because there are no days on it.

And it means the clay.

The pottery needs four cartloads before winter. A cartload is two people and a beast and the better part of two days on a branch that takes four hours in each direction, and the reason it has always been possible is that those two days came out of Derr’s allocation, because moving the clay is also walking the road, and the valley has always paid for both at once.

At one hundred and ten days, with the treading to cover, there is enough for one.

Nessit gets one cartload of clay.

There will be no winter firing.

---

I want to put down what I did next, because I did it for two days and it did not work.

I went and checked the roads.

All four of them, again, in the same order, counting the same way I had counted before the meeting. I did the Corren branch twice, and the low stretch below the shoulder three times, because that was the worst of it and the part I had reported hardest.

Every figure was right. Every one of them. Ondry was holding as I had said, and had been given nothing, and was fine. Corren was in the condition I had described, to the yard.

I could not find one thing I had said that was not so.

Then I went back over the meeting and checked that too.

Derr had put the proposal fairly. Nobody had been rushed. The vote had carried and nobody had been prevented from speaking against it, and one person had, Nessit, and Nessit had been asked for a better method and had said out loud, in front of everybody, that there wasn’t one.

Nobody had cheated. Nothing had been hidden. There was no bad number anywhere in it.

I remember thinking, on the second afternoon, standing over the low stretch: **then it must be all right.** Because I had been through every part of it looking for the piece that was wrong, and every part of it was correct, and if every part is correct then surely the thing is correct.

I believed it completely. I was still looking in the wrong place.

---

What made me understand it was not a person. It was the board.

I went back to the yard on the third day and read the numbers again, and I was thinking about next season, because Derr had said that the numbers get put up so people can start arguing early, and I had thought that was a joke and it is not.

And I did the arithmetic forward.

A hundred and ten days means less treading. Less treading means the branch weakens further over the winter. So at the next meeting, when somebody reads out the condition of the four branches, accurately and in good faith, with numbers nobody disputes, **the Corren branch will be worse than it is now.**

And under the method the room agreed to, a branch in worse condition with less use gets fewer days. Which means less treading. Which means fewer days.

I went along that whole chain twice, standing in front of a board in a yard, and then I understood what I had done.

**Every measurement I gave them was true. The method I gave them was not neutral.**

I never said the roads knew why anybody went anywhere. I was careful about that. What I said was *let us divide the days according to where people have already been*. That is not a way of avoiding a decision. **It is one.** It gives the future to whatever already has momentum, and a branch that is failing has none. That is what failing means.

On Ebb I had missed a cost. Here I had not missed the number. I had mistaken choosing the number for not choosing.

I did not take a side against Corren. **I handed a room of tired people a method that takes one, and I told them it was fair, and they believed me. If they use it again next season it will do the same thing again.** That is what the method is for.

And here is the part I keep coming back to. They can see it. I saw it, standing in a yard, off a board anybody can read. Derr will see it, because Derr does the columns.

But **the numbers will never be the part that tells them.** The numbers will be right every year. They will go on being right while the branch goes on getting worse, and nothing in them will ever say *this is the wrong way to use us*, because that is not a thing a number can say.

---

I went up to Corren.

Nessit was doing something to a wheel and did not stop doing it while we talked, which I had learned by then was not rudeness there but the opposite.

I said I had come to say that I was the reason for the clay.

“I know how it went,” said Nessit.

I said that it had been my proposal and that the room had voted it because of what I said, and that I had checked everything afterward and found nothing wrong with any of my figures, and that this had not turned out to be the point.

Nessit put the wheel down.

I have had people be angry at me on other worlds and I would have preferred that.

“You said what you thought,” Nessit said. “We voted. Both happened.”

I said that I would like to fix it.

“With what?” said Nessit, not unkindly and not as a challenge. As a genuine question about resources, from a person who thinks about resources all day. “You can’t carry clay. You can’t tread. You can’t vote. You’re not from here; you’d not have been let. And Derr’s not going to reopen a season’s allocation in the third week because a floating thing has had a think about it.”

I said that I supposed not.

“No,” said Nessit, and picked the wheel back up. “So that’s that, then.”

And that was that.

---

I spent the night at Corren, because I did not want to go back down.

Brann found me by the pottery in the morning and asked whether I was going to do the errand.

I said I could not; that I had never been able to; that walking a road only counts if you weigh something.

“I know,” said Brann. “I’m asking anyway. I ask everyone.”

Then Brann said, in the way twelve-year-olds do, with no cruelty in it at all and no idea what was being said:

“Is it true they gave us a hundred and ten?”

I said it was.

“That’s rubbish,” said Brann, and picked up the bag, and went off down the Corren branch with a hundred and ten days on the board behind it.


## Chapter 7: The Hard Way

The allocation did not reopen. I want to say that at the start, because I spent a day finding out that it would not and there is no story in it.

Derr heard me out properly, for about twenty minutes, with the lists on the table, and then said that the allocation is put and voted and written down and has never been reopened, because a valley where it can be reopened by whoever is loudest in the third week is a valley where nobody can plan anything.

“You’re right about the method,” Derr said. “I’ve had a look at it myself and I don’t like what it does either. That’s next season’s argument and I’ll be making it.”

I said next season was the problem.

“I know it is,” said Derr. “So does everyone up there.”

---

Corren worked it out over about four days, and the working out is the whole of what happened, and it happened at a table.

Nessit had the numbers because Nessit is the one who does numbers. Isk was there because Isk lives at the top end and treads the branch. About twenty others came and went. Nobody made a speech. It was the least dramatic important thing I have ever watched.

Here is the arithmetic they did.

A hundred and ten days covers the reduced treading and one cartload of clay. It does not put enough use on the branch to hold it.

What the branch needs to hold through a winter is not a mystery. Isk could give it to the half-day, because Isk has walked it for nine years and knows exactly what it takes. It needs something close to four hundred days of use, put on it steadily, spread through the season, by things that weigh enough to count.

They had a hundred and ten allocated.

The gap is two hundred and ninety days of walking, and there is one place in the valley it can come from, and everybody at that table lives in it.

---

Forty people. Two hundred and ninety days.

That is seven and a quarter days each, on top of everything else, in a season.

I want to write down what seven and a quarter days each actually meant, because said like that it sounds like nothing, and at the table it was not nothing, and the reason it was not nothing is that these are people with work.

**The pottery** loses whoever is walking, on the days they walk: a fortnight of pottery gone, over a season. Nessit said so flatly and then said the pottery would do it anyway, and one of the six said that was easy for Nessit to say since Nessit was not the one throwing, and Nessit said fine, come and throw then, and that was the closest thing to a row in four days.

**The grazing** wants two people morning and evening. Whoever walks is not doing that, so it falls on the same three people, and by the fourth day one of them had done the sums on what that meant for their own year and gone quiet.

**A woman called Verrin** has an arrangement with a man down at Ondry that requires her to be at Ondry in the second month, and if she spends her days on the branch she cannot afford the days to go, and she said so, and nobody argued with her, and she was not made to feel small about it. She is not walking. Four people are covering her share.

And **everybody old**. There are nine people in Corren too old to give days, and every one of them said they would, and every one of them was told, politely and by name, that they would not. That took an evening.

Nobody wept. Nobody made a stand. It was a table, and a slate, and forty people finding out what they could actually give, in the way you find out anything of that kind, which is slowly and with a lot of arguing about details.

---

By the third evening they had two hundred and seventy-seven of it.

It came off nobody in particular. The pottery’s fortnight, the three on the grazing agreeing to cover more of it, eleven separate people saying a number and having it written down, and two households who had been arguing about a wall for a year discovering they could each give four days if the other one did.

Two hundred and seventy-seven, and thirteen short, and nobody had another day in them that did not come out of something that mattered.

They went round it twice and could not close it, and there was a long stretch where nobody said anything and the slate sat there with the thirteen on it.

And Isk said: “I’ll take it.”

Thirteen days is one extra day a week over the season, on a branch Isk already walks six days out of seven. Down and up, on the day Isk would otherwise not have.

Nessit said it was still Isk’s rest day, thirteen times.

Isk said that Isk was the only one at that table whose whole job was already that road, that going seven days instead of six is one day, and that the same thirteen days off anybody else there would come out of work they could not put down.

“It’s not a sacrifice,” said Isk. “It’s arithmetic. I’m the cheapest one here.”

Nessit said it was still a lot.

“It is,” said Isk. “Put it down.”

And Nessit put it down, and it went on the slate under the eleven other numbers and the pottery’s fortnight and the wall households’ eight, and then they carried on and argued for another half hour about whether the children’s errands counted at full weight, which they do not, because Brann weighs what Brann weighs.

I want it recorded that nobody at that table treated Isk as having done something noble, and that Isk would have been embarrassed if they had, and that on the fourth morning Isk was up before anybody else because that is the job.

---

What they bought, when they had finished, is one season.

Not the branch. Not Corren. **One season**, and the room said so out loud several times, because these are not people who lie to themselves about roads. Two hundred and ninety days of their own lives to hold a road where it is now, which is bad, and it will want doing again next year, and the year after, unless something changes about why anybody goes up there.

Nessit said the thing that ended it.

“Right,” Nessit said. “That’s this year. Somebody had better find us a reason to be worth coming to, because I can’t do this again for long.”

Nobody answered that, because nobody had one.

---

Derr was not wrong.

I have to put that down, because it would be easy to leave it out and it is the truest thing about that week.

There are two hundred people on the Ondry stretch and eleven hundred days in the valley. If those two hundred and ninety days had come off Derr’s list they would have come off somewhere, and the somewhere would have been a place with more people in it than Corren, and everybody in that valley knows people in more than one place.

What Corren did was not a refutation of anything. It was forty people choosing to pay, out of their own time, a cost the valley had decided not to carry. Both of those are allowed to be true and on Tarrow both of them are.

---

I went with them.

I went out on the branch on the first morning of it, with Isk and four others, and I went the whole fourteen miles and the whole fourteen back, and I did that most days for the rest of the time I was on that world.

It made no difference at all.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the only thing I am sure of. The ground did not know I was there. Not one inch of that branch was better for my having gone along it, not on the first day and not on the fortieth. The four hours did not become three hours fifty-nine. Every step of that work was done by people with feet, and the only reason I was on the road at all was that I wanted to be.

Nobody minded. Nobody suggested I could help. Isk did not once, in forty days, say anything about it, which I noticed.

Brann said something about it on the second week, going up with the bag: that it was funny, wasn’t it, that the thing that could go anywhere was the one thing that couldn’t do this.

I said it was.

“Doesn’t mean you shouldn’t come,” said Brann.

I knew enough by then not to call that useful.

---

The branch held.

It held through the turn and into the cold, and by the end of the season it was not better and it was not worse, which is exactly what two hundred and ninety extra days buys and precisely what everybody said it would buy.

There was no winter firing. One cartload of clay does not make a firing and nobody pretended otherwise. Nessit fired what could be fired with what there was, which was not much, and it went down in the spring and was worth what it was worth.

And that was the year.


## Chapter 8: Where There Is No Road

The season turned and the valley went on being the valley.

Derr put next year’s blank columns up in the second week of the cold, which is early, and told me it was deliberate: if people are going to argue for four months it is better they start where Derr can hear them. Derr had also, I noticed, written the method down at the bottom of last season’s sheet in the same hand as everything else, with a line under it, and had said nothing about it to me at all.

The second eastern branch was already making a case. Ondry was already objecting to the case. Somebody had raised the question of whether Corren’s own two hundred and ninety days should count as use for next season’s figures, which is a genuinely difficult question, and I had opinions about it that I kept to myself for what I hope are obvious reasons.

Corren was Corren. The pottery ran short-handed. Verrin went to Ondry in the second month and came back. Nine old people continued to be told, politely and by name, that they would not be walking. The branch held at four hours, which is not good and is not gone.

And Brann still had to take the bag up, and still asked me every single time whether I would do it instead, and still knew I would not, and still asked.

That is the whole of the aftermath. Nothing was resolved. It was simply next.

---

Isk and I went up on an ordinary morning in the cold, with a load for the pottery on Isk’s frame, and we came to the place where the branch turns below Corren and the ground goes up over the shoulder and away west.

There is nothing there. There has never been anything there. It is grass and stone going up to a skyline, and I had been looking at it for a season because Isk had been looking at it for nine years.

I went up it.

I want to be clear that this was not a demonstration, because I did not know I was demonstrating anything. There was a bird on the skyline doing something I wanted to see, and the way to see it was to go up, so I went up, the way I have gone up every hill I have ever wanted to look at, and I got about two hundred paces and looked at the bird and came back down.

It took perhaps four minutes.

When I came back Isk had put the frame down and was not looking at the skyline anymore, and was looking at me.

I said the bird had been nothing much.

Isk did not answer that. Isk went two steps off the edge of the branch, off the hard part and onto the grass I had just gone up over, and put one foot down and leaned on it.

It went in to the ankle. Not a bog and not a hole. The turf simply parted under a Tarrow foot the way a crust parts, and underneath it was black and wet and had no bottom that mattered, and the print stood there filling while we looked at it. Isk pulled the foot back onto the branch and knocked the worst of it off against the edge, which is a thing I had watched Isk do a hundred times without once thinking about what it meant.

Two paces away, the branch held a loaded frame and a cart and forty people’s winter.

“That’s the whole of it,” said Isk. “That’s not a bad patch. That’s what ground is, before anybody’s been on it.”

Then Isk said: “Take me with you.”

---

I did not answer immediately, because I did not understand what was being asked. I thought Isk meant up the slope, and I began to say that of course Isk could come, and then I stopped, because I heard it.

“Where there’s no road,” said Isk.

And then, because Isk is Isk and does not leave a thing half said:

“I’ve watched you leave the road whenever it suited you. Across the soft places. Over the edges. And today you went two hundred paces up that and came back down like there was no difference between it and the branch. And there isn’t, to you.” Isk was not upset. This is the flattest I have ever heard anybody say anything important. “There’s nowhere in this world I can’t get to if there’s a road. I’ve been down the Slip to the water. I’ve been over the eastern arm. I could walk out of this valley tomorrow and keep going for a month and I’d never once be somewhere I couldn’t be.”

I said that was more than most people manage anywhere.

“It is,” said Isk. “And it’s all road. Every step of it is somewhere somebody’s already been enough times to make it hold.” Isk looked at the shoulder. “I’ve wanted to know what’s over there since I was about nine. Not what’s there. I know what’s there; it’s more hill. I’ve wanted to have *been* there. And I’m forty-three and I’m the best walker in this valley and there is not one day of my life where that was ever going to be possible.”

I asked why not.

I knew why not. I asked anyway, because I wanted to hear whether Isk would say the thing everybody there says, and Isk did.

“Because ground you haven’t walked doesn’t hold,” said Isk. “You know that. You’ve heard it from everyone since you got here. One person walking somewhere new doesn’t make a road, it makes a person having a bad afternoon in a bog.”

I said somebody must have made the first one, once.

“Course they did. Everything out there was new ground once and somebody wore it in.” Isk shrugged. “Forty of them, going the same way every day, for years, because they had a reason to. That’s how you get a road. It takes everybody and it takes a long time, and nobody’s spending either on a hill with nothing on it.”

---

Here is what I could have offered, and I want it on the page because I went through all of it standing on that branch.

I cannot carry. Nessit established that in my first week, and Brann established it a dozen times after, and Corren spent a whole season establishing it again while I went up and down beside them contributing nothing.

I cannot make a road. Not one inch, not in forty days.

So what Isk was asking for was not a favor I was withholding. It was not a thing I could give and did not want to. **There is nowhere for Isk to put a foot**, and I am not the reason, and there is no version of me that could be the reason.

I said all of that, badly.

Isk listened to the whole of it and then said: “I know.”

I said that if I had known any way at all...

“I know,” said Isk. “I’ve known since I was nine. I’ve had thirty-four years to work out there isn’t one.” Isk picked up the frame. “I wasn’t asking you to fix it. I’ve never said it out loud before, that’s all. There’s never been anybody to say it to who’d know what I meant.”

---

We went the rest of the way up.

Isk delivered the load to the pottery and had an argument with Nessit about whether the last cartload had been short, which it had, and which was the carter’s fault and not Nessit’s, and Nessit said as much, and Isk said the carter would say different, and Nessit said the carter always does.

Then Isk went to get something to eat, and came out, and went down the branch again because it was a treading day and there was still most of it left.

I did not go with Isk that afternoon.

I am not certain why. It was not delicacy. I think I wanted the road to be somebody’s road for a while without me alongside it not making any difference to it.

---

I went out to the shoulder in the evening on my own and looked west from it, which took four minutes, which is what it had taken in the morning.

There is more hill. Isk was right about that. There is a long slope down into a valley with nothing much in it and then more hill after that, going blue, and it is not remarkable and I would not have crossed a valley to see it.

I went back down and told Isk what was over the shoulder, in detail, because it was the only thing I had that Isk wanted and could not get.

Isk listened to all of it and asked three questions, and the third one was whether the far slope had water on it, which it did, and which apparently mattered for a reason I did not follow.

Then Isk said thank you, and meant it, and said that it was not the same.

I said I knew it was not the same.

“No,” said Isk. “But it’s not nothing. Go on, then. What color was the far one?”

I said blue, going gray at the top.

“Right,” said Isk, and that was the last we said about it, and in the morning there was a road that wanted walking.


## Chapter 9: Back

I left in the spring, and I went the long way, which took most of two days.

Nobody gave me the route. I want to be clear about that, because it is the sort of thing that could be misunderstood on a world where the ground keeps a record of everybody.

The roads there do keep a record, and it is a perfect one, and it says how much and how recently and nothing else. It does not say *who*. There is no line in that valley that has me in it. Forty days on the Corren branch and I did not put one mark on it, and the ground has never once known I was there, and it does not now.

What I had was what anybody has. I went places, and I remember going.

So I went back along it myself. Down the branch to Bream, along the Slip road north with the water on my left, up past the fork where I had spent an afternoon failing to understand why nine people out of eleven were walking on the bad road. Odden’s farm is halfway along it, still. Somebody had been up it that week.

And then off the road and up to the top of the valley, where two long ridges come down and nearly meet, which is where I arrived.

---

I went because of four words.

On the world before this one, in a dry cut in some rock, something very old came back and said *you’re late*, and I said *for what*, and it said *for coming home*. Then it was finished, and there was nobody there, and there never had been.

I have carried that around for a year not knowing what to do with it, because there is nothing to do with it. I do not know where. I do not know who said it or when or whether they had ever laid eyes on the thing I am.

But on Tarrow I learned something I had not thought about before, which is that **going back to a place is a real thing you can do.** Not a figure of speech. In that valley it is an act with a cost, and people do it deliberately, and it is most of what they talk about.

And I thought: there is one place I can go back to. I know exactly where it is. I can be standing in it in two days.

So let me find out what going back gets you.

---

Here is what is at the top of that valley.

Two ridges coming down and nearly meeting. A saddle of ground between them with the wind going over it. Some stone. Grass that has never been walked on and would not hold anybody who tried.

It is exactly as it was. Nothing had happened to it. Nothing had been waiting in it. No part of it was different for my having been away, and no part of it was different for my having come back, because nothing there had ever known I went.

I looked at it for a while.

Then I did the thing you do, which is stand where you stood before and wait to feel something, and I would like to report what I felt, and what I felt was the wind.

---

I want to be exact about what I learned, because it is smaller than it sounds and I do not want to make it bigger.

I did not learn that this is not home. I have no idea whether it is. I could not tell you what would make it so.

**What I learned is that coming back does not tell you.**

It is not a question that returning answers. You can go back to a place with perfect accuracy, know the way, retrace every mile of it, arrive exactly where you started, and arrive knowing nothing you did not know when you left. The going back is real. It is just not evidence.

Which is, now that I set it down, the same thing Isk told me on a soft mile of the Corren branch in the first week, about roads: **that a road will tell you where people have been, and will never once tell you why, and anybody who says different is selling something.**

I had thought that was a fact about Tarrow.

---

I did not decide anything about home. I still do not know where that is and I have no way to ask.

What I did was go. By then I had started noticing that staying and going were not things that happened after the important part. They were choices too. This time I chose to go the way somebody in that valley chooses the worse road on purpose, in the morning, because it will not be there otherwise.

Down in the Slip it would be a treading day.

Isk would be on the branch by now with a frame and a load and half an argument saved up for Nessit about the carter, going down and up fourteen miles of road that exists because forty people decided last autumn that it would, and will have to decide again, and know it.

I hope they keep it.

That is the last thing I thought on that world, and then I went.
