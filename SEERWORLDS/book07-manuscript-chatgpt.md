SEER WORLDS: THE KEEPER'S PRICE

# Part I: The Market

## Chapter 1: The Harbor Below Me

I entered Arrit sideways, watching the crossing instead of the world.

That is harder than it sounds. A crossing has no hall, no tunnel, and no middle where you can stop to inspect the work. One place ends. Another begins. Usually I rush through because the next world is busy becoming solid around me, but the world behind Arrit had left me with a question. A paved road there had ended at a cut too clean for broken stone. Somebody had meant the road to continue. Something had removed the rest with an edge that seemed to have no width at all.

I wanted to see whether the same edge surrounded the passage I used.

So I slowed myself and turned until I could look along it. For a fraction of a moment, I saw a dark line that might have been nothing more than the last world closing behind me. Then sunlight struck me from below, the line vanished, and a man shouted.

“Move, little thing.”

A wooden crate swung past close enough to fill my sight. I went up. The crate went down, landed on a dock, and joined six others that smelled strongly of fish. I do not have a nose in the usual way, but I knew the smell. Some facts insist.

Look down with me. The harbor curved around blue water. Stone piers reached into it like blunt fingers. Boats pressed against both sides of every pier while crews passed rope, jars, baskets, and complaints between deck and shore. Behind them, the town climbed a hill in pale levels. Flat roofs held washing. Narrow stairs turned between houses. From where I floated, every lane seemed to end in somebody else's wall.

After a world where there had been nothing solid beneath anyone, I was pleased to find gravity working on other people again. I have no weight worth discussing. I still like knowing the floor will be there if somebody needs one.

The dockworkers looked at me, decided I was not cargo, and returned to matters they could charge for. I followed a cart uphill. Its left wheel clicked once every turn, which made it easy to keep pace with. At the first terrace, the cart turned toward the warehouses and I continued past a shop with rope hanging in the doorway.

Two men were arguing on the step. The older man stayed seated. The younger one had both hands open, which I have learned means either *I have nothing* or *surely you can see I am right.* He appeared to mean both.

“Seven,” the younger man said.

“Eight,” said the older one.

“It was one afternoon.”

“It was my rope for three months.”

The younger man looked toward the water as if another answer might be arriving by boat. Then he sat beside the older man. Their voices dropped. I moved closer because I am curious and because nobody had told me not to.

The younger man began describing a fishing trip. That was what I thought he was doing. He spoke of a point beyond the harbor, a calm sea, and his father laughing when a line tangled around a boot. The account lasted less than a minute. When it ended, both men shut their eyes.

Something passed between them.

I cannot tell you what it looked like. Light did not flash. The air did not move. Nothing visible left one head and entered the other. Still, the younger man's face went empty in one exact place, while the older man suddenly smiled at a joke he had not heard aloud.

“Settled,” the older man said.

The younger man stood. He seemed relieved until he glanced toward the point outside the harbor. Then his mouth changed.

“What did you pay him?” I asked.

He looked around for the speaker, found me level with his shoulder, and took one step back.

“An afternoon fishing.”

“You told him about it.”

“No. I paid him with it.”

I waited for the joke. Neither man supplied one.

“Where is the afternoon now?”

The younger man touched the side of his head, frowned at his own fingers, and lowered his hand. “With him.”

“And with you.”

“Not anymore.”

He went downhill without saying goodbye. The older man returned to his rope shop carrying a sunny afternoon that had belonged to somebody else.

I stayed above the step. A cart bumped past behind me. The harbor kept shouting, hauling, and counting as if the rules of a life had not changed beside a coil of rope.

I had come to Arrit looking for the edge of a crossing.

Within ten minutes, the world gave me a different edge to study.

## Chapter 2: One Copy

I spent the morning trying to prove the younger man wrong.

This was not personal. If a person told me he had paid a debt by moving an afternoon out of his own head, I thought checking was sensible. I began with the rope seller because he was still close.

“What color was the boat?” I asked.

He leaned around a hanging net. “Green along the rail.”

“Where did the line catch?”

“Round his father's left boot. The knot pulled hard enough to tip him onto the bench.” The old man smiled before I had told him there was anything funny in it. “Then the hook caught the boy's sleeve.”

I went downhill and found the younger man unloading jars from a narrow boat. He remembered that the debt was paid. He remembered going fishing with his father many times. He remembered the name of the point and could tell me the route there. But when I asked what color the boat had been that afternoon, his eyes moved toward the water and stopped.

“I don't know.”

“Green.”

“If he says so.”

“The line went around your father's left boot.”

He gave me an irritated look. “You bought it from him, then?”

“No. I am testing it.”

“Test somebody else's.” He lifted another jar. “I've got forty of these and they don't become lighter while we talk.”

That seemed fair. I left him to the jars.

The next test involved a woman beating dust from a rug. She had given away a memory of her sister's wedding because, she said, she had two good ones from the same day and needed money for a roof tile. I asked about the one she kept. She described the meal, three children asleep under a table, and somebody spilling red sauce down the front of a pale coat.

“And the other memory?”

“The walk to the ceremony.”

“What happened on it?”

She hit the rug again. Dust went through me, which did neither of us any harm. “I imagine we walked.”

The buyer lived two streets over. He described wet paving, the bride lifting her clothes above a gutter, and a dog following them until the church door. The seller knew these facts after I repeated them. She did not recognize them.

By midday, I had tried six people and made four of them impatient. The rule held every time. A person who gave a memory away kept the surrounding facts. They could know where they had been, what year it happened, and who was present. The lived piece was gone: the light, the feeling, the exact order, the part that made the remembered event seem to be happening behind the eyes.

The receiver did not gain a story. The receiver gained the moment.

That distinction bothered me, so I followed another transfer from beginning to end. A physician took three days of fever from a boatbuilder who had recovered months earlier. They agreed on the memory, named the payment, and sat facing each other. Afterward the physician shivered despite the heat. The boatbuilder touched his own forehead and laughed once.

“Better?” the physician asked.

“Quieter.”

The fever itself did not move. The boatbuilder remained well, and the physician did not become ill. What moved was the boatbuilder's three days inside it. He still knew he had been sick. He no longer had to visit those hours whenever a hot room brought them back.

“Why does she want them?” I asked him once the physician had gone.

“So she knows what patients mean when they say the room won't stay still.”

“You could have told her.”

“I did. This is different.”

It was. Telling makes another account while the first remains. Arrit did not copy. It carried one thing from one person into another, leaving knowledge on one side and experience on the other.

There was only ever one copy.

Once I understood that, the entire town looked less like a market and more like a place where people walked around with doors in them. Any door might open. Something might leave. Something else might enter. No mark showed who had given away a bad winter or received a dead stranger's wedding walk.

Nobody seemed troubled by this except me.

That was when I began going uphill, asking who would explain it properly.

## Chapter 3: Yarrow's Step

At the highest terrace, the streets narrowed until two people carrying baskets had to negotiate who owned the middle. I passed the same doorway three times while trying to find a route that continued upward. On the fourth pass, the woman sitting beside it spoke.

“Are you lost, or do you inspect houses by repetition?”

She was trimming the ends from green pods and dropping the useful parts into a clay bowl. Her hair was white. The dark bands around her wrists told me she was old, but the knife moved quickly and the bowl was large.

“I am looking for somebody who understands memory transfer.”

“Everybody understands it.”

“Everybody says that, then gives me a different answer.”

“That sounds like understanding.” She cut another pod. “You may hover there if you stop crossing my light.”

Her name was Yarrow. I learned this after she made me describe where I came from, which I could not do in a way she found useful. When I said I came through the crossing below the harbor, she said that explained where I had arrived, not where I had begun. I told her I did not know where I began.

“Then we have each asked a question the other can't answer,” she said. “That seems fair.”

I liked her at once, although liking her did not make the pods more interesting.

Yarrow confirmed the rules I had tested. A memory could be moved with agreed words and attention. A person could not steal one by touching somebody in a crowd. A receiver could pass it on later, but would lose it in turn. Telling parts aloud did not divide it. Writing about it did not make a second memory either. Those acts made records, which were useful and different.

I asked whether she had ever transferred one.

The knife paused at the end of a pod. “One that mattered.”

“To buy something?”

“To keep it.”

That answer did not fit anywhere. “You cannot keep a thing by giving it away.”

“I didn't give it away. I placed it with Ess.”

“Who is Ess?”

“A keeper.”

The word had appeared twice that morning, but nobody had explained it. Yarrow said a keeper received memories for safekeeping. Some were held until a named day. Some were passed to a named person after a death. Others remained until the owner asked for them. The work needed no locked room because a lock could not protect what was stored inside a person. It needed a reliable holder.

“What did you place with him?”

Yarrow set the knife across the bowl. She did not look offended. She looked as if she were deciding whether answering would create more work.

“The afternoon my sister and I ended a long quarrel.”

“How long?”

“Nine years.”

“The quarrel lasted nine years?”

“The ending took an afternoon. Most repairs are shorter than the damage.” She picked up another pod. “That does not make them cheap.”

I asked why anybody would remove the repair from themselves. This time she finished three pods before answering.

“My memories have started going. I lost a spring two years ago. Then a man's face. I worked beside him for six years, and now I know him only from what people tell me.” Her voice did not soften. “The afternoon with Del mattered too much to wait and see.”

Below us, somebody called up the price of onions. Yarrow shouted that the caller was cheating and received a detailed answer about last week's rain.

“So the afternoon is safe,” I said when the discussion ended.

“With Ess.”

“And you do not remember it.”

“That was the cost.”

She said this while moving trimmed pods aside to make room for more. No tremor. No grand sadness. She knew what she had bought and what she had spent.

I knew those facts too.

I simply did not believe they could add up to the choice she claimed to have made.

## Chapter 4: The Courtyard With Nothing In It

Yarrow gave me directions to Ess because refusing would only have delayed me. She made that clear.

“Second stair below the potter. Turn where the paving changes. Courtyard with the dead tree.”

“Why is the tree dead?”

“Because it died.”

“Did a memory—”

“Go and bother Ess.”

I went.

The paving changed from gray squares to long brown stones. The second passage opened into a courtyard with one bench, one table, and one leafless tree growing from a cracked pot. Ess sat at the table holding a cup. He was neither young nor old by Arrit's markings. His clothes were plain. His face was the sort you could pass six times in a market and remember only that somebody had been there.

“Yarrow placed a memory with you,” I said.

Ess looked at me, then at the open passage behind me. “She tells people what she wants told.”

“She told me.”

“Then yes.”

“I want you to return it.”

He drank from the cup.

I waited. He continued sitting.

“Did you hear me?”

“Yes.”

“Will you return it?”

“No.”

There are refusals with doors left open inside them. People say no while explaining which price, apology, hour, or changed condition might turn it into yes. Ess's refusal had no such space. It was a wall built from one small word.

“Why?”

“Because you asked.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It is the reason.”

I tried another direction. I explained that Yarrow had begun losing memories. Ess knew. I explained that this afternoon had repaired nine years of separation from her sister. He knew that too. I said it was plainly important and that living without it must hurt.

“I know,” he said.

His agreement made him seem worse. If he had misunderstood the cost, I could correct him. If he had not noticed Yarrow's sadness, I could provide evidence. Instead he had the same facts I did and no visible wish to act on them.

“Does she visit?”

“That is hers.”

“Has she asked for it?”

“That belongs to her as well.”

“How long are you meant to keep it?”

“That is hers too.”

He did not sound angry. Anger would have given me something to push against. He sounded like a person naming which jars belonged on which shelf.

I went around the table once and studied the room beyond the open door. There was a bed, a second chair, a narrow shelf, and no cabinet large enough to contain another person's afternoon. I had not expected a box exactly, but I had expected some sign that keeping was work.

“It is only in you?”

“Only in me.”

“Then all you are doing is not forgetting.”

“That is the work.”

I looked at the dead tree. Ess looked at it too, perhaps because I did. Nothing about the courtyard explained why anyone would trust him with the part of a life they feared losing.

“Yarrow should have it.”

“Yarrow arranged what should happen to it.”

That was a better answer than his first one, but I was not ready to hear it. I heard only a keeper using an arrangement to defend his possession of something precious.

I left the courtyard certain of three things: Yarrow was missing the afternoon that made peace with her sister, Ess had it, and memories on Arrit could move.

Three correct facts can build a very convincing wrong conclusion.

Mine was that I would move the afternoon back.

## Chapter 5: Tam Finds the Other End

The memory market had no market building. It occupied whichever street Tam happened to be walking through.

I met her below the cloth sellers after asking three people who arranged difficult transfers. The first two pointed in different directions. The third pointed at a woman already passing us and said, “Hurry.”

Tam was small, quick, and carrying two folded papers in one hand while eating from the other. She did not slow when I introduced myself.

“You want to buy?”

“I want to understand prices.”

“Worse.” She turned between two baskets without touching either. I went over them. “Keep up.”

For the next four hours, I followed while Tam joined wants to things. That was her trade. A sailor had walked through a northern town once and barely noticed it. A baker who had grown up there wanted to see the lane behind her childhood house again. Tam had remembered both facts, found the sailor before his ship left, and brought them together beside a stall selling hot flatbread.

The sailor wanted coin. The baker offered two mornings from her apprenticeship, which the sailor did not need. Tam interrupted before they could spend an hour proving this to each other. She found a potter who wanted one of those mornings because his daughter was about to begin work with a baker. The potter paid the sailor. The baker gave the potter the morning. The sailor passed his walk through the northern town to the baker.

“Three people for one street?” I asked as we left.

“Four,” Tam said. “I take a fee.”

“What did you take?”

“None of yours.”

She did not mean this rudely. Tam treated information like merchandise even when the information concerned her breakfast.

At midday she matched a rope apprentice with an old maker who could remember the first time his hands completed a hard splice correctly. The apprentice did not receive the skill. After the transfer she tried the work, pulled the wrong strand, and had to begin again. What she gained was a felt standard. She now knew the pressure and final fit she was trying to reach.

“That saved her a year,” Tam said.

The old maker, who had heard this, shook his head. “Saved her a month if she listens.”

“A month is part of a year.” Tam was already moving.

Later, a boy gave away the memory of losing a fight. His mother made him stand beside her and state twice that he understood losing the memory would not remove the bruise, repair the friendship, or prevent the next fight. He agreed with great confidence. Once the transfer was done, he looked pleased until his mother told him he still had to apologize.

“What did that memory cost?” I asked Tam.

“Less than the next one will.”

“There will be another?”

“You saw his face.”

I had. Tam's work was less like finding objects and more like knowing where every loose wish in the town had rolled. She remembered who feared rain, who missed a dead parent, who wanted to understand an illness, who needed coin, and who had an afternoon they could bear to lose. Then she found a line through them.

Near sunset, she took me to a woman who wanted to give her grown son a memory of his first step. The son did not want it. He already had the story from being told, and he said the moment belonged to his mother.

“He can refuse?” I asked after we left.

Tam stopped so suddenly that I passed her and had to return.

“Everybody can refuse.”

“Even a gift?”

“Especially a gift. A price tells you somebody knows there are two sides. Gifts make people forget.”

That went into the plan I was building, though not in the place it should have gone. I heard that Yarrow could ask for her memory and Ess would have to deal with her. I did not hear that she had already refused the gift I meant to arrange.

At the bottom of the hill, Tam finally sat on a low wall. She had joined five transfers, delayed two, prevented one mistake, and eaten nothing since the food she was holding when I met her.

“Now,” she said, “which price are you trying to learn?”

“The price of an afternoon that ended nine years of anger.”

Tam looked toward the upper terraces. I had not named Yarrow. Apparently I did not need to.

“That one isn't in the market,” she said.

“Everything I saw today was.”

“Then learn the difference before you make trouble.”

She got up and left me with the longest useful answer she had given all day.

## Chapter 6: Three Broken Measures

I accepted Tam's warning as a reason to become better informed.

This is one of the ways I get into trouble. A person tells me to leave a matter alone. I agree that I do not yet understand it. Then I treat understanding as permission.

For two days I followed the trade and measured every transfer Tam allowed me to watch. I began with duration because duration behaves well. A year contains more time than an hour. It seemed reasonable that a long memory should cost more than a short one.

The measure lasted until breakfast.

A woman paid more than I had yet seen for five seconds of her dead husband turning in a doorway and asking whether she wanted tea. The seller had been delivering flour and remembered the moment because the husband dropped a spoon. To him it was five unimportant seconds. To her it was the last ordinary question her husband had asked.

Later that morning, a fisherman offered an entire calm week at sea and found no buyer at any price. Seven days lost to five seconds. Duration was useless.

My second measure was pleasure. I counted laughter, comfort, pain, fear, and regret where people named them. Pleasant memories should be wanted. Painful ones should require payment to remove.

That measure survived less time.

The physician who had taken the fever paid well for it. A mother wanted the memory of an argument she had missed because her son had died before explaining what the fight was about. A builder bought three minutes inside a roof collapse so he could understand why one brace had failed. Nobody was seeking comfort. They were seeking knowledge that could not be reached by being told.

Meanwhile, a happy afternoon on a beach went unsold. Tam said the light was pretty, the weather was mild, and nothing else occurred. The owner wanted enough coin to repair a door. Nobody wanted to pay for pleasant weather belonging to a stranger.

“Pleasure is not value,” I said.

“Pleasure is pleasure,” Tam answered. “Useful thing. Different column.”

I did not have columns, but I understood the correction.

My third measure was rarity. A memory of an event nobody else had seen should be worth more than an ordinary walk. Tam let me believe this for most of the second day. Then she arranged four sales of walks along the same beach.

The walks were so alike that I thought one seller had returned under different clothes. Same line of water. Same dark rocks. Similar wind. One ended near a broken stair and another beside a net shed, but no rare event occurred in any of them.

The prices were all different.

The least expensive went to a mapmaker checking the position of the rocks. Another went to a woman who liked storms but could no longer make the climb. The highest price came from a man who had grown up above that beach and left as a child. He bought the dullest walk. It passed his old gate.

“There has to be one property inside the memory that sets the price,” I told Tam.

She was sorting her folded papers on the low wall. “Why?”

“Otherwise the prices are arbitrary.”

“No. They are personal.”

“That is another word for unstable.”

“So are people.” She tucked one paper into her sleeve. “We still charge them.”

I tried to build a fourth measure from need, but need belonged to the buyer. Loss belonged to the seller. Time, pleasure, pain, rarity, usefulness, and money crossed between them in different amounts. No fact inside the memory ruled the exchange.

Two people decided what one thing meant between them. The price was the point where both agreed to move it.

I disliked this answer because it could not be used without asking the people involved.

Yarrow had already answered.

Ess had already answered.

I should have stopped there. Instead I decided their answers were only the opening prices.

# Part II: What Keepers Hold

## Chapter 7: The Boat Race

The next bargain lasted so long that somebody brought chairs.

It began outside a cloth shop with a woman named Ren offering the memory of a boat race. She had been on the winning crew. A man called Hal wanted the afternoon because he had watched from shore and had spent fifteen years insisting his brother's boat should have won.

“Six,” Hal said.

“Ten,” Ren answered.

“I already remember the race.”

“You remember losing it from dry land.”

That was the opening. By the time I arrived with Tam, they had covered weather, boat condition, the strength of each crew, the honesty of three judges, and the fact that Hal had once charged Ren too much for a lamp. None of those matters changed the memory. All of them changed the people pricing it.

Tam leaned against the wall. “Watch.”

“Are you matching them?”

“They are matched. I am waiting to be paid.”

The cloth seller put out a stool for Ren. Hal refused one, perhaps because sitting would have suggested weakness. Other people slowed, listened, and stayed. A child began keeping a count of offers with pebbles. At seven, Ren asked ten. At eight, she asked ten. When Hal walked away, she called after him that his brother had missed the turn because he watched the wrong marker.

Hal returned.

“You cannot sell it to anybody else for ten.”

“Then I will keep winning.”

“You have had fifteen years of winning.”

“And it has worn very well.”

The crowd approved. Hal did not, but I saw him hide a smile before he answered. That was the first thing I had failed to include in any measure: they were enjoying the bargain.

Ren did not merely own a race. She owned the chance to make Hal admit he wanted her side of it. Hal did not merely want the winner's view. He wanted to argue with the winner until the old dispute became part of the price. If Tam had named a fair number at the start, both would have rejected it.

They agreed on nine near sunset.

The crowd went quiet for the transfer. Ren sat straight with both feet planted. Hal finally took the spare chair. They named the race, the boat, and the agreed exchange. For a moment neither moved. Then Ren's shoulders lowered as though she had finished carrying something. Hal gripped the chair and laughed.

“The marker was clear,” he said.

“I told you.”

“My brother looked the wrong way.”

“I told you that too.”

“You could have told me where the wind shifted.”

Ren stood. “I just sold you where the wind shifted.”

They went into the cloth shop together to settle Tam's fee. I remained in the street beside the child's line of nine pebbles.

The price had not been hiding inside the race. It had formed across the table, made from rivalry, history, pleasure, loss, and the fact that Ren could walk away still owning a victory Hal wanted.

I understood that completely now.

Then I made one more wrong turn. I decided Ess's refusal meant I had not yet found the memory he wanted enough to bargain for.

There is a difference between understanding how a tool works and understanding when you have no right to use it. At the time, I knew only the first half.

## Chapter 8: Nothing to Offer

Tam discovered my financial condition while trying to charge me.

She had spent three days answering questions, letting me follow her work, and preventing me from interrupting transfers at the wrong moment. She said this had value. I agreed. Then she asked what I could give her.

“Information,” I said.

“About what?”

I told her about a world without ground, where people lived by catching one another in the air. I told her about a river that ran in opposite directions at once. I described the road whose end had brought me into Arrit looking at crossings.

Tam listened with professional attention. “Those are stories.”

“They happened.”

“I believe you. Can you transfer them?”

I had not tried. I chose the shortest clear memory I had: entering Arrit beside the swinging fish crate. Tam put down her papers. We named the moment and agreed that I would give it to her. I did what I had watched everyone else do. I found the arrival inside myself and held it toward her.

Nothing happened.

We tried again with the road. Then with the weightless world. Tam called over a transfer clerk she knew, who listened to our words and corrected the order. On the third attempt, Tam shut her eyes. I concentrated until the whole memory seemed brighter inside me.

It stayed mine.

“You haven't got the right kind,” the clerk said.

“The right kind of what?”

“Past.”

That was not helpful, but it was as far as his knowledge went. People on Arrit had always been able to transfer memories. They did not know which part of themselves made it possible because nobody had needed to compare themselves with a floating visitor from elsewhere.

Tam tested the result another way. She offered me a memory of walking from the lower pier to the bell tower. I agreed to receive it. The clerk spoke the words. Tam reached for the walk inside herself.

Again, nothing crossed.

She still remembered the route. I still did not.

Tam collected her papers. “No use at all.”

“I can describe six worlds.”

“Anybody can describe things. Badly, usually, but they manage.”

I disliked how much the result mattered to me. Until then I had assumed I was merely choosing not to trade. Discovering that I could not made every exchange feel like a room whose door had been quietly closed. Everyone in Arrit carried wealth, medicine, proof, comfort, and burdens that could move. I carried experiences no one could enter and could receive none of theirs.

Tam noticed my silence and misread it on purpose, which was kind of her.

“You can owe me,” she said. “I collect from impossible people all the time.”

“How?”

“Eventually they become possible.”

She told me to meet her at the lower market the next morning if I still wanted to learn. Then she left at her usual speed.

I remained beside the wall, thinking about Ess. If I had possessed a memory he wanted, perhaps I could have offered it. I had none he could take. Tam had now proved that more thoroughly than necessary.

The market had given me a clear price for my entire past.

It was nothing, because it could not move.

## Chapter 9: Paid for Doing Nothing

I returned to Ess with a number anyway.

The number was not coin. It represented the amount of value I thought Tam could assemble if I found enough people willing to contribute small memories, labor, or payment. I had a list of possible chains in my head. None began with me, which should have warned me that the plan was generous with other people's property.

Ess was in his courtyard watering the dead tree.

I watched water darken the soil around the trunk. “That tree is dead.”

“It is.”

“Why water it?”

“The pot has mint in it.”

I moved lower and found three green leaves near the rim. The world had corrected me before Ess needed to.

“What is Yarrow's memory worth?”

“To you?” he asked.

“To me.”

“Nothing you can pay.”

I told him that Tam could match buyers and sellers until the right value reached him. He put down the water jar and waited. That waiting was becoming irritating because it made me supply the weakness in my own argument.

“What do people normally pay a keeper?” I asked.

“It depends on the terms.”

This answer sent us through duration, risk, later transfer, named recipients, and proof of identity. A memory meant for a child after a parent's death required different work from an afternoon held for a season. Some clients paid coin. Some gave labor. Some arranged care for the keeper when age made work difficult. The payment covered a promise that could last longer than either person first expected.

“But once the transfer is done, what action do you perform?”

“I keep the terms.”

“By sitting here.”

“Sometimes.”

“By not telling anyone.”

“Always.”

“By not forgetting.”

“As well as I can.”

That last answer stopped me. “Keepers can forget?”

“Keepers are people.”

He said it without defense. A keeper could become ill, be injured, grow old, or die unexpectedly. That was why reputation mattered and why some memories were transferred again before a keeper's end. The trade did not promise perfect safety. It promised that one person would organize a life around protecting another person's single copy.

I examined the courtyard again. The empty bench could receive a client. The plain room revealed nothing. Ess's ordinary days were part of the protection because curiosity had nowhere to grip.

“How does anyone know you still have what they gave you?”

“They ask.”

“And you say yes.”

“If the terms allow confirmation.”

“That sounds very close to doing nothing.”

“A great deal of trust does.”

I did not like the answer, mainly because I could not find the false part. Ess was not selling Yarrow's afternoon. He had been paid to continue an absence: no disclosure, no casual use, no return to the wrong person, no change merely because somebody with no place in the arrangement objected.

That somebody was me.

I still believed Yarrow should have the memory. Yet for the first time, my plan had to cross something larger than Ess's stubbornness. It had to cross the reason anyone used a keeper at all.

## Chapter 10: The People Who Turn Back

Two clients arrived before I left, and Ess did not ask me to go. I took this as permission to stay in the courtyard. It may only have been evidence that he knew asking would not help.

The first visitor was an old woman carrying a basket with no lid. She placed it on the bench and spent a long time rearranging three loaves before speaking.

“Do you still have the bridge?”

Ess said he did.

“The whole crossing?”

“Rain, blue coat, broken rail.”

The woman let out air. Ess had named only enough to prove he knew which memory she meant. I wanted to ask whether the bridge still existed, who wore the coat, and why the rail mattered. The questions arrived ready. For once I noticed they belonged to me and not to the woman.

She gave Ess one loaf, took the basket, and left. The visit had lasted less than two minutes. Her walk out of the courtyard was easier than her walk in.

“Was that payment?” I asked after she had gone.

“Bread.”

“For confirmation?”

“For lunch.”

The second visitor came near midday. He stood in the passage without entering until Ess moved the spare chair into shade. Even then, he remained standing.

He wanted to place a memory. I knew this because he said so five times. Each time Ess answered, “All right.” The man described everything around the memory while carefully avoiding the event itself. It happened inland. It involved his brother. It had lasted one night, except for the part in the morning that might belong with it. He wanted it preserved. He also wanted never to encounter it unexpectedly.

Ess asked what result the man needed. The man walked from the passage to the chair, from the chair to the dead tree, and back. He said that was exactly what he could not decide.

I expected Ess to help. A matcher would have offered shapes for the agreement, people at the other end, lengths of time, or prices. Ess waited.

After nearly an hour, the man said he would return another day.

“All right,” Ess said again.

The man looked ashamed. “I have taken your morning.”

“You used your own.”

That answer changed the man's face, though I could not say how. He thanked Ess and left without transferring anything.

“Why did you let him talk that long?” I asked.

“He needed to find out he wasn't ready.”

“You knew earlier.”

“It was not mine to know for him.”

I went toward the passage and then stopped. The old woman's quick proof and the man's slow retreat appeared opposite, but Ess had done the same work for both. He had supplied only the part each had asked him to hold. He did not pull the bridge into public because I was curious. He did not turn the brother's night into an agreement because the visitor had reached his chair.

“How many people leave without doing it?”

“About half.”

“Does that not waste your time?”

“The other half need a place where turning back is allowed.”

I left with that sentence following me down the passage. A keeper's work could end with no transfer, no payment, and no new memory held. The empty result still protected something.

My plan had no place for an empty result. I intended to make sure something moved.

## Chapter 11: The Shape of Silence

I learned keeper manners by breaking them in order.

First I asked how many memories Ess carried. He looked past me toward the passage and said the fishing boats would return early because the wind had changed.

I thought he had not heard. I asked again.

“You already had an answer,” he said.

“You mentioned boats.”

“I mentioned them.”

The answer was the change of subject. Everyone raised on Arrit recognized it. I had been on Arrit less than two weeks and required labels.

Next I asked what kinds of memories keepers received most often. Ess pointed out that the mint needed cutting. I did understand this time, but I argued that a general category would expose nobody. He cut the mint. The smell rose sharply from the pot, and he did not offer a category.

Then I asked whether any of his clients had died. He brought the cut leaves inside.

By the fourth refusal, I could identify the silence before he changed direction. It was small. He did not glare or become stiff. He simply stopped being available to the question. The conversation could continue elsewhere if I let it.

“Why not say no?” I asked.

“Because sometimes people hear no as the start of bargaining.”

I had excellent evidence for that.

Ess explained one general rule because it belonged to the work rather than a client. A keeper did not confirm that a person used a keeper unless the person had made that public. A keeper did not list subjects, dates, amounts, or agreements. A visitor's presence in the courtyard proved nothing because half of the people who came might leave with no transfer. Even identifying an owner after death depended on the terms.

“What if telling would help someone?”

“Help them do what?”

“Understand.”

“That is not always help.”

I thought of the woman and the bridge, and how easily my questions could have turned three proof words into a public life. I also thought of Yarrow, who had told me what she placed with Ess and still had not asked me to discover its terms.

“But I already know about hers.”

“From her.”

“So you can discuss it.”

“Still no.”

“What prevents it?”

“It is not yours.”

The sentence was plain enough to leave no room for my usual repair work. The memory did not become partly mine because I cared about what its absence did to Yarrow. Concern did not add my name to the arrangement. Curiosity did not create a share.

I could repeat those truths now. I could even explain them to somebody else. What I could not yet do was let them stop me.

“Will you at least tell me whether she can take it back?”

Ess went to the doorway and checked the sky. “The boats will be in within the hour.”

This time I knew I had been answered.

I also knew I would ask Tam instead.

## Chapter 12: A Feeling With No Owner

Tam answered the general question. An owner could ask for a held memory back unless the agreement had named another result. She would not guess Yarrow's terms, and she called my attempt to make her guess “cheap work.”

I returned uphill with nothing useful and found Yarrow carrying a water jar from the public pump. She held it against one hip and climbed slowly, resting at every second landing. I could not carry any part of it. The best I could do was go ahead and tell people coming down that the narrow stair was occupied. Yarrow said this made me a sign.

At her door, she put the jar down and stood with one palm against the frame. Her eyes closed for three breaths. When she opened them, she looked tired and angry about being seen tired.

“Does it hurt?” I asked.

“The jar does.”

“I meant not having the afternoon.”

“I know what you meant.”

She went inside. I hovered at the doorway until she told me I was blocking the air.

That small pause against the frame became evidence I carried to Ess. I described the climb, the closed eyes, and the way Yarrow's hand stayed on the wood after the jar was down. I said the missing afternoon was not a clean absence. It sat inside ordinary days and made them harder.

Ess listened without interrupting. This encouraged me.

“She is unhappy without it,” I said.

“I know.”

The words landed harder than a denial. I had expected loyalty to require ignorance. If Ess knew the arrangement hurt Yarrow, then his continued refusal seemed like a choice to preserve pain.

“And you do nothing.”

“I keep what I agreed.”

“Even while she is suffering.”

“Even then.”

I became angry. I told him agreements were made by people, and people could be wrong. I said a promise that kept hurting its owner ought to be reconsidered. I said if Yarrow came tomorrow and asked, he should return it before she could lose another day beside her own door.

He answered only the last part. “If Yarrow comes and asks, Yarrow and I will speak.”

“Will you give it back?”

Ess reached for his cup and stopped halfway.

From the next courtyard came the sound of oil striking a hot pan. A sharp green smell rose over the wall. Ess's hand remained above the table. His face changed, though not toward pleasure or grief. He seemed to be listening inward while the cooking continued outside.

“Not mine,” he said quietly.

Then he picked up the cup as if nothing had happened.

“What was that?”

He looked toward the wall. “Dinner, probably.”

“You know that is not what I asked.”

The silence arrived. I recognized it and hated it.

I left before the food was ready. On the way down, I kept rebuilding the exchange so Ess became cold enough to fit my conclusion. He knew Yarrow hurt. He protected the arrangement instead of fixing her pain. When a smell crossed the wall, something private in him moved, and he would not explain it.

All those facts were true.

The truth they supported was not.

# Part III: The Missing Afternoon

## Chapter 13: Nine Years on the Step

The next morning Yarrow knew I had argued with Ess.

“How?” I asked.

She tipped trimmed pods from one bowl into another. “You were in his courtyard half the afternoon. You left fast. Then you came up here slowly, which you do when you are arranging an argument after it is over.”

I had not known I did that.

The upper terrace knew most movements before the people making them understood where they led. Eleven windows looked onto the stairs. News traveled upward with baskets and downward with water jars. Privacy existed there, but it had to be built from what people chose not to ask, not from what they failed to see.

“Ess will not tell me what the memory is like,” I said.

“Good.”

“You told me what it is.”

“That was my telling.”

I knew the difference by then. Hearing it again did not make me enjoy it.

Yarrow took pity on me, or became tired of watching me circle the same fact. She told me how the quarrel began. Their mother had needed care near the end of her life. Yarrow believed Del had left too much work to her. Del believed Yarrow refused help and then counted the refusal as proof. A house passed between them after the death, and every old complaint found a room inside it.

“Who was right?” I asked.

“Both of us in pieces. Neither of us in the arrangement we made from them.”

The sisters stopped speaking. They lived in the same town. They used the same market, heard the same bells, and sometimes crossed the same square by choosing opposite sides. Their separation required regular work. Yarrow returned messages unopened. Del sent questions through cousins. Each learned which family meals the other would attend.

Nine years is long enough for an injury to become a schedule.

Then Del climbed the terrace one afternoon and stood outside Yarrow's door. Yarrow nearly went inside. Instead they sat on the step where Yarrow now sorted vegetables.

“What did Del say?”

“I don't have it.”

“But you know the facts.”

“Some. We spoke. We were angry. One of us laughed, though Del claims it was her and I think that sounds unlikely.” Yarrow's knife moved through another stem. “By evening we had finished being enemies.”

Not politely finished. Not a greeting arranged for public places. They repaired the relationship and kept it repaired for more than twenty years.

I looked at the step. Its center had been worn lower by feet and weather. No line marked where nine years ended.

“Was it your happiest memory?”

“It was not.”

This surprised me enough that I moved backward. Yarrow named a day at the water, a birth in the family, and a night when the whole terrace ate outside because the heat made sleep impossible. She had enjoyed all of them more.

“Then why preserve the argument?”

“I preserved the repair.”

“Why that one?”

Yarrow set down the knife. “Because happiness can be lovely and still do nothing. That afternoon changed what came after it.”

The answer gave the memory more weight in my mind. I imagined it holding up twenty years between the sisters while Yarrow sat above it with only the factual outline.

I thought learning its shape would help me respect her decision.

Instead it made me more certain I had to undo it.

## Chapter 14: The Inventory

Yarrow had not chosen in panic.

I wanted panic because panic leaves room for correction. A frightened person acts quickly, then may welcome somebody who restores the earlier condition. Yarrow had done the opposite. She had watched her losses for two years before speaking to Ess.

The first missing piece was a spring. She knew where she had lived and which work she had done, but three months had no weather inside them. She found tools she remembered buying and a coat she knew she had mended. The actions existed as facts with no days attached.

After that came the face of a man named Corren. Yarrow could list the workshop, the tasks they shared, and the year he left town. She knew he laughed loudly and cheated at cards because other people had told her often enough. When he returned for a visit, she recognized his name before she recognized him.

“That frightened you,” I said.

“It did.”

Yarrow owned the feeling, plain and clean. She had not waited for a gentler word. Once she saw the pattern, she made lists. The lists recorded people, places, work, debts, promises, and the events she would need factual knowledge of even if their lived pieces vanished. Records could tell her what had happened. They could not give the moments back.

Then she took stock of what mattered most to preserve as experience.

“How did you do it?”

“I sat here and went through my life.”

“All of it?”

“All I could reach.”

She considered childhood, work, deaths, births, journeys, and ordinary days whose importance had become clear only after many years. Some memories mattered because she loved them. Some mattered because another person might need them. The afternoon with Del was different. It had changed the meaning of everything before it and the direction of everything after.

Yarrow selected it, then waited one season. During that season she asked whether fear had made the choice. She visited Ess twice without transferring anything. She spoke with Del. She checked Ess's reputation with people who had used him and people who had walked away.

“Del agreed?”

“Del asked me not to.”

This was the opening I had wanted. “Then she thinks it was wrong.”

“She wanted me to keep it. That is not the same.”

“Did she understand the risk?”

“As well as anyone can.” Yarrow folded the cloth beneath the empty bowl. “She also understood it was mine.”

When the season ended, Yarrow returned to Ess. Together they named the memory closely enough to protect the right afternoon. They agreed on custody and terms. Then she transferred it while still able to recognize precisely what she was losing.

“And the moment after?” I asked.

Yarrow looked toward the street below. “I knew I had done it. I knew why. I knew Del and I were well. But the road between anger and peace was gone.”

She had walked home carrying the result and missing the change that produced it. Del was waiting at the top terrace. Yarrow knew her sister had earned trust over twenty years, yet the older nine years had suddenly become louder inside her than the afternoon that ended them.

“You would still make the transfer?”

“Tomorrow,” she said. “If it had not already been made.”

I had searched the decision for haste, ignorance, pressure, a hidden buyer, or a keeper's trick. Every route closed. Yarrow had understood, waited, checked, consulted, and chosen.

Only one possibility remained for me: she had made the wrong careful choice.

That was a harder claim to defend, so naturally I held it more tightly.

## Chapter 15: Asking in Different Shapes

Yarrow sent me to Del with a basket.

This sounds useful until you remember I cannot carry one. Yarrow gave the basket to a neighbor going downhill and instructed me to make sure it reached the blue door beside the cooper. The neighbor said he had lived in town for forty years and could find a blue door. Yarrow said then I should make sure he did not forget why he was going there.

I followed the basket.

Del lived near the lower end of the terraces where the streets widened enough for carts. She was younger than Yarrow and spoke faster. When the neighbor gave her the basket, she checked its contents before thanking him, which told me Yarrow had sent the wrong thing before.

“You are the traveler,” Del said.

“I am Dot.”

“You are the traveler called Dot who keeps visiting Ess.”

There was no useful denial available.

Del asked after Yarrow through a list rather than a general question. Had she eaten? Was the water jar full? Had she mentioned the pain in her left hand? Had the shutter closed after the damp night? I answered what I knew. Del listened closely and corrected nothing.

“You care for her,” I said.

“That is a remarkable discovery about sisters.”

“You were separated for nine years.”

The humor left her face. “She told you.”

“She told me that part.”

“Then that part is yours to know.”

Even Del spoke in privacy boundaries.

I asked whether she wanted Yarrow's memory returned. Del looked into the basket again although nothing inside had changed.

“Want is easy,” she said. “I want her to have every good thing she ever had. That does not make all of them safe to keep.”

“So you agree with the transfer.”

“I did not say that.”

“You disagree.”

“I did not say that either.” She passed the basket to a boy inside the house. “It was hers to place. I asked her not to. She placed it. Both facts survived.”

I went back uphill with no basket and a new version of the same question. Yarrow was repairing a split handle by winding cord around it. I told her Del still wanted her to have the afternoon.

“I already know that.”

“If you know she wants it—”

“No, Dot.”

“I am only saying—”

“No again.” Yarrow pulled the cord tight with both hands. “You ask whether I regret it. Then whether Ess should decide for me. Then whether Del's wish should decide for me. You keep dressing one question in other people's coats.”

I had no answer to that because it was exact.

She finished the winding, tested the handle, and found it still weak. Instead of adding more cord, she cut the whole repair away and began again from the wood.

“What question am I allowed to ask?” I said.

“Any question. You are allowed to hear the answer too.”

“You do not want the memory returned.”

“Correct.”

“Even though being without it hurts.”

“That is also correct.”

The two facts stood together. I kept trying to force one to cancel the other because I had never learned what to do when pain was part of a chosen price.

Yarrow tightened the new winding. This time the handle held.

## Chapter 16: The Want Behind the Refusal

I decided Yarrow was being brave.

That interpretation let me keep everything I wanted. Her repeated refusal became proof of how hard the sacrifice was. Her anger with my questions became strain. Her clear account of the risk became a story she told herself so she could endure the result.

You can see the trick. I could not, because the trick used concern as its disguise.

If Yarrow secretly wanted the memory and Ess openly refused me, then the remaining problem was the keeper. Tam's trade offered a method. Everyone wanted something. A matcher found the missing desire and built a route to it.

I began with Ess's courtyard. The room held a bed, two chairs, a table, a shelf, and little else. The cracked tree pot contained mint. His cup had been repaired along one side. Nothing suggested wealth, collections, travel, or appetite.

I watched his days from places that were public enough to keep me from calling the activity spying. He bought bread early, visited the pump, swept the courtyard, received clients, and sometimes walked to the sea wall in the evening. He spoke to the potter about weather and to a child about a lost hoop. He did not linger at market stalls.

After two days, I had learned that Ess needed a new broom.

This was not enough.

Tam refused to help when I first asked. “I match people who own what they offer.”

“I only need to know what he wants.”

“For what purpose?”

“So I can find who has it.”

“And then?”

I told her. Tam listened while continuing to sort requests into piles on a table. When I finished, she put one paper into the left pile and another into the right.

“Yarrow said no?”

“She believes no is necessary.”

“That was not my question.”

“She said the word.”

Tam pushed all three piles farther from me, though I had no hands with which to disturb them. “Then no.”

I said Ess might accept another memory in exchange. Tam said he could not exchange property he did not own. I said perhaps the right payment would persuade him to speak with Yarrow. Tam said I was trying to buy pressure while calling it help.

That accusation made me angry because it reached the part I had kept unnamed.

I left Tam's table and continued alone. By evening, I had a list: a better broom, a living tree, a less chipped cup, money, an afternoon away from the courtyard, and any memory he might privately desire. The list was weak because it contained ordinary guesses rather than knowledge.

Then I remembered the smell over the wall and Ess saying the feeling was not his.

Keepers carried other lives. Some part of that weight had to trouble him. If I found what would ease it, perhaps he would listen.

I had turned Yarrow's no into bravery and Ess's privacy into a hidden want. Every closed door became evidence of a key.

The plan felt excellent.

## Chapter 17: The Great-Grandfather and the Ditches

I found Tam in a family dispute that occupied an entire street.

Six people stood outside a house arguing about their great-grandfather. The man himself had been dead longer than the youngest two had been alive. What remained was a memory of him walking a wet road and discussing drainage for nearly two hours.

The memory had passed through three relatives. Its current holder, an older woman with her sleeves rolled high, wanted somebody else to take it before age made the decision urgent.

“It is family history,” she said.

Her son answered, “It is ditches.”

“He explains why the lower field floods.”

“The lower field has flooded for eighty years. The explanation has not helped.”

One cousin tried to solve two unwanted things at once. She would carry the wet road, she said, but only if the family relieved her of a winter in a leaking house. The others objected that trading one person's burden into another person did not make the arrangement fair. A second cousin suggested copying the great-grandfather's words into the town records and allowing the lived walk to end. The old woman refused. Ink could preserve the drainage advice. It could not preserve the man speaking it.

The youngest child sat on a step turning a pebble over in both hands. During a quiet moment, she said, “Could he stop?”

Nobody answered at first.

Then the old woman said, “He could.”

If she died while holding the memory, or lost it without transferring it, the walk and the voice and every ditch would vanish. The family wanted none of it. They also could not agree to become the people who allowed the only copy to end.

Tam stood beside the doorway, listening for an offer that might hold. None did. Eventually the family agreed to meet again after supper on another day, which meant they had changed the time and nothing else.

“Will somebody take him?” I asked as the street cleared.

“At the last possible moment,” Tam said. “People dislike a burden and dislike its ending. The second dislike usually arrives late.”

The dispute gave me another way to approach Ess. “Could Yarrow's memory be passed to someone else?”

“If her terms allow and she agrees.”

“Could Ess trade it for another memory?”

Tam turned toward me with the tired look she usually saved for people who changed one word in a rejected offer.

“Not even then.”

“Even if the new holder were safer?”

“Yarrow chose the holder.”

“Even if Ess wanted the exchange?”

“Ess wanting a thing does not make Yarrow's property his.”

I said every keeper must have some price. Tam said I was confusing the fee for accepting a duty with ownership of what the duty protected. The old woman could ask someone to carry her great-grandfather. The great-grandfather could not be sold by whichever relative happened to hold him unless the family's terms allowed it.

“Then there is nothing I can offer Ess.”

“Exactly.”

“How does the memory return?”

“Yarrow asks. Ess follows their agreement.”

The answer was so simple that it removed my entire plan at once. No chain of buyers. No hidden desire. No fair price. The only person who could begin the movement was the person who had refused it.

Behind us, the old woman called her son back. For one hopeful moment I thought he would take the memory. She handed him a folded coat he had left on the wall.

The great-grandfather remained with her, still walking his wet road and explaining the ditches to nobody who wanted to hear.

## Chapter 18: What the Fact Cannot Hold

I went to Yarrow with the simple mechanism.

She was moving a chair away from the wall because Del had sent word that damp would ruin its legs. Yarrow said the wall was dry. The chair scraped across the floor anyway.

“You only need to ask Ess,” I told her.

“I am aware.”

“There is no trade to arrange. There is no price. You ask under your agreement, and if the terms permit it, the memory returns.”

“I know that too.”

“Then why have you let me spend two weeks learning this?”

Yarrow stood behind the chair with both hands on its back. “I did not assign you the work.”

That was true and therefore unhelpful.

I followed her outside. Del had visited that morning about the chair, arrived late, and left angry. Yarrow said she had done nothing wrong, then began explaining the wrong thing in detail. Del had promised to come before the sun reached the opposite roof. The sun had reached it. Del came after. She claimed a cart blocked the lower street. Yarrow had seen no cart.

“Do you believe her?” I asked.

“I believe her.”

“You do not sound as if you do.”

“That is the difficulty.”

Yarrow sat on the step and looked downhill. She said she knew Del had changed. She knew they had repaired the old injury. She knew Del had returned again and again for more than twenty years. Those were facts stored in lists, habits, other memories, and the present life they shared.

What she could not remember was becoming convinced.

Before the missing afternoon, Yarrow had possessed nine years of reasons not to trust her sister. She had repeated those reasons until they fitted together. Del was careless. Del arrived only when it pleased her. Del left hard work to others and then offered explanations. The reconciliation had not erased the history, but it had changed the meaning Yarrow gave it.

Now the history remained and the change itself lived with Ess.

“When she is late,” Yarrow said, “the old account starts speaking. I tell it we settled this. I know we settled it. But I cannot remember why I believed the settlement.”

I understood at last that the missing memory did more than leave sadness. It had been a support beneath later trust. Remove the support, and the house still stood because twenty years of living had built other walls. Yet sometimes Yarrow could feel the empty place below them.

“That is worse than you said.”

“It is harder to describe than I said.”

“Then take it back.”

The answer escaped me before the new knowledge had time to change anything.

Yarrow closed her eyes. When she opened them, she did not look angry. Anger would have been easier.

“Imagine a wall you have leaned on every day,” she said. “Then imagine somebody tells you the wall is there, but your hand passes through the place where it should be. The room still has a roof. You can live in it. You simply have to remember not to lean.”

“You could have the wall again.”

“And what happens to it next?”

“You keep it.”

“For how long?”

I had no measure for that.

Yarrow returned to the chair. She tested the legs, found one uneven, and folded a scrap of cloth under it. The chair stopped rocking.

I stayed outside with the question she had given me. For the first time, return and safety no longer seemed to point in the same direction.

# Part IV: The Receipt

## Chapter 19: Careful With Her

I found Del beside the chair she and Yarrow had argued about. It had made the trip downhill again.

“She says it rocks.”

“It did. She put cloth under one leg.”

“Then it does not rock.”

“She says the cloth proves the chair is wrong.”

Del closed her eyes. I had seen Yarrow make exactly the same face.

We carried the conversation into the street because Del had work inside and I was already interfering with it. She repaired nets for two fishing crews. Three large nets lay across pegs along the wall, and she moved from tear to tear with a wooden needle. The cord tightened behind her hands in even rows.

I told her what Yarrow had explained: the old account of nine years, the factual knowledge of peace, and the absent experience that once joined them. Del continued mending while I spoke. Only the speed of the needle changed.

When I finished, she tied a knot and said, “I know.”

I had heard those words from Ess and found them cruel. From Del, they sounded tired.

“How long have you known?”

“Eight months, perhaps. I noticed before I named it.”

Yarrow had begun checking small promises. Had Del said morning or midday? Had she offered to bring bread or only said she might? She asked with care, never accusing. That care frightened Del more than anger would have. Before the transfer, the sisters argued freely because they trusted the argument would end. Afterward Yarrow sometimes spoke as though one wrong sentence might reopen an old door.

“I thought I had done something,” Del said.

“You had not.”

“People usually believe that before they ask what they did.”

She repaired another tear. A boy from one of the boats arrived with a wet bundle, placed it beside the wall, and left with instructions to return after sunset. Del's day kept moving around the private matter. I understood why she had asked about food, water, the hand, and the shutter. She was building present evidence one ordinary task at a time.

“Did you ask Yarrow?”

“I asked if she was angry.”

“What did she say?”

“No. Then she explained why she had reason to be.”

That was exactly the shape I had seen around the late chair. Yarrow trusted the fact and argued with the missing support beneath it.

Del had gone to Ess once, months earlier. She reached the passage outside his courtyard and stopped. She knew she could not request Yarrow's property. She also knew asking Ess to persuade Yarrow would use his trust against the person who had given it.

“I stood there long enough for the potter to ask if I was ill,” she said. “Then I went home.”

“You could ask Yarrow to retrieve it.”

The wooden needle stopped.

“Do not do that.”

“I only said you could.”

“You have been saying only things for two weeks. Do not turn me into the next reason she should change her mind.”

Del's refusal had more heat than Yarrow's. It came from wanting the opposite. She wanted the easy voice, the unmeasured argument, and the sister who did not inspect every promise. She also knew what return might risk.

“How do you live with both?” I asked.

“Badly, some days.” She pulled the cord through and tested the join. “That does not make either one untrue.”

Outside the harbor bell sounded once. Del rolled the finished section of net and opened the next tear. Before I left, she asked whether Yarrow's hand had hurt when moving the chair. I said it had not seemed to.

“Seemed?”

“She did not say.”

“Then I will ask her.”

Del could not restore the afternoon. She could keep asking about the hand.

## Chapter 20: What Del Did Not Ask

Del had told me not to make her part of my argument. I obeyed for the length of one night.

In the morning I went back, not to pressure her, I told myself, but to understand the reason she had turned away from Ess's courtyard. That distinction was thin enough to see through. Del saw through it before I finished greeting her.

“No, before you begin.”

“I have not asked yet.”

“Then I am saving us time.”

She was stretching the repaired net across the lane while two crew members checked each join. I waited until they rolled it and carried it downhill. Del collected loose cord from the paving. The work gave her many chances not to speak to me, and she used all of them.

“You said you reached Ess's door,” I tried.

“I did.”

“You wanted the memory returned.”

“I wanted my sister not to hurt.”

“Those are nearly the same.”

“No. That is the whole problem.”

Del sat on the doorstep with the wooden needle in her lap. She told me what happened the day Yarrow announced the transfer plan. Del argued against it. She offered to take the memory herself, which would have kept the afternoon in the family but removed it from Yarrow just the same. Yarrow asked what would happen if Del died first. Del suggested splitting the risk between several people before remembering the memory could not be copied.

They spent a season going around the same limits. One copy. One holder. Yarrow's illness. Ess's health and reputation. The cost of absence now against the chance of total loss later. No choice protected everything.

After the transfer, Del wanted to demand its return. She walked to Ess's courtyard before sunset on that same day. At the passage she realized what the demand contained.

“If Yarrow took it back for me,” Del said, “then I would get a sister who trusted me more easily. She would get the risk.”

“You would both get the afternoon.”

“No. She would get it. I would get the benefit of her having it.”

The difference was exact. Del's pain came from Yarrow's changed behavior, but the remedy would live inside Yarrow's failing memory. Del would enjoy the result without carrying the danger.

“Yarrow could still choose that.”

“She could. I can also choose not to load my want onto her side of the scale every time she has a hard day.”

Del's four words had become more than four. She still looked as if each one cost effort.

“Is that why you did not knock?”

“Partly. I also knew Ess would ask whether Yarrow sent me. He would not tell me anything else. Then I would have stood in his courtyard with my wish made public and achieved nothing except giving him one more thing to protect.”

She resumed winding spare cord around her hand. The loop was too tight. She unwound it and began again.

“Do you regret not asking?”

“Often.”

“Would you make the same choice today?”

Del looked up the hill. “Ask me tomorrow and I may hate it. That still does not make it wrong today.”

Her answer denied me the clean certainty I kept demanding from everyone. Yarrow's choice had a cost. Ess's promise kept the cost in place. Del respected both and sometimes wished she would stop.

I finally understood that nobody was waiting for me to become brave enough to fix them.

They were waiting for me to stop turning their difficult choices into an invitation.

## Chapter 21: A Wedding in the Street

The lower market closed for a wedding that had happened sixty-three years earlier.

I arrived expecting tables of trade and found tables of food instead. Awnings stretched across the street. Benches blocked three shop doors. Somebody had tied white cord around the pump, making it harder to use for no reason I could discover. Tam moved among the preparations with two lists and the expression of a person who had been given responsibility without authority.

“Whose wedding?” I asked.

“Sabb's.”

“Today?”

“Sixty-three years ago.”

Sabb was giving the memory to her granddaughter, Lio. That part required two chairs and a minute. The town had added forty people, four tables, stew, bread, sweet cakes, two musicians, and a boy whose only work was carrying a jug he frequently forgot to fill.

“Why?” I asked Tam.

She pointed at Sabb, who was arguing about the position of a bench. “Tell her a wedding needs less.”

I did not.

Sabb was small, straight-backed, and dressed in blue. Lio was about the age Sabb had been on the remembered day. She kept helping with preparations until three different relatives told her to sit down and be the person receiving the important thing. She sat for less than a minute, then stood to move a bowl out of the sun.

The memory had always been meant for her. Sabb decided when Lio was born, but she waited until the girl was old enough to understand that receiving the wedding meant Sabb would no longer have it. Lio refused twice over the years. The third time, she agreed, then waited another season to be sure.

I noticed the care because Yarrow had taught me to notice it.

Before the transfer, Sabb took Lio aside near the pump. I stayed far enough away not to hear. The talk lasted longer than the ceremony. Lio asked something twice. Sabb answered with both hands around the girl's hands. When they returned, Lio had been crying and did not hide it.

Then the street became quiet in pieces. The musicians stopped first. People near them noticed and lowered their voices. The silence moved outward until the jug boy stood holding an empty cup and staring.

Sabb and Lio faced each other. They named the memory: the morning of Sabb's wedding, beginning before she dressed and ending when she and her husband entered their first home. They named it as a gift. They both agreed.

Nothing visible crossed.

Lio's hands tightened around the chair. Sabb watched her with an expression I could not read and had no right to complete. The market held still around them.

Then Lio laughed.

“His coat,” she said.

Sabb laughed too, but the sound came a moment later. “Too large.”

“At the shoulders. You kept pulling it straight.”

“He borrowed it.”

“I know. I remember.”

The last words changed the street. People cheered. The musicians began at different times and found the same tune by the fourth measure. Food moved from hand to hand. The jug boy discovered the jug was empty and blamed another child who had not been near it.

I asked Sabb whether she was sad.

“Of course.”

“Do you regret it?”

“I do not.”

She still knew the wedding happened. She knew the coat was borrowed and that rain began before they reached the ceremony. She could tell the story because she had told it many times. But the wet street, the weight of her clothes, her husband's shoulders inside his father's coat, and the first doorway belonged to Lio now.

“That is a large price,” I said.

Sabb took a sweet cake from a passing tray. “It is a wedding. We have established this.”

Lio sat at the center table with both palms flat, looking from face to face as the old morning settled among her own memories. People approached, then stopped when Sabb raised one finger. Lio needed a little time before answering questions. Even a public gift had a private first hour.

I moved away.

Nothing about the transfer seemed tragic. The loss was real and chosen. The gain did not cancel it. Sabb could grieve what she had given and still be glad Lio carried it. Lio could receive joy and still feel the weight of taking the only copy from her grandmother.

For weeks I had used pain as proof that a choice must be reversed. The wedding gave me a different measure. Pain could also prove that something valuable had truly been spent.

By evening, I thought I understood Yarrow.

I was close. Close is dangerous when it feels like arrival.

## Chapter 22: If It Comes Home

The wedding made me gentle and confident, which was worse than being loud and confident because I mistook the change in tone for a change in purpose.

I went uphill after the tables were cleared. Yarrow sat outside with no bowl, no knife, and no work in her hands. The street below still carried music in pieces. Somebody sang one line, forgot the next, and began again farther away.

“Sabb gave Lio the wedding,” I said.

“Forty people have told me.”

“There were forty people there.”

“Then all of them came uphill.”

I hovered beside the step. For once I did not begin with a mechanism. I told Yarrow what I had finally understood: a chosen loss could still hurt, and hurt did not prove regret. Del could want a different result without owning the decision. Ess could know the cost and still keep his word.

Yarrow listened. When I finished, she said, “Good.”

The word pleased me. That should also have warned me.

“So if you asked for the memory now,” I continued, “it would not mean the first choice was wrong. It could mean the price has changed.”

Yarrow looked at me for a long time. “You have built the same road from better stone.”

“I am not saying you must.”

“You are standing at my door explaining why I may.”

“You may.”

“That I know.”

I almost stopped. The music below broke into laughter. A window opened across the street and somebody shook crumbs from a cloth.

Then I said, “What are you afraid will happen if it comes home?”

Yarrow's face changed. I had finally asked the question beneath her refusal rather than trying to replace the refusal itself.

“It may leave again,” she said.

“You could place it with Ess later.”

“If I know it is going.”

The lost spring had not announced itself. Corren's face had not faded slowly while Yarrow watched. One day the lived pieces were available; another day she reached and found facts. The illness did not wait for arrangements.

If Yarrow retrieved the afternoon, Ess would lose it. Transfer did not copy. The single experience would return to the person whose memory was already failing. She might hold it for ten years, one year, one month, or only until morning. No sign would tell her to give it back in time.

“Then it would be gone from both of you,” I said.

“From everyone.”

The word opened the full size of the choice. While Ess held the afternoon, Yarrow lacked it and the event still existed. If she took it back, she might gain the wall and later lose the whole house. The reconciliation would remain as history. Del would remember her own side. But Yarrow's exact change from nine years of anger into peace would stop.

No keeper could retrieve a thing that no longer existed.

“How likely is that?”

“I do not know.”

“How long do your memories usually—”

“There is no usual. That is what you keep trying to give me.”

She was right. I had searched for a measure because a measure could choose for us. Duration had failed in the market. It failed more severely here. Nobody could price one day of having the afternoon against a chance of preserving it for decades.

Yarrow turned her empty hands palms upward. “I have the fact. Del and I have the life that came after. Ess has the one piece that might otherwise disappear. That is the arrangement.”

“And the missing support?”

“I live carefully around it.”

“Del does too.”

“I know. That is part of the cost I did not fully see.”

She did not pretend the arrangement was clean. She had learned that removing the afternoon exposed old distrust and made Del question herself. That new knowledge hurt. It still did not make preservation worthless.

I looked through the open doorway at the chair with cloth under one leg. The chair worked. The floor was uneven. Removing the cloth would not make the floor level.

For the first time since arriving, I saw the absence correctly.

It was not evidence that safekeeping had failed.

It was evidence that the memory was still somewhere safe.

## Chapter 23: What the Absence Proves

I stayed beside Yarrow's door until the street lamps were lit.

The absence had looked like a hole because I kept asking what belonged in it. Once I asked what the empty place proved, its shape changed. A missing coin may mean theft. A missing parcel may mean loss. A missing memory on Arrit could be the receipt for preservation.

Yarrow had paid with access. She could no longer enter the afternoon. Ess's possession proved the payment had achieved its purpose.

“I have been telling him you are unhappy,” I said.

“I expected you had.”

“How could you know?”

“He told me you had been telling him things I already told him.”

I wanted the paving to open and let me descend through the hill. It did not.

“He knew before I arrived.”

“He did.”

“He knew the old distrust still comes back.”

“He knew.”

“He knew you might grieve the memory and still not want it returned.”

Yarrow folded her hands. “That was part of our agreement.”

When they set the terms, she had tried to imagine her future self. That person might be lonelier, more frightened, or less certain. She might visit Ess after a quarrel with Del and ask for relief from an immediate pain. Yarrow did not want one bad afternoon to undo the work of the careful season.

So she gave Ess an instruction. Sadness was not a request. Anger was not a request. Saying she wished she could remember was not a request. If she wanted the memory returned, she would have to name the risk, state that she understood the only copy would leave Ess, and ask under the exact return terms they had agreed.

“He is meant to make it difficult.”

“He is meant to make it clear.”

“What if you truly change your mind?”

“Then I can meet the terms.”

Ess was not a wall between Yarrow and her property. He was the pause Yarrow had chosen between pain and irreversible action. He had listened to me describe her hand on the doorway and answered that he knew because he was required to know the difference between her sorrow and her decision.

“I thought he did not care.”

“That would make him a poor keeper.”

“He looks as if he does not.”

“That helps.”

I considered all the visits I had observed. Ess did not perform sympathy because performance could turn the courtyard into pressure of another kind. A frightened client did not need to comfort the keeper. A grieving owner did not need to prove the grief was severe enough. He made room, waited, and kept the terms.

“Why did he tell you I had been there?”

“Because you were asking questions about my arrangement, and that was information I should have.”

Of course. His silence protected Yarrow from me; it did not hide my interference from Yarrow.

The distinction left me with the full weight of what I had done. I had gone to a person entrusted with a boundary and repeatedly argued that visible pain should overrule the words inside it. I had tried price, leverage, Del's wish, and a better explanation. Each attempt asked Ess to fail at the exact work Yarrow paid him to do.

“I owe him an apology.”

“You do.”

“I owe you one first.”

Yarrow waited.

I had learned enough from watching bad bargains to know that explanation can become an added price charged to the person already harmed. I did not tell her I meant well. I did not describe how unusual the world was or how much the missing memory seemed to hurt her.

“I treated your refusal as a problem to solve,” I said. “I kept bringing other people and new reasons into it after you told me to stop. That made you defend a choice you had already made. I am sorry.”

Yarrow looked at the dark street. “All right.”

She did not say I was forgiven. I did not ask.

After a while she picked up the empty bowl beside her chair and asked whether I could see a crack near the base. I went close and found one thin line in the clay.

“There,” I said.

She marked the spot with a piece of chalk. The bowl would need replacing.

The memory would stay where it was.

## Chapter 24: The Question I Kept

The next day I returned to Yarrow's step without a plan.

This was new enough that I nearly announced it. Announcing that I had no plan would have turned the absence of interference into another performance Yarrow had to recognize, so I said nothing. She gave me the cracked bowl and asked me to find whether the potter sold the same size.

I could not carry the bowl. We both looked at it.

“You may memorize the size,” she said.

I measured it against myself. The bowl was a little wider than four of me and as deep as one and a half. Yarrow said no potter had ever sold a bowl by dots. She found a length of cord, wrapped it around the rim, cut the circumference, and handed the cord to a neighbor going downhill.

“What am I for?” I asked.

“Company, apparently.”

We followed the cord.

The potter had three bowls near the right size. Yarrow tested each base on the counter, rejected one for rocking, and struck another lightly with a knuckle. Its sound displeased her. The third cost more than she wanted to pay. She and the potter argued about the price while I stayed out of it with great effort.

They settled on the second bowl after the potter admitted its sound came from the thick glaze rather than a hidden crack. Yarrow paid. The neighbor carried it uphill.

Nothing in this errand involved a memory. I kept looking for the place where the real subject would return. It did not. Yarrow discussed clay, the coming rain, and whether the neighbor's son had found work. At her door she washed the new bowl and left it upside down to dry.

Then she sat.

I had one more question about Ess's promise. It began with *what if.* Those words had started many of my worst questions because they allowed me to invent a future where another person's boundary became inconvenient. What if Ess grew ill? What if Yarrow forgot the terms? What if Del changed her mind? What if a safer keeper appeared?

Some of those risks were real. None required my answer that afternoon.

Yarrow had already planned for a keeper's age and death. Ess had a professional duty to arrange succession if needed. Yarrow's written record held the terms she wanted to remember. Del could speak if her own position changed. The people inside the arrangement had not ignored uncertainty merely because they refused to explain every defense to me.

“You are quiet,” Yarrow said.

“I have a question.”

“That has never stopped you before.”

“It is not mine.”

She glanced at me, then turned the old bowl until the chalk mark faced upward. “You are learning.”

I wanted to ask whether that meant she accepted my apology. I recognized another question that would make her provide something for my comfort. I kept that one too.

We sat through the hottest part of the afternoon. Del came up carrying the new bowl's lid, which nobody had mentioned was separate. She blamed the potter. Yarrow blamed the neighbor. The neighbor, called from two doors away, blamed me for not noticing.

“I do not know what lids belong to,” I said.

“Bowls,” all three answered.

The new bowl fit the lid. Del checked Yarrow's left hand without ceremony and found no swelling. Yarrow asked whether the repaired net had passed inspection. They moved through the practical questions that held their present life together.

No one spoke about the reconciliation afternoon.

I had mistaken silence for failure when Ess used it. On the step, I began to see the work silence could do. It made room for the bowl, the hand, the net, and whatever the sisters chose to say next.

My question remained unanswered.

For once, I did not turn that into an emergency.

# Part V: What May Be Shared

## Chapter 25: From Inside the Work

I waited two days before returning to Ess.

This was not strategy. I needed enough time to separate an apology from my wish to be told I had improved. On the first day, I still imagined Ess saying that anyone would have made the same mistake. On the second, I imagined him explaining that my questions had helped. Both versions gave me something. I went on the third day when I could accept getting nothing.

Ess was sweeping the courtyard with the old broom. Several twigs had bent outward and one dragged across the paving without moving dust.

“You need a new broom,” I said.

“I do.”

“I learned that while observing you without permission.”

He rested the broom against the wall. “I know.”

Of course he did.

I apologized. I said I had treated his privacy as evidence of bad intent. I had tried to discover a price for property he did not own, watched his routine for leverage, and asked him to interpret Yarrow's sorrow against the terms she chose. That was an attempt to make him break trust. I was sorry.

Ess picked up the broom again. “All right.”

He did not comfort me either.

For a while I watched him gather dust into a line that the broken twigs kept dividing. He made three smaller lines instead of one. When the courtyard was as clean as the broom allowed, he sat at the table.

“May I ask about the work?”

“You have asked a great deal about it.”

“About what it looks like. I have not asked what it is like for you.”

Ess considered the difference. “You may ask. I may not answer.”

That arrangement was better than the ones I had been inventing.

I began with the smell from the next courtyard. He could speak about the effect without exposing the memory's owner or content. Held memories did not occupy a separate chamber in his mind. They arrived with their own sensations and feelings, then lived among his. Most stayed quiet until something in the present matched a piece of them.

Hot oil and crushed leaves had touched one such piece. For a moment, Ess felt an expectation that belonged to someone else's kitchen.

“Were you hungry?”

“I was not.”

“Happy?”

“Not exactly. I expected a person to enter.”

“Who?”

“I do not have that part.”

The memory held the sound of cooking and the expectation of arrival, but the person's face or name lay outside the transferred span. Ess could recognize a voice he had never heard with his own ears. He knew the turn of a northern road his feet had never used. Rain on warm stone could make him miss a house he had never entered.

“How do you know which feelings are yours?”

“Practice.”

That was why he had said *not mine.* The words were not rejection. They were sorting.

Ess described it as household work. A cup belonged on one shelf and a plate on another. A sudden feeling arrived; he checked where it came from; he put it with the right memory. Usually the task took a breath. On difficult days, several pieces woke together and the sorting took longer.

“Does it hurt?”

“At times.”

“Do the owners know?”

“They know carrying has a cost. They do not owe me worry every time it costs.”

His answer corrected another assumption I had been ready to make. I wanted to turn hidden labor into sacrifice and sacrifice into a debt the owners must repay. Ess had agreed to the work and set his fees with that cost included. The difficulty did not secretly make Yarrow's memory his.

“Could you refuse a memory because it was too hard?”

“Before accepting it, yes.”

“After?”

“Then I keep the terms or arrange what the terms permit.”

He would not tell me whether any held piece had become unbearable. That answer belonged too close to identifiable clients. I heard the silence and did not move around it.

Ess noticed.

“Most people misunderstand the work,” he said.

“From outside?”

“There is not much to see from outside.”

The bare room, plain table, and repeated cup had made him look inactive. Inside, he carried roads, kitchens, rain, voices, sickness, weddings, grief, and moments without names. His quiet life gave those pieces fewer accidental shocks. Even his ordinary courtyard was part of the storage.

“Yarrow's afternoon,” I began, then stopped.

The question I wanted to ask concerned its weight inside him. It was still not mine.

Ess waited, allowing me to turn back.

“The mint is growing,” I said instead.

“It needs cutting.”

He found a small knife. I watched him work around the dead trunk, trimming living leaves from a pot I had misread on my first visit.

## Chapter 26: The Ones With Nobody Left

The conversation continued because I stopped demanding where it should go.

Ess cut the mint, washed the leaves, and spread them on a cloth inside the doorway. He said most held memories had a clear line of duty. An owner placed one for a period, a condition, or a later person. The keeper followed that line.

“And when the owner dies?”

“The terms continue.”

Some memories moved to children. Some went to another keeper. Some were returned to a family or given to a named trade. A shipbuilder might leave the memory of a failure to an apprentice. A parent might preserve a voice until a child chose whether to receive it. These arrangements could last one year or cross several lives.

Then there were memories whose lines had broken.

The owner died. The named receiver died first, refused, disappeared, or became impossible to find. The reason for preservation lost its people while the memory itself remained. Keepers called those pieces orphaned, though Ess said the word made them sound younger than they were.

“What happens to them?”

“Some agreements name an end.”

“And the others?”

“They pass.”

A keeper nearing death found another keeper willing to carry them. That person might do the same decades later. Each transfer preserved the only copy while making its original purpose harder to recover. Names fell away. Places changed. A fifteen-second scene could outlive everyone who knew why it mattered.

“Why keep one if nobody wants it?”

Ess moved the damp cloth farther from the sun. “Want is not the only promise.”

The answer brought back the great-grandfather and his ditches, except the family still knew the road's place in their history. An orphaned memory had no family arguing around it. It continued because a chain of keepers had each decided not to be the last holder.

“Do you have one?” I asked.

The silence did not come.

“Several.”

I stayed still. Ess had answered a number question, which meant the terms were different here.

“May you talk about them?”

“Some. Nobody living owns those.”

He told me about a short walk through a winter orchard. The person remembered counting five empty bird nests and hearing an axe beyond a wall. No name, goal, or important event appeared. Another memory showed a hand painting a red line around a white door. The paint dripped once. Somebody outside the scene laughed.

“That is all?”

“That is all I have.”

“Why did anyone preserve them?”

“Unknown.”

Maybe the winter walk had been the last calm moment before a death. Maybe the red line marked a marriage, a warning, or the completion of ordinary work. The meaning could have rested just outside the transferred span. The owners had once believed the pieces worth carrying. Time had kept the experience and worn away the explanation.

Ess did not turn the uncertainty into value. He did not claim every orphan held a hidden answer. Most were simply moments that had survived their reasons.

“Do they trouble you?”

“The orchard is cold when the weather changes. The door smells of paint. They are not difficult.”

“Then why mention that some are?”

Ess gathered the corners of the mint cloth and carried it to the table. “Because one repeats.”

It had been with him eleven years. He received it from an older keeper who had received it from another. The chain before that was recorded only as names, then initials, then nothing. Nobody had claimed it. Nobody knew where or when it occurred.

The memory was brief, but certain parts woke whenever Ess saw a frame being built, heard stone struck with metal, or stood near an opening he had not used before. Unlike the kitchen feeling, it did not settle after one sorting. The piece contained a statement without an answer, and his mind kept trying to finish it.

“What statement?”

Ess looked at me rather than away. “I can show you by telling. I cannot give you the memory.”

“I cannot receive one anyway.”

“That too.”

He confirmed the piece was free of living ownership and that describing it broke no agreement. Then he asked whether I wanted to hear.

Weeks earlier, I would have said yes before he reached the question. This time I checked the offer for another person's boundary and found none.

“Yes,” I said.

Ess turned the mint leaves once, placing each green edge inside the cloth.

Then he began with a frame.

## Chapter 27: For the Unanswered

Ess warned me that telling was not the same as giving.

He had the remembered angle of light, the sound of tools, the position of the witness, and the ordinary feeling of being present before anyone knew the moment would need preserving. I would receive only words selected years later by a man who had not been there with his own body.

“I understand,” I said.

“You say that often.”

“I understand the limitation.”

“Better.”

The memory began beside a thing under construction.

Ess did not call it a crossing. The original witness had no name for it inside the fragment, and Ess refused to supply one from my interest. There was a standing frame made from material he described as pale stone or something that behaved like stone. People worked around it. The witness stood to one side, near enough to hear tools strike and voices pass.

Inside the frame, work had opened a place where the world stopped.

Ess paused there. He tried several descriptions. A dark surface would have been something. An empty view would still have distance. This was neither. The remembered person looked through the frame and met no farther point, no color, and no space in which color might exist.

“No thickness?” I asked.

“I do not know. The memory does not measure it.”

“Was there another place beyond?”

“Not in what I have.”

I held my questions while he continued.

The opening was unfinished. Workers adjusted part of the frame while someone out of sight gave short instructions. The witness did not help. That fact mattered because the remembered hands remained still and the view came from a fixed place. Whoever owned the memory had watched the crossing being made.

One worker leaned close to the lower side of the frame. The person's shoulder blocked part of the action. A tool touched the pale material. When the shoulder moved, a fresh mark remained.

A circle with a dot at its center.

The courtyard changed around me without moving. I saw Ess's cracked pot, the cloth of mint, the blunt knife, the doorway, and the bright square of sky. Under all of them, I remembered the same mark cut into stone elsewhere.

“Describe it again.”

Ess did. One outer line, closed and nearly round. One small point set inside, slightly below the exact center. The mark was made deliberately. It was not a chip or stain.

“Could it have been an eye?”

“It could have been many things.”

“A map?”

“Many things includes a map.”

The old witness did not react strongly to the mark. That suggested it was familiar or unimportant, though Ess would not let me call either conclusion a fact. The worker straightened. Someone beyond the witness shifted position. Then a voice, close but outside the view, said three words.

“For the unanswered.”

Ess spoke them plainly. No whisper. No ceremony. In the memory, the voice had used the tone of a person naming where a finished object belonged.

For the unanswered.

The fragment ended immediately after the sentence. No reply. No movement through the opening. No explanation of who or what was unanswered. The witness might have turned away, continued watching, or asked the question I now wanted to ask. Those seconds had not been transferred.

Ess had carried the piece for eleven years. The earlier keeper had given him the description and admitted having no meaning for it. Ess had searched local records for the shape and words when he first received them. Nothing fit. After that he stopped treating the memory as a riddle he was obliged to solve.

“But it is a crossing,” I said.

“You say it is.”

“A frame around a place with no space inside it. Someone building the edge. The mark on the work.”

“Those are in the memory.”

“Then you agree.”

“I agree those are in the memory.”

Ess would not give me certainty borrowed from his authority. I had disliked that habit when it protected Yarrow. Here, with the answer pressing close, I understood its value.

I told him about the circle and dot I had seen cut into stone. I told him about the word *unanswered* appearing elsewhere, attached to a question I still could not define. I told him about the road that ended at an edge too clean for nature and how that edge made me inspect the crossing into Arrit.

He listened to the three accounts separately. When I finished, he did not say they belonged together.

I said it for myself.

“They are parts of one thing.”

“Perhaps.”

“Somebody made crossings.”

“The memory shows somebody making that opening.”

“It carries the mark.”

“It does.”

“And the work was for the unanswered.”

“Those words were spoken near it.”

Every sentence I tried to close, Ess reopened to the exact size of the evidence.

I wanted to be irritated. Instead I was grateful.

The memory had given me a connection, not an answer. After everything Arrit had taught me, I knew better than to make the second from the first.

## Chapter 28: One Larger Question

For several minutes I could not speak.

The silence was not empty. I was arranging three pieces that had traveled separately across worlds.

First came the mark: a circle holding a single point. I had seen it cut into worked stone where no local explanation fit. I did not know whether it named a maker, warned a traveler, or marked ownership.

Second came the word *unanswered*. It had appeared without a person or object attached. I had carried it as language waiting for a sentence.

Third came the cut road. Somebody had built toward an edge and stopped in a way weather and broken stone could not explain. That edge had made me look sideways while crossing into Arrit.

Ess's memory placed the three kinds of evidence near one another: a constructed opening, the mark applied to its frame, and the spoken purpose.

That was enormous.

It was also small. The memory did not prove every crossing shared the same builder. It did not prove the cut road had once entered one. It did not prove the mark referred to me, though I was a dot inside every circle when I passed through. It did not tell me whether *unanswered* named people, questions, places, or something no Arrit word could hold.

“Does it mean something to you?” Ess asked.

“It does mean something.”

“What?”

“That I have been asking one question in three pieces.”

“What is the question?”

I tried to say it. Who built the crossings? Why did they build them? What was the mark? Who were the unanswered? Why could I use the openings? Had somebody intended that I should? Every version split apart as soon as I spoke it.

“I do not know the question yet.”

Ess nodded as if this were an ordinary result.

“How can that be enough for you?”

“It is your question.”

The answer would once have angered me. Now it sounded like room.

I asked whether Ess wanted the fragment's meaning from me if I ever discovered it. He thought before answering. The memory had passed through keepers without a known instruction beyond continuation. No owner remained to define its end. Ess carried it because allowing it to vanish would close the possibility that it might matter later.

“If you learn something,” he said, “you may tell me. I may still not know that is why it was kept.”

“You would keep doubt even with an answer?”

“Answers can belong to the person giving them.”

He had watched me build enough conclusions to know this.

The sun moved across the courtyard. The mint leaves curled slightly as they dried. A client appeared in the passage, saw me, and waited outside. Ess looked toward the doorway. I left without asking who had arrived.

At the passage, Ess called after me.

“The northern road,” he said.

I returned. Weeks before, he had mentioned knowing a road through a held memory though he had never walked it. That memory's terms allowed description without names. He asked whether I still wanted to hear.

I did.

So we postponed the enormous question and spent the next hour on an ordinary road. It climbed from a river through dark trees. One milestone leaned toward the ditch. After rain, water crossed the road at three places, and the middle crossing was deeper than it looked. A house with a yellow gate offered water to travelers. Beyond it, the road divided near a split rock.

Ess described where the shade ended in the afternoon and which bend hid the view back toward the river. The details mattered to whoever had walked there. They did not conceal a system or ask to become one.

“Which branch did the person take at the split rock?”

“Left.”

“Where did it lead?”

“The memory ends before that.”

Of course it did.

When I finally left, I carried no new fact about the frame. I carried a road I could not enter and a junction whose other branch remained unknown. That should have felt like delay.

It felt like practice.

## Chapter 29: She Asked Twice

Four days later, Del came up the hill before breakfast and asked Yarrow to visit Ess.

She did not ask for the memory back. I knew that because she said it first, before Yarrow could answer or I could misunderstand.

“I want one fact from inside it,” Del said. “Only if you agree.”

Yarrow set down the shutter hinge she was cleaning. The front shutter had hung crooked since spring, and Del had arrived with a file, two wedges, and strong opinions about the frame. The work waited between them.

“Which fact?” Yarrow asked.

“Something you did that afternoon when I was not looking.”

Del had spent the four days since our last talk finding a request small enough to respect the custody. She did not want Ess to tell the reconciliation as a story. She did not want the words that had changed Yarrow's mind or the feeling of the repair. She wanted one observed action from Yarrow's side that Del had missed while living her own side.

“Why?” Yarrow asked.

“Because I know what I did. I know what I said, or enough of it. I keep trying to use my memory to guess yours.” Del looked at the hinge rather than at her sister. “I would like one thing I do not have to guess.”

Yarrow could have refused. Ess could have refused if the terms did not allow disclosure. The fact itself might have no use or might hurt. Del named each limit without making it into a reason she deserved the answer.

Yarrow picked up the hinge, wiped one corner, and put it down again.

“All right.”

The yes came quietly. It still changed the morning.

We went downhill together. Del carried the file and wedges because she intended to return to the shutter. Yarrow carried nothing. I floated beside them and did not ask what either hoped Ess would choose.

At the courtyard, Ess heard the request twice: first from Del, then from Yarrow. He asked Yarrow whether she permitted him to share a fact from the held afternoon with Del. Yarrow said yes. He asked whether the permission applied only to the fact he selected that day. She said yes again.

The care took longer than the answer.

Ess closed his eyes. The courtyard remained ordinary around him. Mint leaves dried on the cloth. The pot's dead trunk cast a thin shadow across the table. Somewhere beyond the wall, a broom struck paving in steady pulls.

When Ess opened his eyes, he looked at Del.

“Near the end, you stood to leave,” he said. “Yarrow asked whether you would return the next day. You said you would. At the doorway, she asked you again.”

That was all.

Del pressed the file flat against her coat. She repeated the count as a question. Ess confirmed it. When she said the second asking was absent from her own memory, he reminded her that she had already turned toward the street.

She faced Yarrow. “We had used that whole day to end nine years apart. I reached the door, and you needed my promise again?”

Yarrow listened as though Del had brought news of someone she once knew well. “It seems I did.”

“You never let me see that fear.”

“I had managed to put down the anger,” Yarrow said. “I could not pick up fear in front of you on the same day.”

Del's mouth tightened. She looked down at the wedges in her hand, then at the passage, then back at Yarrow. Nothing passed into her. Ess had not transferred the question at the door. He had given a report, and the memory remained whole inside him.

Yet the report changed what Del could see.

For eight months she had read Yarrow's caution as a return toward anger. The fact from the doorway offered another shape. Yarrow had been cautious at the very start of the repaired years too, after forgiveness had occurred and before fear had learned to trust it.

“When you check whether I said morning or midday,” Del began.

“I know,” Yarrow said.

“You do not know what I was going to say.”

“I know what you think it means.”

“Do you?”

“No,” Yarrow admitted. “But we are in Ess's courtyard, and he has done enough work.”

Ess agreed by moving the mint cloth to catch more sun.

On the climb back, the sisters argued about the shutter. Del said the hinge had worn unevenly. Yarrow said the frame had shifted. Del pointed out that filing the hinge took ten minutes while rebuilding the frame took two days. Yarrow said speed did not turn the wrong repair into the right one.

They reached the house still disagreeing.

The shutter came off after Del removed the upper pin and Yarrow supported the lower edge. I stayed clear because it was broad enough to sweep me across the street. They set it against the wall, tested the frame with the wedges, and discovered both had been partly right. The hinge was worn and the frame had shifted.

“We fix the hinge first,” Del said.

“We set the frame first.”

“The hinge takes ten minutes.”

“You have said.”

They filed the hinge first.

When they rehung the shutter, it closed but left a gap at the top. Yarrow did not say *I told you.* She looked at the gap until Del said it for her.

“The frame has shifted.”

“Has it?”

“Do not enjoy this.”

They took the shutter down again. The woman two doors away came out to report that her husband had corrected the same frame in nineteen minutes six years earlier. Yarrow told her to fetch him. The woman said he was asleep. Del said then his record was safe.

The second attempt corrected the top and made the bottom catch. The third left the shutter slightly crooked but able to open and close without force. Yarrow tested it three times. Del tested it once more because Yarrow's tests had apparently not counted.

Neither sister explained the courtyard while they worked. Del did not ask Yarrow to promise that every careful question came from fear rather than anger. Yarrow did not use the old memory as proof that her present behavior meant only one thing. They had received one small fact, not a complete answer.

By late afternoon, the shutter stood imperfectly in a partly shifted frame, held by a filed hinge. The repaired thing showed every condition that had made the repair difficult.

Del packed the file. Yarrow gathered the old pin and shavings. Before leaving, Del said, “I will come tomorrow and seal the edge.”

Yarrow looked at her.

“Tomorrow,” Del repeated.

“I heard you.”

“I know you did.”

Del went downhill. Yarrow closed the shutter. It caught once, then settled.

The afternoon with Ess stayed exactly where Yarrow had put it.

What changed was the work they could do around its absence.

## Chapter 30: The Edge From This Side

I left Arrit the next morning.

This was not a dramatic decision. The crossing remained where it had been below the harbor, and another world waited on the other side without telling me which one. I had learned what Arrit could teach me. Staying longer because I liked the people would not make the old memory contain another second.

The town knew I was leaving before I told anyone.

Tam found me near the lower market and handed me a folded paper. It listed the value of her time, the questions I had interrupted, two clients I had distracted, and a credit for warning a cart about Yarrow's water jar.

“What am I meant to do with this?”

“Owe me.”

“I may never return.”

“Then the debt becomes rare and increases in value.”

I pointed out that her own market had disproved rarity as a measure. Tam said the paper proved I had learned something and added that service to the total. I could not read the figures because I did not know her marks, which seemed safer.

She folded the paper once more and tucked it beneath the strap around the cracked tree pot in Ess's courtyard later that morning. Ess found it, read it, and said nothing. Tam said this meant he agreed to hold my debt. Ess said it meant he knew better than to argue with her before breakfast.

That was the closest I came to settling the account.

At Yarrow's, the new bowl stood beside the step. The repaired shutter was closed, slightly out of square, with fresh seal along one edge. Del had returned when she said she would.

“I am going,” I told Yarrow.

“So you are.”

“How does everyone know?”

“You went to the crossing yesterday and stared at it for an hour.”

“I was measuring.”

“You were saying goodbye to an opening.”

Both were true.

I asked whether she wanted anything from the next world. Yarrow asked whether I knew where I was going. I said no. She said then she could not know what to request, which was reasonable and still felt like refusal.

“Will you be all right?” I asked.

Yarrow looked through the crooked shutter at the street. “That is a large question for this hour.”

I made it smaller. “Will Del seal the other edge?”

“She says the other edge does not need it.”

“Does it?”

“Plainly.”

That was the answer I took.

I did not ask whether Yarrow might ever retrieve the memory. I did not ask what she would do if another loss came. Those choices belonged to the people who would remain on the terrace after I left.

Del came up while I was there, carrying no tools and denying she had come to inspect the seal. The sisters began discussing the edge immediately. Yarrow said the damp would enter. Del said the wood needed room to move. Neither lowered her voice for me.

I said goodbye while they were still disagreeing.

Ess sat in the courtyard with a new broom leaning against the wall. Tam had found one, though neither of them would tell me what he paid. The mint cloth was gone. The repaired cup stood on the table.

“You are leaving,” he said.

“Everybody knows.”

“It is a town.”

I thanked him for the fragment. He corrected me: the fragment was not his. I thanked him for telling it. That he accepted.

“If I learn what it means, I may return.”

“Return if you choose.”

“You do not sound excited.”

“You have not learned it yet.”

Another exact answer.

I went downhill past the potter, the cloth shop, the rope seller, and the step where one fishing afternoon had first changed the world for me. Boats were already crowding the piers. A crew lowered empty baskets onto a deck. Somebody argued that a rope was too short; somebody else proved it was the same rope used yesterday. The harbor did not stop because I had reached the end of my questions there.

Beyond the last pier, the water darkened. The crossing waited just above it, visible to me as a difference in the place rather than a door anyone else could use. I approached slowly.

I turned sideways as I had on arrival.

From that angle, the edge seemed to narrow until it became impossible. I moved higher, then lower. I looked for a seam, a frame, a circle holding a dot, any trace of tools. The light changed on the water and made a line that vanished when a wave passed.

I tried the tests available to a person with no tools and very little body. I aligned the edge with the end of the pier, moved sideways until the stones disappeared behind it, and counted how far I traveled before they returned to view. I repeated the path from above. The two counts disagreed because the boats moved beneath me and I had no fixed mark in the air.

A gull flew through the space near the crossing without vanishing. That established only that the bird had missed the active part, which I already knew because ordinary creatures used the harbor without leaving their world. A thrown splash of water passed below. Sunlight passed through or around; I could not tell which. Every test required a boundary I could place, and placing the boundary was the very problem I was trying to solve.

On the cut road, the stone had supplied a surface. Here the opening hung over moving water with no frame visible from Arrit. If somebody had built it, the useful structure might exist on another side, or outside the directions I could inspect. The old memory showed workers beside pale material. It did not promise that every crossing kept its frame, or that a traveler standing inside the work could see it.

I moved back toward the harbor until the passage stopped pulling at me. Then I approached along a different line. The same instant arrived: water, air, an impossible thinning, and the sense of another place waiting without distance between us.

I wanted the repeated result to become proof. Repetition only proved my experience repeated.

I could not prove the edge had no width. I could not prove this crossing matched the one in Ess's fragment. I could not prove whoever marked that old frame had anything to do with me.

I knew something smaller and stronger.

At least one crossing had been built. A witness had watched the work. A deliberate mark had been placed on its frame. Near it, a voice had named the unanswered.

Those facts now belonged together. Their meaning did not.

Behind me, Arrit made its morning noise. People bought, sold, gave, refused, preserved, forgot, and carried pieces of lives that were not theirs. Yarrow's afternoon remained in a quiet courtyard. Del would return to the shutter. Ess would sort the next borrowed feeling. Tam would probably increase my debt.

The world did not become complete because I was leaving it.

I reached the boundary. Another place began to gather beyond it, still without shape.

I went through slowly.

I watched the edge for the entire passage.
