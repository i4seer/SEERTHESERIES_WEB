SEER WORLDS: THE LINE AT VELL

# Part I: The Divided River

## Chapter 1: Rain on Both Sides

Rain found me before anything else did.

It was a light, steady rain that made the grass bend and put a silver skin on the river below me. I had arrived above one bank without knowing how, which was becoming less surprising and more annoying each time it happened. Four worlds had come before this one. I could not return to any of them. I could not choose the next. I could only look down, work out what kind of place had taken me, and try not to make a bad decision too early.

Vell did not appear ready to help with that. It looked ordinary.

There were hills low enough to see over. A road followed the water. Wet fields filled the space between two small towns, and smoke from their roofs flattened under the clouds. I had seen cliffs whose tops were missing in the sky, stone plains built from columns, a world under water, and another place where there was no sky to miss. After those, a green valley looked almost suspiciously reasonable.

The rain stopped while I was deciding whether that was good. Sunlight came under the clouds and ran along the wet grass. A cart on the road shone at one wheel. The river stayed brown.

Look at a brown river in a green valley and you already think you know it. Water comes from the high end. It goes toward the low end. Rain makes more of it. Stones trouble it for a while, and then it goes on. I knew those things without needing to know the name of the river.

I went lower anyway.

Leaves turned on the surface near the upstream town. They did not hurry. One leaf caught in a slow curl beside the bank, came free, and drifted toward a shallow crossing. Beyond that crossing, several more leaves shot away so quickly that I lost them among the reflected trees.

The riverbed had probably narrowed. That was the ordinary answer, so I measured the width by moving from one bank to the other. It did not narrow. I followed the water and watched for a steeper fall. The valley did not provide one. The same wet stones showed through on both sides of the crossing.

I chose a pale leaf upstream and kept beside it. This is easier for me than it would be for you. I float, and a river cannot wet my feet because I do not have any. The leaf took its time reaching the shallow stones. At one place, between one moment and the next, it raced ahead of me.

I stopped. The leaf did not.

There was a boundary across the water. Nothing marked it from where I hovered, but I could find it again. On one side, the current carried a twig past a dark stone in five of my counts. On the other, a second twig covered a similar distance in one. I tried three more times. Five. One. Five. One.

Water can speed up. That was not the strange part. It speeds up where a channel squeezes it, where the ground drops, or where something beneath the surface turns it. Here the change occupied less distance than the length of my body. Slow water reached an invisible place and became fast water. The change did not spread downstream or creep back toward the ford. It held still.

I crossed above it and felt nothing. I crossed again lower, then higher. The current changed at the same place under me each time.

By then the last rain had left the grass, and the sunlight was gone. I had spent most of the afternoon proving that a river was doing what I could already see it doing.

That may sound foolish. It did not feel foolish then. A wrong first measurement is common. Four wrong measurements in the same direction are useful. If the ground, channel, weather, and amount of water had not changed, something else had.

I rose until I could see the crossing from bank to bank. A row of squat stones climbed the hill on my left. I had mistaken them for old markers or the remains of a fence. On the other bank, another row continued in the same straight path. The river's change sat exactly between two of them.

The place had not been waiting for me to notice its secret. Someone had numbered it.

I moved toward the nearest stone, and something gray-green struck me hard enough to spin us both over the fast water.

## Chapter 2: Ruck at Full Speed

The gray-green thing recovered first.

It looped beneath me, came up on my other side, and said sorry several times while moving too quickly to hear whether I accepted. Then it held itself still. That appeared to require effort. It was shorter than most creatures I had met, with a narrow head, flat wide-set eyes, four fingers, and a soft fringe along both forearms. The fringe stood out as if the collision had surprised it too.

It announced that I was new, circled me once to make sure, and asked what I was. I gave the only answer I had.

“I don't know.”

That usually creates more questions. This creature only blinked and asked if I came from long. When I said I came from somewhere else, it pointed toward the slow bank with all four fingers. “That's long. This is quick. Which are you?”

I looked from one side of the river to the other. “I don't know that either.”

For the first time since arriving, I saw it become completely still. Its arm fringe settled. Then interest won, and it announced that its name was Ruck, that the river was Vell, that the valley was Vell, and that the world was also Vell. I asked whether nobody had considered three names. Ruck asked why anyone would need them.

That was fair. I had not needed any name until then.

Ruck landed beside the nearest stone post and slapped the moss with one hand. A shallow number had been cut into the face. The next post stood forty or so paces up the hill. I could follow the row all the way to the ridge now that I knew to look.

“The line runs through them,” Ruck said. “Tarr put this one straight yesterday. It was leaning.”

I asked if Ruck meant the line in the water. Ruck corrected me: it was the line everywhere.

Ruck ran uphill. I followed over grass still soft from rain. Nothing visible joined one post to the next. There was no change in the color of the ground, no ditch, no fence, and no different kind of growth. Ruck stopped halfway between two stones and pointed at the turf.

“There.” When I asked how Ruck could tell, it said, “I live here.”

This was not useful, but Ruck had already crossed. I followed.

The hill gave a sudden sideways jump without moving. Light arrived before I was ready for it. The sound of water seemed to fold in the wrong place, and Ruck's body blurred, settled, and became ordinary again. The whole failure lasted less than one of my counts.

I went back.

The jump happened in reverse. Ruck made a noise so sharp that I thought something else had struck it. Then I understood it was laughing.

Ruck said I had bent. I denied it. Ruck then folded its whole body in three different directions as a demonstration. My body cannot do any of those things.

I crossed again to check the place. The lurch came at the same strip of grass. On the quick side, Ruck's laughter burst out in little pieces. On the long side, the same sound stretched into a low noise that seemed unlikely to end. I crossed twice more, partly for the measurement and partly because Ruck's imitation became worse each time.

By the fifth crossing, my senses adjusted almost before I noticed them doing it. The boundary remained strange. Passing it had become a task.

Ruck approved. “Everybody gets it.” Everyone felt the lurch, Ruck explained, but mine was funny because I had not known it was coming. The explanation did not improve the imitation.

Then Ruck ran toward the road, called for me to follow, and reached it before the last word reached me.

I stayed between the posts for one more count. On one side, rainwater slid from a blade of grass. On the other, another drop completed the same journey five times faster. I could not see any mark between them, but my senses now knew exactly where the world changed.

Ruck shouted again from below. I followed, because anyone who can collide with a stranger, explain a divided world, and become impatient with that stranger inside five minutes is probably worth keeping in sight.

## Chapter 3: Both Columns

Ruck led me into Ford and then disappeared.

That is not quite fair. Ruck pointed at a line of roofs, said, “Ford,” as if I might have mistaken the town for a tree, and spent the next several minutes showing me six places at once. Then somebody called from a yard. Ruck called back, remembered an errand, and left so fast that the dust reached me after Ruck was gone.

There are two towns named Ford. I learned that before I learned where to stand in the quick one. Every cart, basket, animal, and Vellin seemed to have a route already, and none of the routes included a floating stranger who stopped to inspect things. I moved above head height until I understood the traffic.

The market appeared in an open square soon after sunrise. Stalls unfolded from carts. Cloth went up. Food came out. By the time I had watched one seller arrange a row of flat gray cakes, two bargains and an argument had happened beside me. The argument ended with both Vellin laughing, though I never discovered what had been disputed.

The gray cake was pressing. It was made from a root, lasted for months, and seemed to be eaten by everyone who complained about it. One seller gave me a piece before either of us knew whether I could eat. I could not, so I carried it while three customers told me it was terrible and bought some.

Quickbread was different. Bakers made it in the morning. By the following day it was no longer worth having, so the town ate every loaf before night. Nothing about that seemed wasteful to them. A quick year held enough mornings to make permanence less attractive.

The food nobody stopped talking about was slowfruit. It grew across the line, took a long year to ripen, and almost never reached this market. A seller old enough to remember tasting one rested both hands on her stall when I asked what it was like. She searched for a useful answer, found none, and finally said it had been the finest thing she ever ate. Then she served three people without looking at them because part of her attention was still somewhere in that taste.

I wanted to know how long a long year was. That brought me to the clocks.

Vellins did not use clocks as I understood them. They used counts. One count was close to what I would have called a minute while I stood on the quick side. Five counts made a span, which was close to one minute for somebody across the line. Posted times appeared in pairs. Market opening, ferry work, deliveries, public games, even a notice about a missing basket had one figure for each rate.

The arithmetic was easy. Divide by five to go one way. Multiply to return.

I started a calendar.

By the third day, I had started a second calendar because the first one was not doing enough damage. One held quick counts and one held long spans. I kept them beside each other and marked every sunset. This should have produced a tidy record of the same days. Instead, I entered a ferry notice in the wrong column, counted one afternoon twice, and converted a market closing that had already been converted by the person who wrote it.

Ruck found me checking the pages near the ford. Ruck had no pages and made no visible calculation before pointing out two errors.

“Children know this,” Ruck said.

“I am not a Vellin child.”

“That explains it.”

It did not explain it. The problem was not five. I knew five perfectly well. The problem was that both columns described the same meeting without agreeing on how much life the wait contained. My marks kept treating one as a translation of the other, and then my attention followed the wrong side.

I said the valley would be simpler with one calendar.

Ruck agreed. “Use counts.”

“That is one calendar for quicks.”

“Yes.” Ruck looked pleased that I had finally understood.

At the end of the market day, the stalls vanished almost as quickly as they had risen. The pressing seller packed what remained for tomorrow. The baker had nothing left. A long Vellin on the far road had taken most of the day to pass between two trees, and from where I floated he still appeared to be standing there.

I added one mark to each calendar. Both were correct. I checked them anyway.

## Chapter 4: The Word Soon

The message frames stood beside the ford where everybody had to pass them.

Each frame held narrow slots. In the slots sat tiles marked with names, dates, and short messages. Some were fresh enough to show dust from the cutting. Others had rain in their marks and moss beginning along one edge. I counted forty-three tiles the first time I stopped. Six days later, twelve of the same tiles were still there.

I asked a woman collecting three of them why nobody carried messages to the house where they belonged. She said the post was where they belonged. A long would check when a long came by. A quick would do the same. Nobody crossed simply to knock on a door, because arriving at a useful time on the other rate was mostly luck.

One tile had two dates and one word: `soon`.

Soon caused more trouble in Vell than either calendar. A quick used it for something expected before much of the day had passed. A long might mean before the season changed. Both understood the difference. Both heard the other rate use the word and knew it had stretched or shrunk. They still used it because the word felt correct while they were saying it.

I watched the result beside the ferry landing. A long called across that a repaired tool would come soon. The quick waiting for it thanked him and went back to tying bundles. When I asked when she expected it, she glanced at the far bank and said, “Not soon. His soon.” Then she pulled another bundle tight and decided to borrow a tool until hers arrived.

No one was confused. That bothered me more than confusion would have. A mistake can be corrected. Here, the word arrived with its problem already understood, and the waiting person simply rearranged her work.

Ruck found me counting old tiles on my ninth day. Ruck did not ask why. By then, the answer was obvious.

I asked what happened when a quick and a long wanted to be friends instead of people who exchanged tools. Ruck said they visited. I asked how often. Ruck said often enough. Every answer moved the question into a new word with the same problem.

Ruck offered an aunt as proof. She had a long friend and had known him forever. When I asked when they last met, Ruck looked toward town as if the date might be written on a roof. “A while.” I asked which kind. Ruck repeated the word and left to meet somebody who had apparently been waiting during our entire conversation.

There was a name for a friendship that survived the line: matched. Vellins spoke it with respect, but they usually applied it to somebody elsewhere. A cousin upriver was matched. Someone's old teacher had been matched for sixty years. Ruck's aunt might be, though Ruck had already lost interest before I learned enough to decide.

That afternoon I watched two children exchange tiles. The quick child placed hers in the frame, ran a complete circle around it, checked twice, and finally went home. The long child arrived while the sun moved across half the sky for me. She read the message, smiled slowly, and pressed her own tile into the empty slot.

The quick child did not return before dark.

Nothing had failed. That was what I kept getting wrong. The frame had held the words. The date in each column had told the truth. The long child had answered on the first chance she had, and the quick child had gone on with her day. Their system had worked exactly as designed.

It did not make either child present for the other's waiting.

I added the exchange to my two calendars and then erased it. It was not mine to record. The decision was late, but I made it.

On the following morning, the quick child's tile was gone. The reply remained. I left it in its slot and stopped counting how long it waited.

## Chapter 5: The Ferrywoman's Wall

I met Sella because I was blocking her landing.

The ferry came out of the fast current at an angle. Its bow cleared the last marker, swung toward the bank, and would have reached the rope post neatly if I had not been hovering where the stern needed to go.

The ferrywoman called for me to move left. I moved right. Her next instruction specified whose left, and I went over the water.

She pulled once on the steering oar, and the boat slid into the space I had left. Four crates, one long passenger, and the ferrywoman arrived without touching the bank hard enough to make a sound.

She was older than Ruck, with the same gray-green skin and wide eyes, and she worked without any of Ruck's spare movement. She tied the bow, fixed a second line, placed a step for the passenger, and waited while he crossed it at long speed. Waiting did not make her restless. She used the time to inspect the nearest rope.

When the passenger reached dry ground, she identified me as the floating creature. I gave my name. She gave hers while lifting the first crate, then said Ruck had reported my bad calendars. I answered that Ruck said many things quickly. “Usually more than once,” Sella said.

That was my first reason for staying near the ferry. The second was the boat.

The current on the quick side moved hard enough to take an empty craft downstream before a long could finish pushing away. Sella crossed by beginning above the place she wanted to reach. She read the pull from floating leaves, the color over shallow stone, and small changes along the bank. I watched five trips before I understood that none followed the same path. They only ended at the same two landings.

On the sixth trip, she caught me measuring the angle from the marker stakes and asked if I was coming. She did not wait for my request. “If you're in when I leave,” she said as she untied the stern. “I do not return for anyone.”

I entered the boat. Sella pushed off at once.

At the boundary, the water beneath the hull changed rate. The boat pulled sideways. My senses lurched, one rope snapped taut, and Sella eased the oar through her hands until the bow came around. She did not hurry. That surprised me enough that I forgot to watch the far landing.

I had decided quick people must be impatient. Their work happened fast; their children grew fast; their bread failed inside a day. It seemed reasonable that they would run after every moment. Sella did the opposite. She arrived before cargo. She set every rope before it was needed. Nothing chased her because she had already met it.

After we returned, I asked why she was always early. “So I can see what's wrong before it becomes late.”

The boathouse explained how she did that. Its back wall held six years of marks in two careful columns. Every day had a water level. Crossings were counted. Weather changes had short notes. When the current shifted enough to alter her route, Sella marked the place and what she had changed. The newest entry was from that morning.

No one required the records. The ferry belonged to her. No office collected them, and no older Vellin checked her totals. She kept them because tomorrow's river had usually started giving notice several days earlier.

I asked how far ahead she could read it. Four days, she said, if the rain behaved in its ordinary way. Less otherwise. When I asked why she wrote instead of remembering, Sella glanced at the wall. “Because remembering changes to fit today.”

That answer made me like her before I knew enough to decide whether liking her was sensible.

I stayed through the next unloading. This time I kept clear of the stern. A crate had split at one corner, so Sella turned it, moved the sound crates first, and made the owner bring a board before she let him lift it. She did not soften the instruction. She also held the weight while he fitted the board.

Near sunset, she entered the final water mark and compared it with the previous three. The levels made a slight descending line. Tomorrow's crossing would move upstream, she said, but when I asked how far she gave me no guess. “I'll know in the morning.”

It was the first uncertain thing I heard her say. She wrote nothing more because she did not yet know more. I looked at my two calendars, full of corrections and confidence, and put them away.

## Chapter 6: A Board on the Line

In the morning, Sella moved the crossing upstream by seven paces.

She had expected six. The river had cut a little harder at the bank during the night, so she pulled two marker stakes, sounded the shallows with a striped pole, and set them again. I kept the guide cord straight between the landing and the first safe stone. That was useful work I could do without hands, provided nobody asked how.

By midday the ferry had a clean route. Sella checked it with an empty crossing, returned with cargo, and entered seven paces on the wall. She did not change yesterday's forecast. A forecast that had missed by one pace still belonged to yesterday.

The people gathering near the posts that evening were not waiting for the ferry. They carried a board ruled into dark squares and two cloth bags full of river stones. Sella tied the boat above the new landing and joined them.

Riverstones was played directly across the boundary. Half the board rested on quick ground and half on long ground. The quick player placed a piece, crossed a marked line, and waited. The long player answered from the other side. A long experienced a game of about an hour. The quick spent closer to five.

That should have made the game unbearable to both of them. It did not.

The quick player had to hold attention much longer than usual. The long player had to decide much sooner. Each was uncomfortable in a different direction, and neither had an advantage from being at home in the rate of play. I asked for the rules. Three people explained at once, disagreed on the fourth move, and began a game before resolving the disagreement.

I watched Sella wait for an answer. She inspected one fingernail, looked once toward the ferry rope, and then did nothing at all. Her long opponent moved a stone. Sella responded so quickly that he laughed, though the sound reached our side as a deep vibration.

Children used the boundary differently. They stood in two groups and shouted across it. A quick child's sentence reached the long side as a brief chirp. A long child's reply came back as a stretched groan. No words survived either direction. This was the entire game. The children found it excellent. Their parents had the look of people who had already heard every possible version.

Ruck joined, produced the highest chirp yet, and ran away before the answering groan ended.

Near dark, one of the board's legs sank into the damp earth. Players caught the stones, but the corner struck a small route marker beside the bank. The marker leaned toward the fast water.

Sella was beside it before the board lay flat again. She cleared the wet soil from the base and sent somebody for broken stone. I held the marker upright while she packed the pieces around it. This marker was wood and guided the ferry; it was not one of the numbered line posts on the hills. Even so, Sella checked it from the landing, returned, shifted it the width of one finger, and checked again.

“You could do that tomorrow,” one player called.

“And move every crossing until then?” Sella answered.

Nobody asked twice.

The marker wanted to turn while the packing settled. I held the guide cord against its notch. Sella pressed the last stone into place and stood back.

“I used to do all this with somebody,” she said.

I thought she meant the riverstones board. “Play?”

“The landing. The markers. Turnings when the channel moved.” She checked the cord once more. “All of it.”

“Who?”

Sella pulled mud from between two fingers. “It doesn't matter now.”

She left the name out, and I let the empty place stay empty. That was a choice, though I would like credit for it only if you forget how recently I had written two children's messages into my private calendar.

We finished packing the marker. By then the riverstones game had resumed. No one remembered whose turn had been interrupted, so both players accused the other of stealing a move. Sella listened long enough to discover that neither could prove anything. Then she took the board apart and announced that everybody had lost.

She stored the board in the boathouse, entered the damaged marker on her wall, and left no space for the person she had mentioned.

I noticed the missing name anyway.

# Part II: Two Accounts

## Chapter 7: Across for a Day

Sella took me to the long side two days later because three empty crates needed collecting and I was already in the boat.

I had crossed the boundary many times by then. I had not stayed long for more than a few counts. The lurch ended as usual, the ferry eased into the slower current, and the far landing became solid beneath us. Then Sella began tying the boat.

Her hands barely moved.

I knew she was working at an ordinary pace for that side. I could see each part of the knot forming. From where I floated, however, the rope seemed to rest in her fingers while the sun continued across the water. I went up the bank, inspected the frame around a shed door, counted twenty-seven roof stones, and returned before she completed the second loop.

“Do I look like that from here?” I asked.

Sella's answer came low and stretched. I understood only that she found the question unhelpful.

Long Ford had more space in it than the quick town. Houses stood farther apart. The road was laid from fitted stone rather than packed dirt, and every gutter held the same slope. Nobody had built those things in a hurry, because hurry had never been useful here. A Vellin greeted me from a doorway. The greeting lasted long enough for me to notice a cracked hinge, watch a cart enter the road, and wonder whether leaving before the final sound would be rude.

It was. I waited.

Quicks usually returned across the line before half their day had passed. Sella unloaded the crates, collected the empty ones, and left me with the clear understanding that the ferry would depart when she did. I told her I would find my own way back. She looked doubtful, but doubt did not alter her schedule.

I followed a track uphill. The verges were trimmed to equal widths. Old drainage stones stood under every low place. Someone had repaired a washed section with rock that did not quite match, and moss had grown over the joins. Work stayed visible here long enough to become part of the ground.

The orchard began above the town.

At first I thought the trees were scattered wild across the slope. They stood too far apart for a planted field, and their trunks were thick enough to belong to a forest. Then I saw the alignment. Each tree occupied the center of a broad square. Narrow channels carried water between them, and ladders rested against several trunks.

A Vellin stood high in the nearest tree with one arm inside its branches. I waited below. After what felt like several minutes, he turned his head.

His fringe grew long along both forearms. Ruck had told me that was a long-side fashion and had demonstrated strong feelings by making a face. I had thought the face unfair until I saw the fringe catch on a twig twice during the Vellin's descent.

“You must be Dot,” he said when he reached the ground.

“How do you know?”

“A floating stranger is difficult to confuse with anyone else.” He smiled. “I'm Bern.”

He was twenty-two, he told me, and he cared for the orchard. Then he offered me a slice of something, forgot I could not eat, apologized, and asked if I had seen the upper ford before I answered. He asked questions the way Ruck moved, except every question arrived slowly and the next began before he had used the answer.

I liked him. I also wanted him to complete one subject.

Bern climbed partway back up the ladder to show me a branch he was training. He had tied it toward an open space where light would reach next season. Nothing about the work looked careless. His talk wandered; his hands did not.

That corrected another idea I had been carrying. I had expected longs to plan every choice because they possessed so much time. Bern decided what to discuss as each thought reached him. He changed his mind easily and trusted later to remain available.

“Does later ever run out?” I asked.

He looked honestly puzzled. “It hasn't yet.”

Wind moved across the high branches while he returned to work. From the orchard I could see the whole valley: slow river above the ford, fast river below, the post line cutting straight over both hills, and Sella's ferry already back at the quick landing.

I had spent less than one long morning there. On the other bank, most of Sella's day was gone.

## Chapter 8: Fruit for the Boat

Bern's orchard grew the fruit everyone in quick Ford remembered.

The nearest ripe one hung under three broad leaves. It was dark gold, almost as large as I was, and its weight had pulled the branch into a wooden support. Bern lowered it in a sling rather than cutting it free at once. He checked the skin, turned it toward the light, and found one pale mark I could barely see. A bird had made it. The skin was still whole, but Bern said it might not be tomorrow.

He wrapped that side with mesh and left the fruit on the tree. Slowfruit took a long year to ripen. Five quick years passed below the hill while one crop formed here. A quick farmer could plant, harvest, and plant again many times before Bern chose a single fruit.

When I asked whether a quick could keep the orchard, Bern considered it while moving the ladder. “For a little while.”

The oldest trees had been planted by his grandmother. His father had set most of the younger ones. The youngest were older than Bern, and Bern had planted none. “I mean to,” he said when I noticed. “There's no rush.”

The orchard made his answer appear reasonable. He cared for work begun before him and expected somebody else to receive what he began. Trees outlived the person tending them. A gap this year could wait until another year, and another year did not look scarce from that slope.

Bern finally chose a fruit from a shaded tree. He cut the stem, guided the sling down, and set the fruit on a padded frame. Its smell reached me then. I do not eat, but smell does not require eating. It was warm and sharp and green at once. I understood why the market seller had stopped serving people properly when she remembered it.

Bern asked me to take the fruit to the ferry. Whoever was working would know its destination. When I asked when it needed to arrive, he looked at the fruit, then at me. “When you go.”

I moved beneath the sling and found I could keep the fruit steady against myself. It was not heavy in the way a stone was heavy, but it required attention. Bern secured the mesh around it, forgot he had already offered me food, offered another slice, and returned to the high branches.

I should have left then. Instead, I followed him between trees because I wanted to understand how a person could arrange work for fruit he might never eat. Bern answered freely. He spoke about soil, channels, pruning, and a bad wind that had once taken three trees. Then he mentioned an old friend at the crossing.

He did not give a name.

They had met as children, he said, and helped with the river whenever its safe path moved. She knew the water better than anyone. She could place a marker after one reading and make every other worker look as though they had been guessing.

The person sounded familiar. I still did not ask. Sella's missing name belonged to Sella until she gave it.

Bern tied one branch, untied it, and moved the cord higher. “She's been different lately.” By different, he meant she seemed finished with him before he finished speaking. He glanced toward the ford. “I keep thinking I missed something.”

There was no accusation in his voice. He seemed to be inspecting a damaged branch whose cause he could not find.

I asked whether he had spoken to her. He planned to do it at the next turning, when they worked the river together. He named the time in long spans. I converted it, wrote it in both calendars, and got two different dates before I found my error.

Bern waited without making me feel hurried. That may have been kindness. It may only have been his rate. I could not tell, and for once I kept both possibilities.

When I left, he was back in the tree. I carried the slowfruit downhill with its damaged side protected under mesh. The quick bank shone beyond the river. I could see the roof of Sella's boathouse and the line of her landing.

Bern had given the fruit to whoever happened to be working there. He had described his oldest friend without naming her. Those facts might have met in the same person, but they did not have to.

I carried the fruit toward the ford and tried not to make them meet before Sella did.

## Chapter 9: The Uncollected Crates

I carried the slowfruit for two quick days.

That sounds careless. The fruit was not forgotten. Bern had said whoever worked the ferry would know its destination, but the ferry had three different helpers during that time and none claimed it. I kept the mesh dry, turned the pale mark away from direct sun, and moved it each time it blocked a landing task.

Sella returned at the end of the second day. She had gone downstream to inspect a rope supplier and came back with two coils, a list of faults, and no new rope. I thought that meant the trip had failed. Sella said accepting bad rope would have been the failure.

While she entered the missed crossings on her wall, I saw a list near the edge. It held names, cargo, and debts. One line named an orchard and two crates that had not been collected. When I asked which orchard, she said Bern's.

The name made the facts meet without my help.

I brought the fruit from the shaded corner and told her Bern had sent it to the ferry. Sella looked without taking it, checking the mesh, or asking about the mark. For several counts, she only looked. “Did he?”

I wanted to tell her everything Bern had said. That would have proved I had respected her privacy only until the moment information became interesting. Instead I explained the practical part: the fruit came from a shaded tree, a bird had marked the other one, and Bern had not named an owner.

Sella lifted the mesh and inspected the skin. The fruit was for the crossing table, she said. Bern sent one from the first picking, and it would be cut when both rates were present. When I said she knew him, her answer was shorter. “I did.”

She carried the fruit inside and placed it on a high rack. That was all the answer she offered.

The next morning, I asked about the crates. The two empty boxes had waited through three quick months. Sella had left tiles. Bern had not collected them.

I said Bern believed he came to the ford often. Sella was setting a new rope stop into the bank. The old rope had cut a groove in the post, and she worked the knot lower to spread the wear. He came for every turning, she said. When I asked how that could mean he had stopped, she pulled the knot hard. “Because he stopped.”

The rope held. Sella picked up her tools and went to the boat.

I had two statements. Bern saw his friend at every turning and believed they met regularly. Sella said Bern no longer came to see her. Neither looked like a lie. I had learned the cost of maintaining a lie on other worlds: attention had to guard it, answers had to bend around it, and ordinary facts became dangerous. Bern had spoken freely. Sella had spent no effort defending her words.

That left error.

Memory changes to fit today, Sella had told me. Her own wall said so. Eleven days earlier, I might have understood those words as a warning about myself. Now I used them to suspect that one of two people had remembered badly.

The question was measurable. The wall held crossings. The message frames held dates. My calendars held both rates. Turnings required marks on each bank and could not happen unnoticed.

I asked Sella whether I could examine her old records. She studied me long enough that I almost withdrew the request. “For the river?”

This was the place where I could have lied without speaking an untrue word. Bern and Sella's visits happened at the river. I could have said yes.

“For Bern,” I said. Sella looked toward the fruit on its rack. “Don't move anything. Don't copy names that aren't his.”

She opened the oldest panel on the wall.

I believed I was about to find which account had failed. Sella believed she had set a boundary. Only one of those beliefs was useful.

## Chapter 10: Eleven Days of Proof

Sella's wall was not one record. It was six years of them layered over boards.

The newest months remained open beside the door. Older sections folded on wooden hinges, and the earliest panels had been covered by later ones. Sella showed me how to release each catch without scraping the marks. Then she drew a line down a blank sheet and wrote Bern's name at the top.

“Only his entries,” she said.

I agreed. She did not ask me to promise. The condition was enough.

For eleven days I worked between wall, ford, and line.

On the first day, I copied every turning date connected to Bern. On the second, I checked ferry crossings on those dates. On the third, I compared the dates with the message tiles that had survived. Most tiles had been cleared years earlier, but the frame ledger still marked when full rows were emptied. No missing period matched a turning.

The fourth day went to conversion. I wrote the figures in counts, spans, quick days, long days, and then back again. Each path returned to its starting number. I made one error by carrying a remainder into the wrong column, found it, and began that page again.

Sella did not help unless I asked about a mark. Her symbols for flood, wind, and damaged cargo were obvious after explanation. Her symbol for a private passenger was a closed square. I skipped every one, even when it appeared beside a turning. A rule matters most when breaking it might give you a useful answer.

On the sixth day, quick Ford held a festival.

The market square filled before breakfast. Bakers hung bright cloth from their awnings, children painted stripes along the road, and somebody began a contest that involved shouting at a loaf. Sella said it was a small festival and never explained what the loaf had done.

I needed to check a marker on the long bank, so I crossed before the music began. From there, the whole celebration passed as a burst of color and a thin insect noise. Stalls opened and vanished. A crowd gathered, jumped, and scattered before the long Vellin beside me finished trimming one hedge.

She noticed me looking. “Ford is having one of its days.”

“Don't you want to go?”

She considered the bright movement across the river. “They'll have another.”

She returned to the hedge. The festival did not seem lost to her. It was part of what the far bank did, like smoke rising or weather moving over it. I had been in Vell long enough to expect sadness whenever one rate missed another. The Vellins kept refusing to arrange themselves around my expectation.

I crossed back after the music ended. Quick Ford smelled of warm bread and wet paint. Ruck found me near a table and pushed a festival ribbon toward me. I could not wear it, so Ruck tied it to the corner of my calendar and announced that this was better anyway.

That night I returned to Sella's wall. The seventh and eighth days were for Bern's ordinary crossings. There were fewer marks than I expected, but an unmarked crossing was possible. I spent the ninth day at the ferry, recording everyone who used it. I spent the tenth above the ford where a person might wade instead. Then I compared my counts with Sella's.

She had missed two waders in six years. I had missed three in one day.

“How?” I asked.

“One went behind you while you were writing down the other two.”

She had been tying a load at the time.

On the eleventh day, I spread my permitted pages across the boathouse floor. Rain tapped the roof. Sella stepped around the papers, completed two crossings, and entered the water level without touching my arrangement.

The work had become much larger than the disagreement that started it. I knew the exact line between Sella's record and my inference. I knew which gaps could hide a visit and which could not. I knew every mistake I had found in my own figures.

I still did not know which of them was wrong.

That should have stopped me. Instead, I decided the answer must be in the interval between the turnings, where neither person's words had yet been turned into the same unit.

## Chapter 11: The Empty Months

A turning was not a holiday or a date chosen by custom. It happened when the river moved.

Water pressed against one bank until the safe path across the ford no longer matched the markers. Then workers sounded the bottom, shifted the stakes, and called measurements across the current. Sella's wall held each event because a turning changed ferry work as well as the ford.

Bern's name appeared on every one.

I began with the most recent entry and moved backward. He had set the far marks. At the turning before that, he had helped replace two stakes. Before that, the note said only `Bern—north bank`, but the ferry count placed him there from first light until late afternoon. Thirteen turnings existed in the years since Sella began keeping the wall. Bern had attended thirteen.

I checked the older village ledger. It held no water levels, only names assigned to shared work. Bern appeared there too. A torn corner removed one date but not his mark; the clerk had repeated the workers on the next page when recording payment in food.

Sella had told me he stopped coming. Her own records gave me no missed turning to show her.

The interval was the answer, I thought. A turning came once each long season. For Bern, that placed one meeting about two of his months after the previous one. For Sella, almost ten months passed. I converted the interval six times because the result felt too large, though the number five had not changed.

Bern came at dawn. They worked the ford for a day. He returned to his orchard and experienced a season before he saw her again.

Sella finished that day, ran the ferry, repaired ropes, moved routes after rain, watched quickbread fail, attended festivals, and saw Ruck grow. Month followed month until most of a quick year had passed. Then Bern came down the hill and continued what, to him, had barely paused.

The same interval held two different amounts of ordinary life.

I took my pages outside and checked the rate at the ford again. A chip of bark crossed a measured section on the long side. I crossed, allowed the lurch to settle, and timed another chip below the line. The ratio remained exactly five. I repeated it above the road and near the lower posts. Five each time.

Ruck found me at the third place and asked what I was proving. I said I was checking whether the rate had changed. Ruck said it did not. When I pointed out that it might have changed before I arrived, Ruck pointed toward the numbered stones. “Then Tarr would fix it.”

I wrote that answer down and did not understand it yet.

Back at the wall, I looked for visits between turnings. Bern's orchard crates appeared occasionally. First fruit reached the crossing table each crop. Message tiles came and went. A visit might have escaped the ferry count if he waded, and a wading might have escaped Sella. I had proved that by missing three myself.

So I counted the gaps that could hide him. I compared market witnesses when their names lay inside the permitted notes. I checked weather that made the ford unsafe. I separated no evidence from evidence of no visit, because those are not the same thing.

The likely answer did not change. Bern sometimes passed near the line. He reliably came to the turnings. Nothing supported regular visits to Sella between them.

At sunset I returned the oldest panel to its catch. Sella was coiling the new rope she had finally accepted from a different maker. I said Bern had never missed a turning. She ran the rope through her fingers, checking for rough places. “I told you.” Almost ten months of her life passed between visits, I said. Her answer did not change. “I know.”

I waited for her to ask what I would do with that result. She did not. The access had been mine to request, and the answer was mine to carry.

For the first time, the two accounts no longer looked like a problem of memory. They looked like a problem with no empty space left for an error.

## Chapter 12: Nothing Missing

I checked four more times.

The first check used only Sella's water records. The second used the village work ledger. The third began with Bern's crop dates and worked toward the river instead of beginning at the ford. The fourth ignored every calculated interval and counted visible quick months between his marks.

Every path reached the same place.

I did not distrust the arithmetic. I distrusted an answer in which nobody had made the mistake. Problems are easier when one fact is wrong. You remove it, replace it, and the pieces fit. Here, Sella's record supported Bern. Bern's regular attendance supported Sella's absence. Each proof strengthened the statement that seemed to oppose it.

I tried to reduce Sella's words. Perhaps `he stopped` meant he came less often. Perhaps hurt had enlarged the gap when she spoke about it. I did not say this to her, which allowed me to feel less unfair while continuing to think it.

Then I laid out the months.

There was no enlargement. Sella had lived every marked day. She had made crossings, changed routes, rejected rope, kept water, watched markets open and close, and arrived at the next turning with nearly another year behind her. Calling that exaggeration would only mean I preferred Bern's shorter experience.

I was ashamed. The shame did not repair the thought. It only made it impossible to keep pretending the thought was neutral.

The slowfruit remained on the high rack. Sella turned it each morning and checked the bird-marked side through the mesh. She never cut it. Both rates were not yet gathered, and the fruit was meant for a shared table.

On the last evening of my audit, I returned every panel, stacked my permitted pages, and gave Sella the sheet with Bern's name. She checked that no other names had crossed onto it. Then she folded it once and handed it back. When I objected that the sheet concerned her, she tapped one column. “It is numbers about the river. The part about me isn't on there.”

That should have told me what the pages could not do. I put them with my calendars anyway.

Rain began after dark. I stayed under the boathouse roof while Sella drew the boat higher and doubled the upstream rope. The first drops darkened the landing boards. I asked whether the weather would delay the next turning.

“Turning isn't a meeting someone can move,” she said. “The river chooses it. We only read when it's ready.”

Bern had said he would speak to her then. Sella knew the date and had made no mention of him coming. Both were carrying the same approaching day without sharing what they expected from it.

I could take my result to either one first. Sella had given me the records, lived the long absence, and set the limits of my search. Bern had asked what he had missed.

I chose Bern.

At the time, I told myself the order was practical. His orchard was uphill and the rain would make the path worse. He had asked the question. He was the one who seemed not to understand.

The less flattering reason was that I thought he needed my answer and Sella did not. I could arrive at the orchard with pages, show him the interval, and watch a hidden mistake become visible. Sella had already looked at the same numbers without changing. There was less for me to accomplish with her.

Sella finished the second knot and came inside. I told her I was going to the orchard in the morning. She looked at the folded sheet and said she knew. I asked if she wanted me to carry a message, then whether she wanted me to stay silent. Sella set the wet rope end on a hook. “I gave you the records. I didn't give you either of us.”

I understood enough to stop asking. Not enough to stay.

In the morning, I took the pages up the long road. Behind me, the river ran at both rates through the rain. Ahead, Bern's orchard waited with its long trees and all the time he expected to have.

# Part III: The Wrong Answer

## Chapter 13: What the Orchard Keeps

Bern had forgotten I was coming.

He had invited me to return after the rain, named the morning, and left a tile at the lower path. Then the drain above the west trees clogged, and that problem replaced me. I found him at the far edge of the orchard lifting wet leaves from a stone channel.

I could have reached him at once. Instead, I waited near the oldest tree and watched water make its slow way downhill.

The trunk beside me was wider than six Vellins standing together. A metal tag had been set into the bark long ago, and the tree had grown around its corners. The mark belonged to Bern's grandmother. She had planted it for people she would not meet. Bern now cleared its channels, trained its branches, and gathered fruit from work begun before he was born.

On the quick bank, a farmer might fit twenty crops inside the wait for one here. I had treated that difference as a number. In the orchard it became trust stretched across people. Someone planted. Somebody else kept the channel open. A third person ate.

Bern arrived while I was reading the tag, mud covering both hands. He said he had named the morning. I pointed out that morning had not ended. He checked the sky before accepting this.

He remembered the arrangement then and brought me to a work table under a roof. I set down the folded pages. Bern washed mud from his fingers and tried to offer me pressing, which proved that remembering one arrangement had displaced another.

I gave him the result without preparing him. Bern had appeared at all thirteen recorded turnings. The interval that felt like one season to him filled nearly ten months for Sella. The records supported no regular visits between.

Bern listened. He did not interrupt, offer food, or look toward another tree. When I finished, he pressed one wet finger to the converted interval and made me confirm that it meant nearly ten months for Sella, every time.

He read the figures again. I expected surprise at the calculation. What I saw was slower and harder to name. His hand remained on the page after his eyes moved away.

“I know five,” he said. I told him I had checked it in three places, and he shook his head. “No. I mean I know. We learn it before we learn the road. Five quick years for one of ours. Five of their days. Five of everything.” He drew his finger down the figures. “I knew.”

The pages had not taught him the ratio. They had put Sella's ordinary months beside his ordinary season and refused to let one hide behind the other.

Bern carried the sheet to the edge of the roof, where rainwater still dripped from the last row of leaves. “I go down. She's there. We work. We argue about the far stake. I go home and prune one section, or mend a channel, or wait for the second fruit to set. Then I return. It has not felt like leaving her alone.”

I said it had felt like being left to Sella. Bern agreed without defending the difference. He could see it on the page. I reminded him that the page was not where she had lived it.

Bern looked at me then. I had repeated Sella's correction as if I had always understood it. We both knew I had arrived carrying numbers.

He folded the sheet along Sella's crease and gave it back. He asked whether she had sent me and whether she knew I had come. I answered no to the first and yes to the second.

Bern waited for more. There was none that helped me.

The drain above the orchard overflowed again. Water left the stone channel and began cutting a narrow path through the soil. Bern saw it first. He took a shovel and went uphill. The conversation did not end; work had become urgent, and the orchard did not agree to wait because a friendship had become painful.

I followed. Bern cleared the blockage while I tracked where the loose water went. We moved one support before the soil softened beneath it. The tree held.

When the channel ran clean again, Bern set the shovel against a wall. He said he had planned to ask Sella what he had done. He would still ask at the turning, but not in that form.

The difference sounded small. It was the first thing my figures had changed, and it belonged to him rather than to me.

## Chapter 14: What Was Not on the Page

Sella was fitting a new pin through the ferry's rowlock when I returned.

The old pin lay on the landing beside her. One side had worn flat from years of pressure. Sella had measured the opening, cut the replacement, and was removing a rough place with a small file. She did not ask how the orchard visit had gone.

I waited until she tested the fit. The pin entered halfway and stopped. I told her Bern knew. Sella withdrew the pin and filed the same edge. “He knew before you went.”

I explained that he knew the ratio but had never placed her months beside his season. I said he understood now that the interval had not felt the same to her. I said he planned to speak differently at the turning.

The pin went in farther on the second test. Sella marked the place where it caught. When I asked whether she already knew the interval and his perfect attendance, she reminded me that she could multiply by five and had written the dates I copied.

She was not angry in any way I could point to. Her hands stayed exact. The file kept its rhythm. That did not mean she was calm. It meant I had no right to claim a feeling she had not given me.

I asked what she thought I had been finding. Sella set down the file. “You asked for Bern's entries. I thought you wanted Bern's entries.”

That answer left me with my own assumption. She had not asked me to settle anything. Bern had not asked for an investigation. I had turned two accounts into a problem, requested access to solve it, and mistaken access for a place inside their friendship.

I returned to the statement that had started my work: he had stopped coming, yet he attended every turning. “For the river,” Sella said, picking up the pin again. “He came because we said we would do the work. He never failed the work.”

She tested the rowlock. This time the pin seated cleanly. She rotated it once, pulled it free, and brushed away a curl of metal.

“Between turnings,” she said, “I lived most of a year. I took the ferry out in winter water. I lost a landing in a flood and rebuilt it. Ruck learned to talk faster, though nobody thought that was possible. People left. People came back. Then Bern arrived and asked what was new.”

I had written some of those things into my evidence. Weather. crossing totals. replaced landing. They had been proof of elapsed time. For Sella, they were the time.

I asked why she had not told him. “Tell him what part?” She placed the new pin beside the worn one. “That he kept coming? He knows. That I waited? He knows five. That I was glad when he came? I was. That by the time he arrived I had already stopped expecting him that day? That was also true.”

No single sentence would have made the rest false.

Sella lifted the rowlock into the boat and fixed it to the side. I followed over the landing boards, carrying the new pin because she pointed at it and I could. The task gave me somewhere to put my attention.

I admitted that I had first expected one of their accounts to be false, then expected conversion to explain the difference. Sella's answer was exact. “It explained the conversion.”

She did not make the answer kinder. She did not need to.

We set the pin. Sella pulled the oar twice under load and listened for the scrape. There was none. She removed the old pin from the landing and carried it to a box of worn parts.

“I am sorry I treated your year like a missing number,” I said.

Sella closed the box. “You treated it like something that needed proving.”

The correction belonged inside the apology, not after it, so I said it again. “I am sorry I made you prove time you had already lived.”

She did not forgive me. She handed me the file and asked me to return it to the shelf.

I did that. It was the only useful thing I had left to offer.

## Chapter 15: Children at the Ford

Sella told me about the promise that evening after the ferry stopped.

I did not ask again. I was sweeping metal dust from the shelf with a scrap of cloth while she checked the new rowlock in the last light. The boat sat half in water, held by both lines. Sella gave the oar one final pull and began speaking as if continuing a conversation I had not known we were having.

She had been three when she met Bern. Bern had been twenty.

Those ages look wrong beside each other until you put them back into Vell. Three quick years and twenty long years placed them at about the same stage of childhood. They were small enough to be underfoot and old enough to believe that helping made them workers.

The ford had been their meeting place. Sella came with a family member who worked the ferry then. Bern came down from the orchard with whoever kept the far markers. They could not hold an ordinary conversation across the line, so they met at the board, carried stakes, fetched stones, and learned each other's timing through jobs.

One summer the river cut into the quick bank and shifted the safe channel. Adults reset the ford. The children were allowed to carry light markers and repeat numbers. Sella remembered the mud reaching her knees. Bern remembered dropping a plumb line and having to pull it from reeds. At the end of the day, both believed they had saved the crossing. When I asked whether they actually had, Sella said, “No. We carried two stakes and lost a line.”

That had not mattered to them. The turning became their work. They promised they would both come whenever the river required it, regardless of what else filled their lives.

It was the kind of promise children make because they mean it completely and know almost nothing about the size of `always`.

Bern kept it. So did Sella.

When Sella was young, the turnings arrived far apart but still belonged to the shape of growing. Bern would come down, they would work, and each would be older. As she reached adulthood on the quick side, their ages stopped moving together. Bern remained a young orchard keeper. Sella built a trade, kept a house, and took the ferry as her own.

The promise stayed the same because changing it felt like breaking it. Sella counted eleven of her years since the friendship narrowed to turnings.

Eleven quick years. A little more than two for Bern. Thirteen days of work held the whole friendship in place.

Sella sat on the side of the boat with the worn pin in one hand. “If I said he failed me, I would be lying. He came when the river turned. He was early when he could be, and he stayed until the far marks held. Ask anyone. They will tell you Bern keeps his word.” The promise had still become all they did.

The distinction mattered. Bern had not broken the promise. Sella did not want me to turn his faithfulness into neglect. Yet the promise had taken a friendship made of daily shared work and reduced it to one perfect duty repeated across widening gaps.

I asked whether she had ever told him she missed him. Sella rolled the worn pin along the wood. Once, she said. After a particularly long interval, Bern answered that he had missed her too, and she knew he meant it.

He had missed her for about two months. She had missed him through most of a year. Neither feeling was false. Hearing his did not return the months or tell her how to place them beside his.

She had told him she was glad he came. When I asked whether that was true, Sella looked directly at me. “Yes.”

I had asked as if pain could make the gladness untrue, or gladness could erase the pain. I was still trying to make one answer rule the other.

We put the worn pin in the parts box. Sella closed the boathouse shutters and checked the fruit on its rack. Outside, the two currents made different sounds along the same bank.

The turning would come soon. I knew enough by then to ask whose soon, but the river did not use either one.

## Chapter 16: Ruck's New Work

Ruck had become taller while I was counting Bern's visits.

I noticed because Ruck no longer passed under the message-frame bar without ducking. The first time we met, there had been space above the upright fringe. Now the fringe caught the wood and folded sideways. When I pointed out the growth, Ruck ducked back through to repeat it and said everyone had noticed.

Four quick months had passed since my arrival. I had marked each one. Ruck had moved from the restless end of childhood into the beginning of work, acquired a new way of shaping the forearm fringe, and formed opinions about which market tasks deserved respect.

The new work was carrying notices between quick Ford and the lower farms. Ruck knew every path and could make the round before an older messenger finished arranging the tiles. Listening remained a problem. Ruck delivered the right tile to the wrong field twice in the first week and argued that both fields were close.

I asked whether growing that much felt fast. Ruck stopped sorting notices. “Fast beside what?”

I had comparisons ready. A long child. Bern. The fruit still waiting on the rack. None answered the question Ruck had asked. Ruck had lived every day at the only rate that belonged to Ruck. Growing had taken exactly as long as growing took.

At last I offered myself as the comparison. Ruck accepted this and returned to sorting. I had not grown at all that either of us could detect. Ruck decided that must feel slow.

I could not improve on the conclusion.

That afternoon Sella took me to a first-gathering for a family near the market. Their child was three days old and already pulling herself across a blanket. Forty relatives, neighbors, and coworkers filled two rooms. The child moved from arm to arm while food vanished at quick-party speed.

Half the family lived across the line. None could attend on two hours of final notice, though preparations had been discussed for a long season. Their message tiles stood in a small rack beside the child.

An aunt read them aloud. The first long relatives wished the child steady hands. Another promised a visit before the next crop. A tile from Herrel repeated whatever Herrel had written at the previous first-gathering, and the whole room laughed before the aunt reached the final mark.

I expected the missing relatives to hang over the party. They did not. Their words were present in the form the family had learned to carry them. People answered the tiles aloud even though no answer would cross the line with sound. Later, the aunt would mark replies and take them to the post.

When the child had gone to sleep, I asked the aunt whether the gathering felt incomplete. She looked around the packed room. “No. It feels crowded.”

I meant the relatives. She understood after I pointed to the rack. “They know her,” the aunt said. “They'll know her slowly.”

The child would become Ruck's age before some long relatives experienced two years. They would meet every stage at wide intervals. To them, she might seem to change between visits. To her, they might appear nearly fixed.

No one had chosen a wrong way to belong to the family.

Ruck arrived late because three message tiles had been delivered correctly and one had gone to a mill. The baby woke. Ruck held her with great seriousness for about half a count, then handed her back at the first unhappy noise.

“Work is harder than growing,” Ruck told me.

The statement was not supported by the earlier sorting, but it felt true to Ruck and required no correction from me.

When we left, the long relatives' tiles remained beside the empty blanket. The party had ended. Their visit had not begun yet. Both facts belonged to the same family, and this time I did not need one to cancel the other.

## Chapter 17: Music Nobody Shared

Music happened at the line three evenings later.

No one had planned a joint performance. A quick group brought small stringed instruments to the bank after market. A long musician arrived across the river with a frame larger than Ruck and began sounding one deep string. Each side noticed the other and decided to continue.

Quick music fit more notes into one count than I could follow. The players answered one another, broke a pattern, and found it again before the long musician's first tone ended. From our bank, his part was a low sound that changed so slowly I could not tell whether it had changed at all.

On the long bank, the quick group must have reached him as a bright buzzing. A whole tune crossed during one movement of his hand.

They played at one another anyway.

The quick players stopped after their final run and waited. The long note continued. Some people went home. Others brought stools. Sella checked the ferry line, moved the lantern, and returned before the long musician reached the end.

When he finished, the quick bank applauded. The long musician bowed. Then the quick group played one more piece because, from his side, he had barely received the first.

I asked Sella whether both sides had heard music and whether it had been the same. Yes to the first, no to the second. When I asked what they had done together, Sella looked across the river. “Staying.”

That answer followed me after the instruments were packed away. Neither rate pretended the other sound matched its own. They did not combine the notes into a hidden correct song. They remained until both sides had finished.

The slowfruit on Sella's rack was ready to cut. More workers began leaving tools at the ford. A fresh plumb line appeared in the boathouse, and Sella sharpened new marker stakes. The turning would begin in fourteen quick days if the water kept its present course.

Bern and Sella knew. Neither had asked me to carry anything between them.

I stayed out of their messages. That was not the same as staying out of the problem. At night I spread my calendars beneath the boathouse window and tried to find a form of contact that did not require one of them to live at the other's rate.

The answer was not one visit per long season. It was six.

Six visits placed Bern at the ford about every six or seven quick weeks. For him, each interval would be short enough to fit among orchard tasks. For Sella, it would be long enough not to take over the ferry schedule. The visits could be fixed in counts rather than described as soon. Both would know every date.

I tested the schedule against floods, harvest, and the recorded turnings. It survived all three. I moved one visit to avoid the first picking and another to clear the quick winter rise. I wrote both columns correctly on the first attempt.

The plan did not require either person to experience the other's rate. It only required them to use the arithmetic they already knew.

I thought of the musicians and told myself the schedule was the same kind of bridge. Each side would do something slightly difficult. Neither would be asked to pretend. They would meet at a form both could keep.

That comparison should have warned me. The musicians had chosen to stay. Nobody had assigned them six performances and called the result music.

In the morning, I copied the plan onto one clean sheet. I put Sella's name on one side and Bern's on the other, then stopped.

The last time I had made a page about them, Sella had told me exactly what the page lacked. I knew that. I also knew the plan would work.

I carried it uphill to Bern first.

## Chapter 18: Six Visits

Bern accepted before I finished explaining.

He was sorting cut branches into three piles: sound wood, damaged wood, and pieces thin enough for tying supports. I placed the schedule on the table. Before I reached the second adjusted date, he put down the branch in his hand and said yes to six visits. I told him to check the orchard dates for himself. He trusted my check. When I pointed out that trust did not mean he wanted the dates, Bern glanced over the columns. “I want to see her. If this is what she needs, of course I will do it.”

His answer pleased me. I had expected resistance from the long side: harvest work, distance, the inconvenience of fitting six trips into what had been one. Instead, he treated the plan as a small correction. I felt the bright relief of a machine starting after you fit the right part, although I have never been a machine and do not know whether they feel relief.

Bern asked why Sella had not requested it. I should have heard the problem. I heard gratitude for my solution.

He wanted to go to the ford immediately. The schedule's first date was after the turning, but Bern said there was no reason to wait. I persuaded him to let me take the page to Sella before he arrived with an agreement she had not seen. This was a limited improvement in my behavior. I still took the page.

Sella was laying marker stakes in order of length. She measured each against the old set and shaved one that stood too high. I waited until she finished the cut, then set the schedule on an empty crate and told her Bern had agreed. She did not read it. “To what?”

I explained six visits, fixed in counts, cleared around first picking, floods, winter rise, and the turning. I showed her that the longest quick interval was seven weeks. Bern had agreed. He wanted to begin before the first marked date.

“No,” Sella said. I thought she meant one date and began explaining the orchard conflict. She put the stake down. “No to the page.” When I said it solved the gap, she said it only divided the gap. Bern wanted to come, I insisted. “Bern wants to agree.”

I said those were the same. Sella looked at the six entries, finally, and then at me.

“You decided how much of him I need. You asked him whether he would provide it. Now you are asking me to be grateful that the amount is available.” She slid the page back across the crate. “That is cargo, Dot.”

The ferry had schedules for cargo. A crate appeared on a list, moved at a stated count, and arrived because both sides had agreed to the delivery. Cargo did not choose to pass because it had thought of somebody at the river.

I repeated that Bern wanted to see her. Sella said he could come and see her. When I asked how often, she picked up the stake. “That is for us to learn.”

There it was: the space my schedule could not tolerate. No promised number. No proof in advance. A friendship that might ask too much of Bern or leave Sella waiting again, with no two-column page to prevent either.

I took the schedule away. Sella returned to her stakes. She did not tear the page or tell me to destroy it. Refusing it was enough.

At the message frames, I found a fresh tile from Bern addressed to Sella. The mark held only a few words. I did not read them. I placed the page behind my calendars and left the tile where her hand would find it.

That night I looked at six correct dates and understood that knowing what would work gave me no right to make anyone live it.

How much waiting could friendship fairly require? My plan offered a number. Sella had refused the question inside it.

The page remained correct. That was the worst part. I had made the wrong answer without making a single error.

# Part IV: What the Line Holds

## Chapter 19: Work I Was Given

I folded the schedule until the columns disappeared and put it beneath my calendars.

Destroying it would have been easier. Then I could have treated the page as the mistake and myself as the person who removed it. The dates were not the mistake. I had been. Keeping the page made that harder to tidy away.

The next morning I stayed clear of Sella's landing until she called me over.

The turning tools had to be checked. The new stakes were straight, but the painted depth bands on three old poles had worn thin. Sella gave me a cord with five marked lengths and asked me to hold it against each pole while she repainted the bands.

It was work she chose to share. I did not make it larger than that.

We began with the longest pole. I kept the first mark level with its end. Sella painted, turned the pole, and painted the other side. The smell of the dark coating stayed close under the shed roof. She did not mention Bern's tile or my schedule.

At midday she went to the message frame. I was near enough to see her remove Bern's tile and no nearer. She read it with her back to the road. Then she put it into a pocket rather than leaving it in the used-tile box.

I wanted to know whether that meant hope, anger, caution, or nothing. Wanting did not make any of those mine to choose.

Sella returned to the poles. “The third band is low.”

I checked my cord. She was right by less than the width of one paint line. We cleaned it and began again.

That afternoon a quick farmer waited at the landing for a long buyer who had promised to arrive `before market`. The farmer had understood that the phrase belonged to the long market and still began waiting after quick breakfast. By midday he had reorganized his baskets twice. By afternoon he sold half the load to somebody else.

The buyer arrived as the quick market was closing. Neither accused the other of lying. They argued about which market the words had named and how much fruit remained. The ferry took them across with four baskets instead of eight.

Their waiting had a price that could be counted. Four baskets had been sold elsewhere. The friendship question did not become easier because this one did.

I had asked how much waiting could be fair. Now I tried to leave the question open. Fair to whom? Measured in which life? Requested for what reason? Chosen again after how many years? The more exact the question became, the less one number could answer it.

Look at that carefully, because it was difficult for me. I was not learning that numbers were useless. The farmer needed the basket count. Sella needed exact bands on the poles. The ferry route depended on depth, not feeling. I was learning that a correct measure can stop at the edge of what it owns.

On the second day, Sella found a crack in one pole below the third band. The damage had hidden under old coating. We bent the pole gently and watched the crack open.

“Discard it?” I asked.

“Cut it down.”

The lower section remained sound. Sella removed the split length, capped the new end, and turned a long depth pole into a short one. Nothing was repaired by pretending it could still do the old work. It became useful after its limits were accepted.

I noticed the comparison. I did not tell Sella. She had asked for painted bands, not a lesson about her promise.

Ruck arrived with notices and tried to leave them in the wrong frame. Sella redirected Ruck with one finger. Ruck changed direction without losing speed, caught a heel on the stacked poles, and landed in the grass. No pole moved because I was holding the cord around them.

“I saved those,” Ruck said from the ground.

“You nearly broke those,” Sella answered.

Both claims contained observable evidence. I did not investigate.

By evening, every usable pole had clean marks. The shortened one lay in the proper stack. Sella entered its new length on the tool board and gave me the marked cord to coil.

I coiled it without turning the work into permission for anything else.

The turning was now four quick days away. Bern's tile remained in Sella's pocket. My schedule remained beneath my calendars. Three objects carried three different kinds of waiting, and I left them separate.

## Chapter 20: Below the Moss

Tarr found me on the hill because I was occupying her hole.

I had followed the numbered posts above the ford to check whether their spacing changed on steeper ground. One post stood beside a fresh opening in the earth. The post was already out. I hovered over the hole to see how deep it went, and a voice behind me said, “Move.”

Tarr was 180 years old. She had a barrow, two levers, a wooden frame, a mallet, and no interest in why a floating stranger was staring into her work.

I moved.

The post lay in the grass beyond the barrow. Moss covered its upper face. Below the old soil line, the stone continued clean and pale for more than twice the visible height. I had crossed beside these posts for months and had thought of them as waist-high markers. Most of each one had been hidden.

Tarr cleaned the bottom with a narrow tool. The hole was not vertical. It leaned slightly toward the quick side. She lowered the wooden frame over it, sighted through two notches toward the neighboring posts, and adjusted one foot of the frame. When I asked what had moved, she said the bank. I argued that the post had looked straight. “From which direction?”

I checked. From below, it had. Along the line, its top sat a little out of place.

Tarr put a lever through a rope sling and began guiding the stone upright. I held the free side of the sling when she told me to. The post descended slowly, scraping damp earth. Its weight pulled against me in a way I could feel without having weight of my own. Tarr stopped twice to clear packed soil.

When the moss line reached ground level, she sighted through the frame again. Then she lifted the stone half a hand and turned it. I said its first position had been close. Tarr pointed along the hill. “Close moves the next one. Then that one moves the next. In ninety years somebody has six stones disagreeing and no first mistake.”

The posts stood forty paces apart. I had estimated that after Ruck showed me the line. Tarr did not estimate. She laid a measured cord between this post and both neighbors, corrected the angle, packed stone fragments around the base, and measured again.

I asked whether the stones were only markers. Tarr kept packing. “Only?”

I explained the river, the fixed change, and the way the post row made the invisible boundary visible in fields. Tarr listened until she understood which mistake I was making.

“Markers tell you where a thing is,” she said. “These tell it where to stay.”

She put one hand on the clean stone below the moss line. The material came from the head of the valley, not this hill. The buried length, the forty-pace spacing, and the alignment held the edge at five to one.

I thought of Ruck pointing toward the posts when I asked if the ratio could have changed. When I asked what happened after a post came out, Tarr set another packing stone. “The edge loosens. Not at once. It wanders near the gap. Clocks disagree. Water changes in the wrong place.”

It had happened at the north end when Tarr was young. A flood took a post and hid the loss through a season. By the time linekeepers found the gap, the ratio nearby had slipped somewhere between four and five. Meetings failed. Farm timing broke. The river changed pace across a wide uncertain strip.

They had fixed it by returning the post to its old bed.

The line returned slowly after that. Two years passed before every test gave five again. The keepers had not forced the ratio with a machine or calculation. They restored the missing piece and maintained the row while the edge settled.

I asked whether setting a post closer could produce a ratio of four. Tarr stopped packing. She could ruin the existing line that way. She had no idea whether she could create another stable ratio. “I know where this stone belongs.”

No living linekeeper knew how to begin a boundary, choose its number, or move it safely. They knew how to recognize the old beds, use the right stone, keep the distances exact, and restore what weather disturbed.

Tarr finished the packing and struck the earth flat with the mallet. From below, the post looked as it had before. Along the line, every top agreed.

I had spent months thinking the posts described a strange fact of Vell. Tarr had just shown me the hidden length of one.

The five-to-one world was being maintained.

## Chapter 21: A Thing Someone Set

I followed Tarr to the next post.

She did not invite me. She also did not tell me to leave, which was the most permission Tarr offered. The next stone had not moved enough to lift. She exposed its soil line, measured both gaps, struck it twice from the long side, and measured again.

I stayed where she could see me and tested without touching anything.

At the post she had reset, the lurch passed through the center of the stone. Half a body-width uphill, it still did. Near the next post, the boundary remained narrow enough that I could cross it in one movement. Between them, the invisible strip bowed slightly toward the quick side before returning to the line. Tarr said the ground had moved during the last wet season. The bow would straighten if the posts held.

I marked the position with loose sticks placed well away from the edge. Tarr watched me do that and moved one stick farther from her work. I understood the correction.

The boundary was not a wall laid under the soil. The posts did not join below ground. I checked the space between two old beds where erosion had exposed layers of earth. Nothing connected them that I could see. Stone, spacing, depth, and placement were enough to give the edge a path.

When I asked who had placed the first stones, Tarr answered, “Someone who knew how.” It had happened before her teacher's teacher.

Her teacher had received a set of working steps, and Tarr received them later. Results kept the steps honest. A fallen stone restored to its recorded socket caused the nearby rate to ease toward five again. A missing or crooked stone made the edge spread and drift. The caretakers still possessed a reliable practice even though its first explanation was gone.

Tarr gave me the end of her forty-pace cord while she moved the barrow. I held the mark against the post until she reached the next. The distance did not vary for a hill, ditch, road, or river. Where the ground fell away, the measured path followed its surface. Where the river interrupted it, the linekeepers sighted between banks.

I called after her to ask why the ratio was five. She did not answer until she returned. “Because that is the line we have.”

It was not a reason. It was the limit of the evidence.

Five crossings had brought me from one world to the next. Each began in a black place with one bright line and an opening shape I still could not explain. I had treated that passage as something that happened, the way Vellins treated the rate edge until Tarr showed me the buried stone.

I had never asked whether my own crossing had been set by someone.

The thought made me rise too quickly. The cord lifted from the post. Tarr stopped walking and pointed out the movement. I returned the mark to the same cut in the moss and apologized.

She checked the distance herself and then took the cord from me. I had turned her exact work into support for my question and nearly introduced an error while doing it. The lesson was familiar enough to be embarrassing.

I helped by moving the barrow only when told. At the hilltop, Tarr opened a covered box built into the ground. Inside lay replacement number plates, spare cord, small tools, and a map made from carved boards. The post row crossed the whole valley. Old repair dates sat beside individual numbers.

No mark named a maker. No instruction described how to choose five. Nothing pointed beyond the last post. I asked to copy the map so I could learn where the line began. “Map won't tell you,” Tarr said.

Tarr closed the lid. She did not refuse because the map was secret. She refused the purpose I had attached to it. A record of where posts stood could not answer who first set them.

We reached the valley ridge near sunset. The line continued down the other side until trees hid it. From that height I could see both Fords, the slow upper river, the fast lower reach, the orchard, and Sella's landing. Every life I knew there had been shaped by a number chosen before anyone remembered choosing.

That did not make their lives unreal. It made the number a decision whose maker was gone.

Tarr began downhill without waiting. I looked once more along the even stones, then followed. The turning was two quick days away, and there was work in the valley that belonged to the people still there.

## Chapter 22: First Light at the Ford

The river chose the following morning.

Sella had expected one more day. Before sunrise she found the current striking the quick bank below the lowest marker instead of above it. She checked twice, entered the change on the wall, and sent Ruck with six notices. Ruck asked whether this counted as important work. Sella handed over two more notices, which answered the question.

By first light the ford held tools, rope, stakes, food, and people from both rates. The young quick who had brought the new plumb line uncoiled it on a dry cloth. Two long workers carried old far-bank markers down the slope. Tarr stood at the hill post above them, checking that the permanent edge had not moved with the river.

The turning was river work, not line work. The rate boundary stayed where the posts held it. The ford's safe path crossed that boundary at an angle, and the water had shifted the path. Everyone needed to know where a foot, ferry, pole, or wheel could pass now.

Sella laid the repainted depth poles beside the landing. The shortened one fit the first shallow section exactly. She glanced at me after setting it down. I kept the comparison to myself again.

Bern arrived while the sunlight was still below the long ridge.

From the quick bank, his walk down the far road seemed almost motionless. Sella saw him before I did. She did not stop assigning tools, but the next stake went into the wrong pile. She moved it before anyone else noticed.

Bern carried the old plumb weight from their childhood work. The line attached to it was new. He reached the far table, set both down, and raised one hand toward Sella.

She raised hers.

Nothing in that greeting told me what either felt. It told me that they had seen each other.

The slowfruit came out before the sounding began. Sella placed it on the crossing table with the mesh still around the bird-marked side. A long worker on the other half of the table passed a knife through the boundary. Sella cut from the quick side while the worker held the fruit steady on long ground.

Its smell opened across the bank. Market sellers stopped moving. Ruck arrived with empty hands and claimed all eight notices had reached the correct people. Sella gave Ruck a slice before checking that claim, which may have been trust or may have been the only way to keep Ruck away from the knife.

Bern received a piece on the far side. He held it up toward Sella before eating. She took a piece from the same cut and did the same. The custom required no words, which was useful because ordinary speech could not cross fast enough.

I could not eat. Sella placed a piece on a clean leaf near me anyway so I could smell it. The gesture did not settle anything between us. It was still kind.

Work began at the upper marker. Sella stepped into the quick water with the longest pole. A safety rope ran from her waist to two people on the bank. Bern and another long worker took the far stakes. Between them, the plumb line crossed the boundary.

Sella lowered the pole until it touched stone, read the wet band, and called the depth in a long, shaped sound. She held each number so it would arrive across the line as speech rather than a chirp. Bern repeated the number with one hand, set a marker, and looked up.

The first reading was too deep for carts. The second could take a person but not the ferry. The third found the old route, but loose stones had collected there during the shift. Sella moved downstream one pace and read again.

Every body had a place. Quick workers moved stakes and fetched tools. Long workers fixed the far marks and waited through bursts of activity. Tarr watched the permanent posts. I held the spare safety rope above the fast current so it did not snag on a marker.

Bern and Sella did not speak about the tile, the schedule, the orchard, or the years. They worked.

That could have been avoidance. It was also the job in front of them. I no longer believed I could choose between those meanings from where I stood.

Near midday, the safe line had advanced halfway across. Sella called a depth, then waited without checking the count. Bern finished seating his stake, raised his head, and signaled. She moved at once.

No one had taught them that pause. The other workers used count calls. Sella and Bern carried the timing in their bodies from thirteen earlier turnings and the childhood work before those.

The promise had become too small for the friendship and too large for their lives. The skill inside it was still real.

They continued toward the center of the river, one exact measure at a time.

## Chapter 23: The Pause They Knew

The center of the ford was the difficult part.

The old route crossed the rate boundary there. Water struck the sounding pole at two speeds, twisting it toward the quick bank. Sella could not stand in the safest place because the deepest channel now occupied it. Bern could not hear a quick warning unless she shaped the sound for his rate.

They changed the method without discussing it.

Sella passed the pole to the young quick on the safety line and moved upstream. Bern shifted the far plumb weight two paces toward her. She watched the line settle, pointed once, and waited. Bern adjusted until the weight hung over the place she had indicated. The other workers looked to Sella for counts. She looked to Bern. He raised two fingers, and she began.

The call crossed low and slow. Bern marked the number, tested the bottom, and signaled that the loose stones continued through the middle. A cart could not take the old path. They would have to bend the route upstream, which meant moving every near stake set that morning. Nobody complained. The river did not owe them the work they had already done.

Sella came out of the water, accepted a dry cloth, and drew the new angle on a board. Bern watched from the far side. She placed three small stones for the near route and one for the deep channel. He moved his own pieces to mirror hers.

From my position, their gestures did not happen together. Sella's hands made the whole plan before Bern seemed to begin. Yet each waited at the places where the other needed room. They were not matching speed. They were matching attention.

We pulled the first near stake. The hole filled with brown water. Ruck carried the stake upstream, heard half an instruction, and planted it at the wrong stone. Sella called Ruck back. Bern waited with his far stake lifted until the quick side corrected itself.

I expected impatience from somebody. The work had already lasted most of a quick day and only part of a long morning. The quick workers wanted the ford open before dark. The long workers had spent less of themselves but moved too slowly to recover time on our bank.

Sella shortened the task. Pedestrian stakes would be completed first. Cart marks could wait until morning. The ferry could use its own sounded route. That choice reopened the crossing without pretending every job would finish today.

Bern signaled agreement and reset the far pedestrian line. He did not offer to remain until carts were complete. For him, morning was near. For Sella, another quick day would pass before he returned to the work. The old promise might have required both of them to stay anyway. Neither mentioned it.

We reached the far shallows in late afternoon. Sella called the final depth. Bern set the last pedestrian marker and held both hands above his head. The workers on our bank cheered. Their sound crossed as a bright burst. Long workers answered with a cheer that rolled back over the water after ours had ended.

The first traveler tested the new path with a staff. Sella watched every step. At the bent center, he paused, shifted upstream, and continued. The route worked. Only then did she leave the water.

Sella dried the marked pole and entered each reading on a temporary board. Bern sat on the far bank beside the plumb weight. The other workers began stacking tools and dividing tomorrow's jobs.

After every third entry, Sella paused.

There was no practical reason now. Bern was no longer setting stakes. The old timing continued because her body expected him to look up.

On the third pause, he did.

Bern said her name in the stretched way a long voice crossed to us. Sella waited for the rest.

He did not ask what he had done. He said he could see she had been ending their conversations before he did, and asked what he had failed to see.

Sella put down the marking tool.

Around us, workers kept packing rope, wiping poles, and setting aside the cart stakes. Tarr passed above on the hill. Ruck carried a bundle toward the wrong shed and was redirected again. Ordinary work did not stop to give them privacy, but nobody came closer.

I was holding the temporary board. I set it beside Sella and moved far enough away that I could not be mistaken for part of the answer.

She looked across the water at Bern.

“It has been eleven years for me,” she said.

## Chapter 24: The Promise Lets Go

Sella spoke toward the far bank in the slow calling voice she used for depths.

She told Bern what eleven years contained. Not every event. A life cannot be passed across a river as a complete list, and listing more would not make him present for it. She named the shape instead: the long stretch after a turning, the months in which the promise said they were still friends but gave them nothing to do with the friendship, and the arrival of another workday that asked her to act as if the previous one had just ended.

Bern listened from beside the plumb weight. His face changed too slowly for me to read. I watched his actions instead. He lowered his hands to his knees. He did not reach for a tool or interrupt.

Sella said she had begun finishing their conversations early because she already knew how they ended. Bern would ask how she had been. She would choose a few pieces small enough to tell during the turning. He would mean to hear more later. His later and hers would separate as soon as he climbed the bank.

“I kept waiting for you to fail,” she said. “Then I would have had something I could point to.” Bern's reply crossed low over the water: he had not failed. Sella agreed.

He had arrived at every turning. He had carried the old plumb weight, set the far line, stayed through weather, and done what the promise required. Sella had matched him. Neither could accuse the other without changing the facts.

Bern picked up the weight and turned it once in both hands. He could not keep the turning promise through the rest of Sella's life. She said she knew. Neither had been capable of knowing that when they were children.

They had been children with two stakes and a lost line. They had promised from the largest understanding available to them. Keeping the words had carried them beyond what those children could have imagined and into a life where the words no longer protected what they meant.

No one said sorry.

I expected it at first. Bern might have apologized for the missing months. Sella might have apologized for going short with him. Either apology would have made one person the wrongdoer and the other the judge. They had both kept the agreement. They were deciding whether it still owned them.

Bern said they should stop. Sella agreed, after today. He looked at the unfinished cart markers stacked beside him and pointed out that his working day had not finished.

For Sella, another quick day stood between the pedestrian route and tomorrow's cart work. For Bern, both belonged to one long working day. The difference almost pulled the promise back around them.

Sella considered the tool stacks. He could finish the far cart marks if he wanted the work, she said, but he owed her no turning. Bern rested the weight on the ground. “I want to finish this one.”

The distinction was small enough to fit inside a sentence. It changed the whole next day.

They sat on opposite banks while the remaining workers left. That is the closest the line allowed without one of them entering the other's rate. The fast water moved beside Sella. The slow reach carried Bern's reflection almost without disturbing it.

I took the cleaned poles into the boathouse. Sella's temporary board remained where I had set it. The schedule I wrote was hidden behind my calendars inside. I did not bring it out. Six visits had no claim on what happened next.

Ruck returned after dark to report that one notice had reached the wrong mill but the right person had found it. Sella told Ruck the morning crew would change. Bern would finish his side by choice. The quick bank needed someone else beside her for the near cart marks.

The young quick who owned the new plumb line was still packing rope. Sella asked if he wanted the work. He said yes before she finished, which reminded me of Bern and made me uneasy.

Then he asked what time, who would hold the safety rope, and whether he could check the board first. His yes belonged to a job he had chosen and understood.

Sella gave him the temporary readings.

Across the river, Bern lifted the old weight and started up the long path. He stopped once and turned. Sella was checking tomorrow's pole stack. She looked up at the same moment.

Neither raised a hand this time. They had already spoken.

The promise ended with unfinished cart marks, a new worker, and two people leaving the ford on different schedules. It did not tell them what their friendship would become. For once, nothing had been put in that empty place.

# Part V: A Keepable Thing

## Chapter 25: The Next Crew

The cart route took another quick day to finish.

Bern returned according to his own same afternoon. He set the far marks because he had chosen to finish this turning. On the near bank, Sella worked with the young quick who owned the plumb line. I never learned his name, which matters here. He did not become important because he replaced Bern in a friendship. He became useful because he wanted river work and learned fast.

Sella gave him the temporary board. He checked the depths before entering the water and found one figure he could not read. She showed him the mark, then made him call it back without looking. On the first crossing he set the safety rope too low. Sella corrected it. On the second, he saw the problem himself.

Bern watched from the far side. He offered no advice across the line.

By midday the cart route bent upstream through the center and returned to the old far landing. Two loaded wagons tested it one at a time. The first wheel slipped beside a loose stone, so workers moved the near stake half a pace and tested again. The second wagon crossed cleanly.

Sella entered the final route on her wall. Beside Bern's name, she added the young quick's work mark. No one erased thirteen turnings above it.

The larger decision took four quick days.

It did not happen in one talk. Bern left a tile. Sella answered the next morning. He returned a second tile during his same long afternoon. She carried that one for a day before replying. I saw each tile move and read none of them.

On the second quick day, Sella asked the young worker whether he wanted the near-bank turning work whenever the river moved. He asked whether the position belonged to Bern. Sella said the work belonged to whoever agreed to do it and could do it safely.

He asked for three more practices before deciding.

That answer pleased her. I know because she gave him the longest pole, which was not a reward but was certainly trust.

On the third day, he read the ferry shallows while Sella watched from the bank. He missed a soft patch, found it on the second pass, and moved the route marker before she told him. Then he accepted future turnings with a condition: if he took other work upriver, he would train the next person before leaving.

Sella put the condition on the tool board.

On the fourth day, Bern came down without his old plumb weight. He carried the two orchard crates that had waited three months and a small repaired handle from the crossing table.

Sella met him at the landing. I was repairing nothing nearby, which is another way to say I stayed where I could hear only what they chose to say aloud.

The decision was Sella's. Bern would no longer take a place at the turnings merely because children had once promised it. The young quick had the near work. Any capable long worker could set the far marks from the readings. Bern could help if he happened to be there and wanted the task, but no future turning would be proof of friendship.

Bern asked what would be proof.

Sella said, “Nothing once.”

One visit could be guilt. Six dates could be a schedule. A promise could be kept while the reason beneath it disappeared. Whatever they rebuilt would have to exist through repeated choices too small to stand as evidence by themselves.

Bern took the old fruit crates to the ferry and checked the bottoms before stacking them. One needed a corner brace. He repaired it while Sella loaded passengers. They did not use the time to discuss the next ten years.

When the boat returned, Sella found the brace finished. She tested it under weight, marked the debt paid on her wall, and gave Bern a short tile before he left.

He read it at the message frame. Then he put it into a pocket, the way Sella had kept his.

I did not know what either tile said. I knew the turnings had a new crew, the crates had returned, and neither of those things was being asked to carry the whole friendship anymore.

## Chapter 26: A Bird on a Tile

Bern began leaving small messages whenever orchard work brought him near the lower path.

The first tile said the wind had stripped leaves from one west tree. Sella read it while waiting for two passengers and placed it on her record shelf. She did not add it to the water wall. The tree was not a ferry fact.

Her reply went up the next day. I did not read it.

A second tile reported that the later fruit was setting badly. A third said Bern had seen a bird with one white wing. Neither message asked Sella to do anything. No date demanded an answer. They were pieces of an ordinary day offered because he had thought of her during it.

Sella answered some at once. Others waited until she passed the frame with something to say. She did not match tile for tile. Bern did not count, as far as I could observe, and I had stopped counting for him.

This did not repair eleven years. The tiles could not reach backward. They did not turn Bern into somebody who had been present during the flood, the lost landing, Ruck's childhood, or every quiet month between turnings.

They changed what happened next.

One quick week, the west wind rose. Sella saw branches moving on the long slope and left a tile asking whether the damaged tree had held. Bern's answer arrived during the same long afternoon and two quick days later. It described a split limb, three saved fruits, and the support he had moved before the rain.

Sella read it twice. Then she went to the boathouse wall and looked at her old symbol for wind.

I wanted to join the two records. Wind here, damaged tree there, same weather, matching dates. The connection was real. I did not draw it. Sella carried the tile to her house.

Several weeks after the turning, Bern came down the long road without a crate, plumb weight, repaired part, or message for someone else. He was simply there.

Sella had three ferry crossings waiting. Bern sat beside the far landing while she completed them. From his rate, the wait was short. From hers, he remained through a piece of the afternoon without becoming another task.

On the third return, Sella tied the boat and walked to the board at the line. Bern came from his side. They did not begin riverstones. They sat with the board between them because it gave each a place to be.

Bern asked about the flood that took her old landing.

Sella answered with more than one selected fact. She told him how the upstream rope failed first, how a neighbor lost a stack of quickbread trays while helping, and how the replacement landing had to be built twice because the first height matched the old water rather than the new bank.

Bern listened. When he asked a question, it was about the trays.

Sella laughed. The sound crossed to him as a chirp, so he could not have understood it. He smiled after it reached him anyway.

I stayed with the ferry. Two passengers arrived while Sella was away from the landing. I could not run the boat. I could tell them she would return when she chose, and they could wait or use the ford.

One waited. One walked upstream.

That was a cost of the visit. Sella had not ceased having work because Bern came without a task. She had chosen a break and let two other people arrange themselves around it. When she returned, she apologized to the waiting passenger for the delay and carried him across. She did not explain Bern.

The next time Bern passed the frame, he left another short tile. When he and Sella discussed visits, they expected he might come down about once within each of her years and do nothing useful. They fixed no date and made no promise about the count. The next arrival would be unknown even to Sella, and I could not say how long the pattern would last.

I could say the messages were small enough to fit beside their separate lives. The old promise had saved everything for the turning. These tiles wasted words on wind, poor fruit, and a bird.

That waste was the useful part.

## Chapter 27: My First Game

I finally played riverstones.

I had watched enough games to believe I knew the rules. This was false. I knew how the board looked after other people made sensible choices. Once a bag of stones was placed beside me and Sella asked for my first move, the board became thirty-two marked spaces with too many possible errors.

Bern sat on the long side. He had come down without warning again, which made it his second visit that week by Sella's count and part of the same afternoon by his. No one said this was too many. No one promised to repeat it.

Sella explained that I could enter from either side because I had no rate. I chose quick ground out of habit. That gave Bern the first move.

He placed a black stone near the river mark. I put mine beside it. Sella removed my stone because Bern's first placement gave him the current. I objected that one stone should not own anything. She said that was exactly how ownership worked in the game.

I asked why anyone would play such a game. Bern laughed slowly from across the board. Sella gave my stone back and told me to choose a bank instead.

The game forced both rates away from what felt natural. Bern had to make choices before his ordinary pace would have required them. Sella, playing as my adviser, had to wait through his moves without filling the space. I had to stop measuring the board as if the largest controlled area automatically won. Riverstones counted crossings, not land.

My third move opened a route Bern used against me. My fifth blocked Sella's planned reply. My seventh was legal, which was the best thing anyone said about it.

Children gathered. Ruck offered advice to all three players and changed sides whenever the board changed. A long child began translating Ruck's chirps into moves, mostly by guessing. The guesses were better than Ruck's advice.

Near the middle, Bern placed a stone that could either close my route or invite me across his. I counted the open spaces. Sella stopped me and pointed to the narrow path he had left toward his bank. She believed it was probably a trap and also believed I should probably take it.

I took it. The path was a trap, but Bern had miscounted the return and trapped one of his own stones with mine. Sella laughed. Bern argued that the rule should favor intent. Every spectator rejected this because no game survives a rule about what Bern meant to do.

I lost eleven moves later.

The loss did not trouble me. For one hour on Bern's side and nearly five on Sella's, the board required both of them to adjust. Neither accommodation was treated as the normal one. The marked surface held the shared limits. Within them, choice still mattered.

Bern and Sella played after me. He was worse than I was, though much happier about it. Sella won by closing two crossings in one move. Bern accused her of inventing the move. She found the same move carved into the rule edge of the board.

They argued for most of another game.

Three quick days later, they played again. Bern had not come to repair anything. Sella did not clear the afternoon for him. She finished the ferry crossings already waiting, then joined the board. Bern lost again and disputed a different rule.

The change was not that they became easy with each other. Sella still refused to turn messages into a schedule. Bern still sometimes began speaking as if no quick week had passed. Once, Sella ended a story with, “That happened before the flood,” and he had to ask which flood.

The difference was that the question could continue into another tile or another visit. One turning no longer had to hold everything they had failed to say.

After the second game, I returned my stones to the bag and counted them. There were thirty-one. We searched the grass until Ruck admitted using one to mark a delivery path.

Sella made Ruck return it. The game could tolerate disagreement about rules. It still needed all its pieces.

## Chapter 28: Two Dates on Every Stone

Ruck told me about the burial ground and refused to take me there.

The reason changed three times. First Ruck had work. Then the hill was dull. Finally Ruck said people should visit graves only when they knew who they were visiting. I had no person to visit, but I went to see how the valley kept time after a life ended.

The ground lay above long Ford on a slope facing the river. Quicks and longs rested in the same grass. Every marker carried two dates, one in counts and one in spans, because even death did not make one calendar sufficient.

The quick family stones stood close together. Four generations occupied a strip no wider than one old long grave and the grass around it. Names repeated. Work marks changed from ferry to farm to market. A child's stone sat between two adults with only a few quick months cut between its dates.

Long stones stood farther apart in time even when they stood beside the quick ones in earth. One orchard family had three markers covering more years than the whole quick row. Moss filled the oldest letters. Someone still trimmed the grass around them.

I expected the difference to make one set tragic and the other fortunate. The ground refused. A short life was not explained by a quick rate. A long date did not tell me whether the person had wanted another day. The numbers described duration. The stones carried names, work marks, and small objects because duration had never been the whole person.

One marker held a carving of a ferry knot. Another showed a tree branch. A third had a riverstones piece set into the top. Rain had filled its shallow center, and a bird drank from it while I watched.

The bird did not know which rate the player had lived.

From the slope I could see the repaired cart route bending upstream. Wagons followed it without stopping. The permanent post line crossed above the ford, each stone showing only its mossy top. Tarr was working near the lower hill with another linekeeper. By evening, two posts had new earth around them.

The work of the valley continued below its dead.

When I returned, Sella had covered one section of her wall with a landing plan. She wanted to lengthen the quick dock upstream so cargo could be staged without blocking passengers. The new section would need to clear the latest ferry route and remain useful when the channel shifted back.

She explained the angles twice. I understood the second time that she was asking me to check the open space, not approve the plan.

We took a measuring cord outside. I held one end over the proposed corner while Sella walked the future edge. A stack of crates would fit between landing and road. Two waiting passengers could pass if they turned sideways. Sella changed the width.

“Planning for more people?” I asked.

“Planning for people to be people.”

They would stand in the wrong place, arrive early, wait with large objects, and stop to speak in the narrowest section. A landing built only for perfect movement would fail on its first busy day.

Ruck ran through while we measured and proved the point by catching the cord at one ankle. This time no poles stood nearby. Ruck stayed upright, delivered three notices, and announced that work had improved.

“Your work?” I asked.

“All work.”

Ruck had grown again. Not enough to surprise me this time. The messenger route now included the lower mill, and Ruck had learned to read the field marks before delivering a tile. One error had become part of the job rather than proof that Ruck was bad at it.

At sunset, Sella added the new worker's third practice to the tool board. The ferry wall now held six years of water, thirteen turnings with Bern, one with a changed crew, returned crates, and a landing that did not exist yet.

Records did not have to trap a person in what had happened. They could prepare space for what came next.

I thought of the grave markers above long Ford. Their paired dates were finished. Sella's next blank line was not.

## Chapter 29: Whichever Bank I Stood On

I knew I was leaving before I knew how.

That happens each time. A world begins to feel complete around me, and the forward pull returns. There is no road I can point to. I only know that staying has changed from possible to temporary.

I told Sella first. She was comparing timber marks for the new landing and asked where I would go. I said I did not know. She said that was not useful but appeared to accept that it was true.

Ruck said I should wait until the messenger route expanded to the upper farms because otherwise I would miss seeing how fast it could be done. Tarr said nothing at all. She had another post open and assumed, correctly, that leaving was my work rather than hers.

Bern walked with me from the orchard on my last long morning.

Walking with a long is different from waiting for one. Once I stayed inside Bern's rate, the pace became ordinary. We followed the stone road between trimmed verges. He carried no fruit and I carried no pages. The six-visit schedule remained under my calendars at the boathouse.

Bern said I had worked out what happened. I corrected him: I had worked out the interval. He said that had helped him see it, and I reminded him that I had then tried to assign the rest. Bern considered this through several steps before agreeing that this part had been bad.

He did not rescue my intention. I was grateful, though gratitude was not what he was trying to produce.

Below us, quick Ford moved through a market morning. Stalls rose, traded, and began closing while we passed one bend. Sella's ferry made two crossings. From our side, its quick water route looked sharp and almost impossible.

I asked whether that bank looked wrong to him. Bern looked across and said it looked quick. When I asked if it looked less real, as if our speed were proper and the other side were being hurried, he stopped walking so he could give the question attention. It did not look less real to him. He could not see why it would.

I had spent months switching allegiance without noticing. On the quick side, ten months felt like the true cost and Bern's season looked small. In the orchard, his workday felt complete and the quick market became a blur. Whichever rate held my senses became the one I treated as actual.

I told him I kept choosing the real year and that my choice changed whenever I crossed. Bern started walking again and advised me to stop choosing. I said that was easy for him because he had learned it as a child. Most useful things, he replied, were easy for somebody else to say.

He told me children in long Ford usually discovered the problem around six, when quick cousins became older enough to notice between two visits. Quick children found it earlier because long adults stayed young for so much of their childhood. No lesson removed the strangeness. They learned to stop treating it as an error.

“Which year do you use when you think about Sella?” I asked.

Bern did not answer at once. “Hers, if I am thinking about what she lived. Mine, if I am thinking about what I can keep.”

That was not a single real year. It was a choice to place the question with the person whose life it affected.

At the lower road, the post line crossed our path. Bern stepped over without slowing. I passed after him and felt the lurch. On the quick side, his last movement stretched behind me while the ferry snapped toward its landing.

Both views were mine. I still could not hold them at the same instant.

Bern's laugh arrived deep and slow because he had seen my crossing face. I had crossed the boundary too many times for that to remain funny. This did not stop him.

I waited until the laugh finished. Then we continued to the ford at the rate of the bank beneath us.

## Chapter 30: The Measure I Left

My last morning in Vell began with a crossing I did not need to make.

I woke on the quick side before the first ferry run, put my few things in their usual places, and then discovered there was no usual place for anything because I would not sleep in the boathouse again. The pages went into my pack. The riverstones stayed on the sill for Ruck. I carried the two calendars outside and sat on the landing with them across my knees. The columns were accurate. That was the difficulty.

One marked the days I had lived beside Sella. The other carried the smaller count beside Bern's orchard. Between them lay the page with six visits arranged as if two people were cargo and I had authority over the route. I had stopped using it, but I had not decided what to do with it.

Across the water, long Ford remained in an early hour. The orchard held a pale strip of sun. On my bank, the same light had already cleared the roofs and reached the market road. Both calendars described what I saw. Neither could tell me what the view had cost anyone.

Sella arrived with a coil of line over one shoulder and found me still holding the pages. She set down the coil, checked the ferry cleat, and asked whether I planned to depart by studying my own handwriting until it carried me away. I showed her the schedule and said I had made it before I understood that an interval was not a life.

She read the six entries. Her face did not change, but she took enough time that I knew she was reading rather than merely waiting for me to finish speaking.

She asked what I wanted from her. The first answer that came to me was forgiveness. That was another assignment: a clean feeling placed in her hands so I could leave with it. I folded the page once instead and said, “Nothing. You should know I kept it.”

Sella held out her hand. I gave her the page, and she put it beneath the ferry log.

When I asked why she would keep it, Sella said, “A bad plan can still be a record. It may stop the next person who is very impressed with six boxes.”

This was fair. I had been impressed with them.

Ruck came down the road before the first passengers, carrying the smooth riverstone that had gone missing during our game. He had found it for a second time under a stack of landing boards. I told him the stone belonged with the set. He said a set did not need every stone to stay in one place forever, then put it in my pack before I could turn his idea into a rule.

The first ferry run carried two baskets, a chair with one shortened leg, and a woman who gave Sella three different instructions about keeping the chair dry. Ruck managed the forward line. I managed the rear because Sella assigned me the task, and because leaving did not excuse me from work that was already at my feet.

We entered long water. The woman's last instruction stretched behind us. Ruck's pull slowed into a careful, visible sequence. Sella adjusted the angle before the current could take the loaded side. I knew the direction of the change, the ratio between the banks, and the count that would appear in both calendar columns by evening. The lurch still caught in my stomach.

I let it remain a bodily fact. I did not turn toward the passenger and explain her own crossing to her.

At the far landing, Bern waited beside a returned fruit crate. He was not there for my departure. He had brought the crate for the next ferry and intended to ride over for the new landing work. That made the meeting easier. We could say goodbye while each of us was already doing something else.

He placed the crate, checked the lash, and told me the white-wing bird had appeared in the orchard again. Sella said it had probably been visiting both banks all along and simply had better sense than the people who tried to account for it. Bern accepted this possibility. No one proposed another schedule.

On the return crossing, Sella and Bern discussed the depth of the upstream footing. Ruck interrupted twice with questions about timber. Their words changed speed at the line, but the work did not break. Each person waited where waiting was needed and acted where action belonged to them. I had once mistaken that adjustment for proof that Vell's people had solved the boundary. Now it looked like maintenance: skilled, tiring, ordinary, and never finished.

Tarr stood at the quick landing when we returned. Mud covered both sleeves. She had opened the last post nearest the market road and found its lower stone sound. The repair crew would reset the cap after midday.

I asked if she had discovered anything that named the builders or explained why the line had been made. She had not. “A post says someone wanted a post there,” Tarr replied. “You can make it confess more if you bring a hammer, but it will be your confession.”

She had managed to summarize my early work in Vell without mentioning me.

I walked with her to the open bed. The post lay on cloth beside the trench, its worn number facing up. Under it, the dark stone ran toward the river and continued beyond what the crew had cleared. I could imagine hands placing it, a purpose for the straight course, a maker who understood the two rates or perhaps caused them. Imagination provided several complete answers before Tarr finished cleaning one corner. I wrote none of them down. Instead, I recorded the post number, the depth, the direction of the stone, and Tarr's finding that the base remained firm. I gave her the page. She checked the figures and corrected my drawing of the cap.

By the time I closed my pack, the quick market had reached its loudest hour. Sella was back on the ferry. Bern and Ruck had joined the landing crew, one moving with long deliberation and the other in quick trips between the timber stack and the water. From the road, their work looked mismatched until I watched the beam itself. It moved steadily into place because neither worker tried to make the other carry time at the wrong rate.

I said goodbye once. Sella lifted a hand from the tiller. Ruck called that I still owed him a riverstones game, which was false by every count except his. Bern pointed toward my pack, where the missing stone made one small weight among my pages. Tarr was already looking into the trench.

The road out of Ford climbed beside the posts. I followed it past the market, past the burial ground, and past the turn toward the orchard. At each crossing the world lurched and settled. I stopped counting how long the discomfort lasted. A sensation did not become more truthful because I gave it a number.

Near the head of the valley, I reached the stone Tarr had shown me. The buried course rose there and disappeared into the hillside. Beyond it, the road narrowed among dark trees. Nothing marked the exact place where Vell ended.

I put one hand on the final visible post. Quick water flashed far below. On the opposite bank, long light still lay across the orchard. The line held both without declaring either one the standard.

I had arrived wanting the river to admit which part of it was wrong. I left knowing the arithmetic, the direction, and the limits of what those facts could answer. The rest belonged with the people who had lived it, and any question I carried onward would have to remain with them long enough to hear their measure.

Then I took my hand from the post and followed the narrowing road. Behind me, the river continued at two rates. Ahead, the next step required only one.
