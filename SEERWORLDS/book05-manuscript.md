## Chapter 1: The Most Ordinary Place

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I know more than I used to. I can move. I can look at a thing until I understand it. I have been to four worlds now, and I used to think that meant I was getting good at them.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I arrived on a riverbank, in the rain.


It was the most ordinary place I had ever been.

The first world was a cliff going up out of sight. The second was under water. The third was a plain of stone columns and the fourth had no sky at all.

This one had grass. It had a river, brown and wide and slow, going down the middle of a green valley with low hills on both sides. It had weather. Actual weather, moving, with clouds in it. It was raining lightly and then it stopped and the light came out sideways and everything went bright.

There was a town downstream and a smaller one up. There were fields. There was a road.

I floated over the water and thought: *there is nothing peculiar about this at all,* which is the sort of thing I have learned to be suspicious of.

Then I looked at the river properly.

## Chapter 2: Two Speeds

There is a ford about halfway along the valley, a shallow place where the bank flattens out and the stones come up near the surface and you can get across.

The river runs through it.

And below the ford it was moving faster than above the ford.

Not a little. Not the way water speeds up over stones and slows again. **Below the ford the river was running about five times as fast as it was above it**, and the change happened at a line, and the line did not move.

I went and looked at it from directly overhead.

Upstream: slow brown water with leaves on it, turning gently.

Downstream: the same brown water, the same leaves, going like something poured.

And in between, a place about the width of my body where one became the other.

I stayed there a long time. I could not make it work. Water does not do that. Water in the same channel with the same fall and the same volume goes the same speed, and this water was not, and there was nothing in the riverbed to explain it.

I was still there when something hit me in the side at what I would later learn to call speed.


“Sorry! Sorry.”

It bounced off, spun, came back, and stopped in front of me. It did all of that in about the time it takes to say *sorry* twice, which it had.

It was about knee-high. Gray-green, loose in the joints, with a narrow head and two wide flat eyes set far apart, and four fingers on each hand, and a fine fringe of growth along its forearms that was standing straight up.

“You’re new.”

“Yes.”

“I’d have seen you.” It went around me once, quickly. “What are you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Are you from over the line?”

“I don’t know where that is.”

It stopped dead, which I would learn was unusual, and its fringe came down.

“You don’t know where the *line* is.”

“No.”

“It’s right there,” it said, and pointed at the water, at the exact place where the river changed.

## Chapter 3: Which Rate Are You?

Its name was Ruck.

It was a Vellin, which is what the people there are. The world is called Vell and the valley is called Vell and the river is called Vell, and when I asked about this Ruck said yes, and did not understand the question, and went on to something else.

It was, by its own account, nearly a year old, which meant nothing to me at the time and turned out to mean it was roughly the age of a child who has just started to be embarrassed by its parents.

It talked continuously because it was in a hurry, permanently, about everything.

“That’s the line,” it said. “That side’s long. This side’s quick. We’re quick. You’re...” It looked at me. “What are you?”

“I still don’t know.”

“Which rate are you?”

“I don’t have one.”

“Everybody has one.”

“I don’t.”

Ruck considered this for what was, for Ruck, a long time. About two seconds. Then it decided I was strange, which was correct, and moved on to the next thing.

“Come on. I’ll show you the posts.”

A rate is which side of the line you were born on. Quick or long. It is not a job and it is not a choice and you do not get a second one, and asking a Vellin their rate is the same as asking where they are from, because on Vell those are the same question. Ruck asked me mine before it asked me my name.


The line has posts.

That was the first thing that told me somebody had been here a long time. They are cut stone, about waist-high on a Vellin, set in a row that runs from the top of one hill, down across the valley, straight through the ford, and up the other side. They are numbered. They are slightly mossy. Two of them near the bank had been recently straightened and the earth around them was still raw.

“Tarr did those yesterday,” Ruck said.

“Who’s Tarr?”

“Linekeeper.”

“What’s a linekeeper?”

“Keeps the posts where they go,” said Ruck, already looking at something else.

“What are they for?”

“So you know where it is.”

“Can’t you see where it is?”

“You can see it in the *water*,” said Ruck, in the tone of somebody explaining that you cannot see the wind. “You can’t see it in a field.”

Which was fair, and which I only fully understood a moment later, when I crossed one.

## Chapter 4: A Bit of the Afternoon

Everybody there does this every day and nobody thinks about it.

I went over the line from the quick side to the long side, and the world **lurched**.

It was not painful and it did not last. It was about half a second of everything being wrong. The light was the wrong speed. The sound arrived in the wrong shape. Then it stopped and everything was completely normal again.

I turned around and went back and it happened again in the other direction.

Ruck fell over laughing.

It did it three more times because Ruck asked me to and then twice more because Ruck was still laughing, and by the fifth time I had stopped noticing the lurch, which I understand is what happens to everybody.

“Doesn’t it do that to you?”

“Course it does.”

“Then why is it funny?”

“Because you go all...” Ruck did an impression which involved its entire body and which I did not think was accurate.

Then Ruck looked downstream and made a sound.

“I’m late.”

“For what?”

“Everything.”

It went before I could ask anything else.


Here is what I worked out that afternoon, and I worked out all of it by looking, because nobody explained anything to me.

On the upstream side of the line, everything is slow.

Not sleepy. Not lazy. **Slow.** I watched a Vellin walk up the far bank and it took most of an hour, and it was walking normally, and its arms swung and its head turned and it looked at things as it went. From where I was it appeared to be standing still. You had to look away and look back to see that it had moved.

On the downstream side, everything is fast, and I only knew that because I was on it.

And it is the same creature. That is the part that took me longest and it is the most important thing about that world. Ruck and the one on the far bank were both Vellin: same body, same hands, same fringe, same wide flat eyes. If you put them side by side you could not tell which was which.

There is one kind of person on Vell. There are two speeds. Everybody there knows this and nobody there thinks it is interesting, and it took me most of a day.

You can only tell by watching them move.

---

Late in the day I went up to the line and looked across at the far bank for a while.

There was somebody over there doing something to a fence. As far as I could see they were not doing anything at all. But when the light started going I looked again, and the fence was mended.

There was a quick near the ford repairing the strap on a basket.

“How long has that taken?” I asked.

“What?”

“That. The fence.”

She squinted across.

“That’s Herrel. Bit of the afternoon.”

“It’s been the whole afternoon here.”

“Yes.”

“So how long has it been for Herrel?”

She looked at me with the particular expression of somebody who has been asked what color the sky is.

“A bit of the afternoon,” she said.

## Chapter 5: Ford

I spent the next several days on the quick side, because that was where I had arrived and because it did not occur to me to choose a side.

The town is called Ford, which is not imaginative, and there is another town on the long side also called Ford, which everybody agrees is confusing and nobody has ever done anything about.


The market runs every day and it is the fastest thing I have ever seen.

Not chaotic. Everybody knows exactly what they are doing. Just *fast*. Stalls go up in a few minutes. Trade happens at a speed I could barely follow, in sentences that were half-finished because both parties already knew the end. A dispute broke out over the weight of something and was conducted, resolved, and forgiven while I was still working out what the object was.

They eat **quickbread**, which is made in the morning and eaten by evening and is not good the next day, so there is no next day for it. There is also **pressing**, which is a dense gray root cake that keeps for months and which everybody in that valley eats constantly and complains about without stopping. I have never encountered a food so universally disliked and so universally consumed.

And there is **slowfruit**, which I did not see, because it takes a long year to ripen and almost none of it comes down to the quick side.

I asked a stallholder about it and she stopped what she was doing, which nobody there does.

“Had one once,” she said. “When I was small. My mother knew somebody over the line.”

“What was it like?”

She thought about it and gave up.

“It was the best thing I ever ate,” she said, and went back to work, and I noticed she was still thinking about it for some time afterward.

## Chapter 6: Two Calendars

They keep time in **counts** and **spans**.

A count is about a minute. A quick minute. A **span** is five counts, which is about a minute to a long. Everything on the quick side is scheduled in counts and everything on the long side in spans, and every schedule anybody writes has both, in two columns, and every child in that valley can convert between them before it can properly walk.

This matters later, and I got it wrong for a while.

**Nobody there is confused about the arithmetic.** Not one creature. It is not difficult and it is not mysterious. You take the number and you multiply or divide it by five and there is your answer.

They have been doing it for as long as there has been anybody in that valley.


I started keeping two calendars on the third day.

This was a mistake. I knew it was a mistake at the time and did it anyway, because I count things and I cannot help it.

I kept counts for the quick side and spans for the long side and I kept them in parallel, and within about a week I had made four separate errors, been corrected twice by Ruck and once by a Vellin who did it in passing without looking up from what she was doing, and developed a genuine grievance about the whole arrangement.

“It would be simpler,” I said to Ruck, “if there were one.”

“There is one. There’s counts.”

“For you.”

“Well, yes,” said Ruck.

## Chapter 7: Soon

Then there is **soon**.

If somebody wanted one thing about that world, it would be this.

*Soon* is the most dangerous word on Vell. Everybody uses it. Everybody knows it means something completely different on the other side of the line. Nobody stops.

A quick says *soon* and means before the end of the day.

A long says *soon* and means this season.

They both know this. They both know the other one knows. And they go on saying it, constantly, to each other, across the line, because it is what you say. About half the arguments in that valley grow out of it, and every single one of those arguments is entirely avoidable and completely inevitable.

I heard a long call across the water that he would send the thing over soon.

I heard the quick on my side say thank you.

I asked her, afterward, when she thought it would arrive.

“Oh, ages,” she said, comfortably. “He said soon.”


I asked Ruck, at the end of that first week, what happens if a quick and a long want to be actual friends. Not neighbours. Friends.

“They manage,” said Ruck.

“How?”

“They just do. There’s loads of them.” Ruck thought about it, which took a moment. “My aunt’s got one. She’s known him since she was little. He comes over sometimes.”

“How often?”

“Dunno. Sometimes.”

“Does she mind?”

“Mind what?”

I could not work out how to finish the question, and Ruck had already gone.

There is a word for two who have kept it up across the line for a long time: **matched**. It is said admiringly and with a certain amount of disbelief, the way you talk about somebody who has been married sixty years. I heard it perhaps four times in the whole valley and every time it was about somebody else, somebody further off, somebody’s aunt.

I did not think about it again until much later.

## Chapter 8: It Goes When I Go

The message posts are at the line, and they are the only way anybody arranges anything.

You do not knock on a door across the line. You leave word at the post: a marked tile, slotted into a numbered frame, with your mark and the date in both columns. It sits there until the other party comes past, and they come past on their own schedule, and their schedule is not yours.

The frame nearest the ford holds about forty tiles. I counted them on the fourth day and then again on the tenth and eleven of them had not moved.


Sella came at the end of that week.

The ferry runs from the quick bank, because you need somebody quick to work a boat in that current, and the ferrywoman is the only Vellin in the valley whose whole working life happens at the line.

She brought the boat in, tied it, unloaded four crates and a long, helped the long up the bank at a pace that must have been agonizing for her, and then turned round and saw me.

“You’re the floating one.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve heard about you from about nine people.” She was already coiling rope. “Ruck says you can’t count.”

“Ruck said what?”

“Ruck says you can’t keep two calendars.”

“That is not the same as being unable to count.”

“No,” Sella agreed. “It isn’t.”

I wanted to stay at the ferry after that. Sella did not seem to care whether I did, which was probably healthier.

“Do you want to go across?”

“Yes.”

“Then get in the boat,” she said. “It goes when I go. I don’t wait and I don’t come back for anybody.”

She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way you would state your opening hours.

## Chapter 9: Already There

I spent a long time at the ford after that, mostly because Sella was there and Sella was the most interesting creature in the valley.

She is fifteen. On the quick side that is an adult with a trade, a house, and a settled reputation, and she had all three.

And she was, without any competition, the most organized living thing I have ever encountered.


She keeps records. Nobody asks her to. The ferry is hers and she answers to nobody. She has simply decided that a thing worth doing is worth knowing about.

She has the water level for every day of the last six years. She has the crossings, counted, by day. She has a mark for every time the current changed enough to matter and a note of what she did about it.

She knows what the river is going to do about four days before it does it.

“Why do you keep all this?”

“Because then I know.”

“Know what?”

“What it does,” said Sella, and looked at me as though I had asked why she kept the boat dry.


She is early for everything. I do not mean punctual. I mean that in the whole time I knew her I never once saw her arrive at the moment she was expected. She was always already there, having got there earlier, having done the first part of whatever it was.

I asked her about that too, eventually.

“If you’re early you’re ready,” she said. “If you’re on time you’re starting.”

**And here is the thing about Sella**, which is the opposite of what I expected, and I expected it for stupid reasons.

She is not in a hurry.

I had assumed that because she lives fast, because everything on her side of the line goes at five times the pace, she would be frantic. Quick people ought to be impatient. It seemed obvious.

She is the calmest creature in that valley. She plans, and then she executes the plan, and the plan is good, so nothing is ever urgent.

It is the *long* side that improvises.

## Chapter 10: Riverstones

Riverstones is the one game both rates play, and they play it at the line, and it is the only thing I saw the whole valley do together.

It is slow enough for a quick to bear and fast enough for a long to follow. You put stones on a marked board and take them off again and there are rules, and I never fully learned them, and there is a great deal of arguing.

A game takes a long about an hour and a quick about five hours, and both of them experience it as *a game of riverstones*, which is, I think, the entire point of it, and which took me an embarrassingly long time to understand.

Children play calling games there too. They stand on opposite banks and shout.

From the quick side, a long’s voice arrives as a low groan, going on and on and never getting anywhere.

From the long side, a quick’s voice is a chirp, like a bird, over before you have turned your head.

Neither side can understand a word of the other and the children find this so funny that they do it for hours, and both sets of parents find it maddening, and it has evidently been going on for generations.


Sella said the thing on the eleventh day and she said it in passing, while doing something else, which is how she said everything that mattered.

We were re-setting a marker post that the water had loosened. She was doing the work; I was holding the line straight because it turns out that is a thing I can do.

“I used to run this with somebody,” she said.

“The ferry?”

“All of it.” She packed stone in around the base. “Doesn’t matter.”

I did not ask, because I had learned by then that Sella tells you things on her own schedule or not at all, and pushing gets you nothing.

She did not mention it again for a long time.

## Chapter 11: The Long Side

I went across to the long side two days later.

*Slow* does not cover it.

You cross the line and the lurch happens and then you are the fast thing. Everything around you is treacle. A Vellin greets you and you have time, inside the greeting, to look around, count the trees, notice the weather changing and come back, and the greeting is still arriving.

It is exhausting. Quicks can stand about half a day of it and then they have to come back over.

The town is quieter and larger and there is more space between everything. The buildings are better built, because they were built slowly. The road is better made. Somebody has been tending the verges for a hundred years.

And there is an orchard on the slope above it, and that is where I met Bern.


He was up a ladder doing something to a branch, and he came down when he saw me, and the coming down took about as long as a conversation.

“Hello,” he said. “You’ll be the one from over the line.”

“Yes.”

“Ruck’s friend.”

“You know Ruck?”

“I know of Ruck,” said Bern. “Everybody over there knows of Ruck. Word gets across.”

He was twenty-two, which on the long side is a young adult with his own orchard and opinions about it. He was the same creature as Sella in every particular: same gray-green, same wide eyes, same four fingers. The difference was that he wore his fringe long, which is a long-side fashion, and quicks think it looks ridiculous, and I could see why.

He was warm and easy and slightly chaotic. He offered me things I could not eat, three separate times, having forgotten each time. He changed the subject constantly. He asked me four questions and did not wait for the answer to any of them.

**And here is the second thing that was the opposite of what I expected.**

I had assumed the long side would be deliberate. Patient. Considered. Two hundred years of life, and all that time to think.

Bern makes every decision on the spot and changes it later.

Why would he not? He has always had time. Nothing has ever been urgent. *Later* has never once let him down.

## Chapter 12: It’ll Keep

The orchard grows slowfruit and it takes a long year for the fruit to set, which is five of Sella’s.

“Nobody quick can do this,” he said, not boasting, just explaining. “You’d not live to see the first crop. It’d be like planting for your grandchildren, except you’d have no grandchildren yet.”

He gave me one to look at. It is about the size of my whole self, dark gold, and it smells like nothing else I have encountered on any world.

“Take it down to the ford,” he said. “Give it to whoever’s on the boat.”

“When?”

“Whenever you’re going,” said Bern.


He talked about his oldest friend that afternoon and I did not connect it to anything.

He said he had a friend down at the crossing. He said they had known each other since they were both small, which on the face of it made no sense and which I filed away and did not ask about. He said she was the best of them.

And then he said, with real puzzlement, and no bitterness at all:

“She’s gone strange with me lately. I don’t know what I’ve done.”

“Strange how?”

“Short,” said Bern. “Not rude. Just finished. Like she’s already had the conversation.”

He shook his head and went back up the ladder, taking most of a minute about it.

“I’ll ask her at the turning,” he said. “It’ll keep.”

## Chapter 13: Because of a Crate

I worked it out four days later and I worked it out because of a crate.


Sella keeps a list of who owes her what. Of course she does. It is on the wall of the boathouse in her own small precise mark, and it is up to date, and one of the lines on it read: *orchard; 2 crates; not collected.*

“Whose orchard?”

“Bern’s.”

I had the slowfruit with me. I had been carrying it around for two days because I had not established who to give it to.

“Bern up on the long slope? Long fringe? Talks a lot?”

“That’s him.”

“He gave me this for whoever was on the boat.”

Sella looked at the slowfruit for slightly too long.

“Did he,” she said.


I got it wrong next, and I got it wrong in a way I would not have two worlds ago.

I did not think either of them was lying. I have met liars and I know what it costs to hold one steady, and neither of these two was spending anything at all.

What I thought was: **somebody has their facts wrong.**

Sella said he stopped coming. Bern said he sees her all the time.

Those are statements about the world. They are checkable. One of them is describing something that did not happen, not on purpose, but because memory is bad and time is confusing and people misremember.

So I did what I do.

I went and measured it.

## Chapter 14: Eleven Days

I measured for eleven days and I have never been more thorough about anything.

Sella’s records are the reason it was possible. I had not understood, until she let me at them, what six years of daily observation looks like when the observer is somebody who does not miss days. Water level. Crossings, counted. Weather. Who came over and roughly when. Every date for six years with a note on what the river had done to deserve it.

It is on the boathouse wall in her own small mark, in two columns, counts and spans, because everything at the ford has to work for both sides.

“Why two columns?”

“Because half my crossings are long,” said Sella, “and if I wrote it in counts only they’d have to do the arithmetic, and they’d do it wrong, and then they’d argue with me about it.”

“They can all do the arithmetic.”

“They can all do the arithmetic,” she agreed. “They still do it wrong when they’re cross.”

---

There was a festival on the quick side in the middle of my eleven days, which I mention because of where I watched it from.

It is a small one. The quicks have a great many small ones. It involves bread and a particular kind of shouting and it lasts most of a day.

I went up the long bank to time something and I looked back across, and from over there the whole festival was a smear. A brightness on the far bank and a noise like an insect and then it was over, and the long standing next to me had not looked up.

“That’s Ford having one of their days,” she said, when I asked. “They have a lot of them.”

“Doesn’t it bother you?”

“Bother me?” She thought about it, at length. “It’s nice. It’s like weather. You look over and something’s happening.”

She had watched perhaps four hundred of them in her life and had never been to one, and did not consider this remarkable.

## Chapter 15: Every Turning

I timed Bern’s crossings by waiting at the ford. I established the exact conversion at the line, from three separate directions, and got the same answer every time.

I built the whole thing out in counts and spans, twice, in parallel, and checked one against the other.

Every single number was right.

And here is what they said.

**Bern comes to every turning.** The turning is when the river shifts against the near bank and the crossing has to be re-marked from both sides at once. It is a day’s work, needing somebody on each bank, calling measurements across the water. It happens about once a long-season.

**He has never missed one.** Not one, in his entire life. Sella’s own records confirm it: the turning dates are all there, six years of them, and Bern is at every single one.

**And a long-season is about ten months of Sella’s life.**


So he arrives, and they do the work, and he is glad to see her and she is glad to see him, and then he goes home.

And she lives the better part of a year.

And then he comes back and asks how she has been.

## Chapter 16: Both of Them

I checked it four more times, because I did not believe it.

Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is trivial. What I did not believe was that there was nothing else. I kept looking for the extra thing: a missed turning he had lied about, a visit she had forgotten, a stretch where he had been ill, some quantity somewhere that would tip the two accounts into agreement.

I went back six years. I found nothing. Six years of Sella’s records and every turning date accounted for and Bern at all of them.

I even considered, for a while, that Sella was exaggerating. That *he stopped coming* was the sort of thing people say when they are hurt, and that if I went at it carefully she would revise it downward.

I want that written down because it is the worst thing I thought on that world, and because it took me four days to work out why it was wrong.

She had not exaggerated by a single day. She had lived every one of them.


I stayed with the numbers that evening and could not find anything wrong with them.

*He has never missed a turning*: true.

*He stopped coming*: also true.

Nobody had made a mistake. There was no error anywhere in it. I had eleven days of measurement and two accounts that could not both be right and were.

## Chapter 17: There’s Time

I took it to Bern first. That was the wrong order, though I do not think the other order would have been better.


He was in the orchard. He had forgotten I was coming, which he had also arranged.

I had time to look at the place properly that morning, because Bern was at the far end doing something to a drain and was going to be a while.

A slowfruit orchard is the strangest farm I have seen anywhere. The trees are enormous and there are not many of them, and they are spaced so far apart that from the middle you cannot see the edges. Everything about it is arranged for a creature who will still be here in eighty years.

Bern’s grandmother planted about half of it and his father planted most of the rest, and the youngest trees in it are older than Bern is.

“Who planted these?”

“Not me,” said Bern, when he eventually arrived. “That’s how it works. Nobody plants these for themselves. You are given somebody else’s trees and you look after them for a while and then somebody else gets them.” He put his hand on the nearest trunk. “I’ve not put one in yet. I keep meaning to. There’s time.”

He said it without any weight at all. It is simply what an orchard is, to him.

And there are quick orchards down the valley: small, fast, four crops a year, everything grown from seed to fruit inside a season. The quicks think slowfruit is a miracle and the longs think quick fruit is barely worth eating, and both of them are perfectly happy with this arrangement.

## Chapter 18: Eleven Years

I did not lead up to it. I gave him the numbers.

He listened all the way through, which he does not usually do, and at the end of it he said:

“Ten months.”

“About that.”

“For her.”

“Yes.”

He stood there with his hand on the ladder.

“I knew that,” he said.

“You knew it?”

“Of course I knew it. Everybody knows it. It’s five to one, it’s always been five to one, I’ve known it since I was...” He stopped. “I’ve always known it.”

“Then?”

“Knowing it isn’t the same as it happening to you,” said Bern.

He turned the branch over in his hand and did not look at me.

“I come down. I see her. I think: there she is, good, nothing’s changed. And I go home and I do a season’s work and I come back and it’s been *no time at all*.” He put the branch down. “That’s what it is for me. That’s the truth of it. It’s been no time at all.”

“And for her it’s been ten months.”

“Yes.”

“Every time.”

“Yes,” said Bern. “Every time.”

---

**Then I went and told Sella, and this is where I found out what I had actually been doing for eleven days.**

She was mending a rowlock. She let me get all the way to the end.

Then she said: “I know.”

“You...”

“I know. I’ve always known.” She turned the rowlock over. “He comes every turning. He’s never missed one. It’s ten months of mine and about two of his and it’s five to one and I could do that arithmetic when I was four.”

“Then why did you tell me he stopped coming?”

“Because he did,” said Sella.

I did not have anything to say to that.

“He stopped coming *for me*,” she said. “He comes for the turning. He always comes for the turning. And in between there is a year of my life in which he is not there, and then he arrives and he is pleased to see me and he asks how I’ve been, and I have to decide how much of a year to tell him about.” She set the rowlock down. “You’ve told me a number. I’ve had that number for eleven years.”

## Chapter 19: The Promise

That was the moment I understood what I had done.

I had spent eleven days establishing, with total rigour, the one thing neither of them needed established. They both had the arithmetic. The arithmetic had never been in dispute.

I had assumed that two true statements which disagreed must have a third, truer statement underneath them that would settle it.

There wasn’t one.

There was what happened to him and what happened to her. Both happened. No clock in that valley could make one experience cancel the other.

---

The promise came out that evening, from Sella, flatly, because I had earned enough of an answer by then to get one.

They were children. She was three; he was twenty. Both children. That works, on Vell, and nobody there finds it odd.

They were at the ford every day, the way children are, and one summer the river shifted and the marks had to be moved, and the two of them helped, and it was the most important day of either of their lives.

And they promised, the way you do at that age, meaning every word of it:

> **”Every turning. Both of us. Whatever else is happening.”**

“And he’s kept it,” said Sella.

“Yes.”

“That’s the thing I can’t get past. He’s kept it *exactly*. He has never once been late and never once not come, and if you asked anybody in this valley they’d say Bern’s the most reliable friend a person could have.” She looked out at the water. “How do you complain about that? What is there to say? *You did everything you promised and it wasn’t enough?*”

## Chapter 20: Because It Was

I asked her, because I had to, whether she had ever tried.

“Once.”

“What happened?”

“I said I’d missed him.” She was looking at the water. “And he said he’d missed me too. And he meant it. He had. Two months of missing me, and he’d meant every bit of it.” She turned the rowlock over. “And I’d had a year, and I’d got through it, and by the time he said it I’d already stopped needing to hear it.”

“What did you say?”

“I said it was good to see him,” said Sella. “Because it was.”

---

I went back over the line that night and did not sleep, which is not a thing I do anyway.

I had come to that valley a month earlier believing that if I could establish what had actually happened, something would follow from it. That is what happened on the other worlds. You find the thing everybody has got wrong, you show them, and the world adjusts.

Here there was nothing to show anybody. They both had it. They had both had it the whole time.

The only creature in that valley who had learned anything in a month was me.

## Chapter 21: Compared to What?

Ruck had grown.

I noticed it that week and it stopped me. It is not a sad thing and I do not want to make it one.

When I arrived Ruck was a child. Hurtling, shrieking, playing calling games at the line and losing every waiting game it entered. Nearly a year old. About nine, in the way that matters.

By the time I had finished measuring, Ruck was most of a head taller, had opinions about work, and had begun doing a thing with its fringe that I gathered was fashionable.

“You’ve grown.”

“Yes,” said Ruck, delighted. “I know. Everybody says.”

“It’s been about four months.”

“Has it?”

“Doesn’t that seem fast to you?”

Ruck genuinely did not understand the question. It thought about it, out of politeness, and then answered honestly:

“Compared to what?”

I did not have an answer, and I still do not.

I had been about to say *compared to how fast a thing should grow*, and there is no such rate. There is the rate Ruck grows at, which on the quick side is simply the rate children grow at, and there is the rate a long child grows at, which is the rate children grow at over there.

From inside, both of them are just growing up.

“Is it good?” I asked instead. “Being nearly grown.”

“It’s the *best*,” said Ruck, with enormous feeling. “I’ve got work. Did I tell you I’ve got work?”

It had told me four times.


I went to a first-gathering that week, on the quick side, for a child born to a family Sella knew.

It was a good afternoon and it lasted about two hours and there were forty people at it. The child was three days old and already crawling.

The thing I noticed was the messages. Half the family are long. They married across, which happens more than I had expected. None of them could come, because two hours’ notice on the quick side is no notice at all on the other. So the tiles had gone up at the post a season earlier and the long relatives had sent word back, and the word was read out by an aunt who had been holding the tiles for weeks and had clearly practised.

Nobody found this sad. It is simply how a cross-rate family does a party. They read the tiles out, and everybody cheered at the right places, and somebody said *Herrel says the same as last time* and the whole room laughed at what was evidently an old joke about Herrel.

## Chapter 22: Music at the Line

There was music at the line that week, which happens when both sides decide to do it at once, which happens rarely and by accident.

Quick music is fast and intricate and there is a lot of it. Long music is enormous and slow and to a quick ear it is one held note that goes on for an afternoon.

They played at the same time, on opposite banks, at each other.

Neither side could make anything of the other. The quicks heard a drone. The longs heard a buzz. And both of them stood there and listened politely to the end, and applauded, and said what a fine thing it had been, and went home, and I do not believe a single creature on either bank had any idea what they had just heard.

Nothing else on that world stayed with me the way that did.

---

**The next turning was coming.**

I found this out from Sella’s wall, where it was written down, because everything is written down on Sella’s wall. Fourteen days off, on her count.

Neither of them had mentioned it to me. Both of them knew.

So I went away and solved the problem, and the solution was good. It was not a stupid solution. I had watched both of them for weeks and I did the arithmetic properly and what I came up with would have worked.

**Come more often.** Not once a long-season. Six times. A fixed interval, measured in *counts*, set against Sella’s life instead of Bern’s. Bern would experience it as dropping in rather more than he used to. Sella would experience it as somebody being in her life.

I took it to Bern and he said yes immediately.

He did not even think about it. He said: *is that all? Yes, obviously, why didn’t she say?* He meant it, and he was pleased, and he wanted to go and tell her that afternoon.

I took it to Sella.

## Chapter 23: A Delivery

“No,” she said.

“You haven’t heard it.”

“I’ve heard it. Six times instead of one.” She was doing something with a rope and she did not stop. “He said yes, did he?”

“Straight away.”

“Course he did.” She pulled the knot through. “He’d say yes to anything. That’s not the same as wanting to.”

“He does want to. He was pleased.”

“He was pleased because *you* asked him,” said Sella.

And then she stopped working, which she almost never does, and looked at me properly.

“You went and worked out how much of him I need. And you took the number to him and he agreed to supply it.” She let that sit. “That’s not a friend coming to see me. That’s a delivery.”


I did not have an answer.

I understood, out over the water that night with the two calendars I had made and the eleven days of measurement and the entirely correct solution nobody wanted, that I had been trying to answer a question that is not a measurement.

**How much waiting is a friendship allowed to ask of somebody?**

There is no number. Not in counts and not in spans. Nobody in that valley has one and nobody in any valley has one, and I had gone and built a schedule as though there were.

I knew everything about both of them.

It gave me no authority over either of them at all.

## Chapter 24: What the Posts Are For

I met Tarr the day before the turning, because she was replacing a post and I was in the way.


She is a linekeeper. There are two in the valley and they maintain the boundary: keep the posts upright, keep the numbers legible, replace them when the river takes one, which it does about twice a year.

She is a hundred and eighty and she had a barrow and a mallet and absolutely no interest in me.

“Move.”

I moved.

She set the post, checked it against the two on either side by eye, adjusted it by an amount I could not detect, and checked it again.

“Does it matter that much?”

“Does what.”

“The exact position.”

Tarr straightened up and looked at me the way Sella looks at somebody who has asked why the boat needs tying.

“They’re forty paces apart,” she said. “All of them. All the way up both hills. If I put that one in wrong then in ninety years somebody’s got a post in the wrong place and no way of knowing which one’s the mistake.”

She went back to it.

I stayed, because I had been measuring things for weeks and I had noticed something and had not had anybody to ask.

“Can I ask you something about the line?”

“You can ask.”

“It’s exactly five to one.”

“Yes.”

“Not about five. Exactly. I’ve measured it at the ford and at both ends of the valley and it’s the same number in all three places.”

“Yes,” said Tarr.

“Why?”

She stopped, for the first time, and looked at me as though I had asked why water is wet.

“Because that’s what the posts are *for*.”

“I thought they were so you know where it is.”

“That’s what they’re for on the *outside*,” said Tarr. She put her hand flat on the top of the one she had just set. “They go down. They’re twice as long under as over. And they’re stone from up the head of the valley, not from here, and they’re forty paces apart. If you get one wrong,” she shrugged, “the edge goes soft.”

“Goes soft.”

“Slips. Wanders. Stops being five.” She said it the way you would say a fence sags. “Happened up at the north end when I was young. Post came out in a flood and nobody found it for a season, and the whole edge up there went... I don’t know. Four and a bit? Four and a half? Everybody’s clocks wrong, nobody could arrange anything, took two years to walk it back.”

“You *walked it back*.”

“We put the post in,” said Tarr. “It came back on its own after that. They do.”

“Could you make it four?”

Tarr looked at me.

“No idea.”

“Has anybody tried?”

“Not that I know of. We know how to put a post back in the old bed and let the edge come home. We don’t know how to make one, and we don’t know how to set it somewhere else.” She picked up the barrow. “Nobody alive does.”

“So you maintain it.”

“That’s the work. Everybody thinks we’re marking where the line is. We’re not. We’re holding it where it was set.”

## Chapter 25: A Made Thing

She said all of it in about the time it takes to lift a barrow, and she did not think she had said anything, and she was thinking about the next post before she had finished.

I did not say anything, because there was nothing to say to Tarr.

Somebody surveyed that line. Somebody chose five. Somebody quarried stone from the head of the valley and cut it and sank it twice as deep as it shows and set it forty paces apart all the way up both hills. Somebody has been maintaining it ever since, with no idea how to make another one or choose a different number, and there is a profession for it, and it is dull.

The edge between the two halves of that world **is a made thing**. Not a fact of the place. A thing somebody built and somebody else keeps.

---

I have crossed between worlds five times now. Five times into a black place with a white line in it and a shape that opens.

I have never once asked whether that edge was made.

I have a question I did not ask, and I did not ask it of Tarr, because Tarr was busy and because she would not have known what I was talking about.

## Chapter 26: The Turning

The turning came the next day and it went the way it always goes.

The river had shifted about two body-lengths against the near bank over the season, which meant the safe line across the ford had moved, which meant the stones had to be lifted and reset and the marks moved on both banks and the depths read again.

You cannot do it from one side. Somebody stands on the quick bank and reads the water and calls the measurements, and somebody on the long bank sets the far marks, and it takes a day, and it has been done that way for longer than anybody remembers.

Bern came down at first light, the way he always does.

They were good at it. That was the thing I had not expected.

I had been imagining something awkward: two people who had not properly spoken in eleven years, doing a difficult job together. It was nothing like that. They worked like one creature.

Sella stands in the water to about her middle, upstream of the crossing, with a marked pole. She reads the depth, calls it, moves three paces, reads it again. The calling has to be done in a particular way for a long to hear it: slower, lower, each number held. She does it without thinking about it, and it is the only time I heard her speak slowly.

Bern is on the far bank with the marks. He sets a stake where she tells him and looks up for the next one. From my side he barely appears to move at all; from his, I imagine, she was a blur going up and down the shallows shouting.

And the whole time there is a thing they do that nobody taught them. After every third call she stops and waits, without being asked, because that is how long it takes him to finish setting and look up. She does not count it out. She simply knows the length of it, the way you know how far away somebody is behind you by the sound of their feet.

They have done this thirteen times, which I know because Sella has all thirteen on her wall.

It took most of the day and it went perfectly, and about halfway through I understood that this was the only hour of any year in which the two of them were properly together, and that they had both been very carefully not saying so.

## Chapter 27: The Promise Was Wrong

And then, in the middle of the afternoon, with the far marks set and the light going yellow over the water, Bern said:

“You’ve gone short with me.”

Sella did not look up.

“I know.”

“Is it something I’ve done?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

And she told him. Not angrily. She told him the way she tells you the water level: eleven years, every turning, ten months at a time. What it is like to have somebody who is entirely reliable and almost never there. That she has never once been able to say it because there was nothing to accuse him of.

Bern listened to all of it, from the far bank, across the water.

Then he said: “I can’t do this every turning for the rest of your life.”

“I know.”

“I’ve never been able to.” He sat down on the bank, which took a while. “I didn’t know that when I said it. I was twenty.”

“I was three,” said Sella.


Neither of them apologized.

I have thought about that since. Neither of them said sorry and neither of them asked the other to, because there was nothing to be sorry for. He had kept the promise. She had kept the promise. The promise was the thing that was wrong.

They sat on opposite banks for a while, which is what that world has instead of sitting together.

I stayed and said nothing, which was the correct thing to do and the hardest thing I did on Vell.

## Chapter 28: What We Can Actually Keep

What they decided took about four days on her count and most of an afternoon on his, and I was there for some of it and not for the rest, which was right.

They did not find a better schedule. I had offered them one, and it was good, and they did not use it.

**Bern is not coming to the turnings any more.**

That was Sella’s idea and it startled me. There is a young quick down the bank who can read water nearly as well as she can and who wants the work, and the far marks can be set by anybody with a plumb line and an afternoon, and the truth is it was never the work that mattered.

The turning was just the thing the promise was tied to.

So Bern gave it up. And what he does instead is: he leaves word at the post whenever he passes it, which on his side is most days and on her side is often enough. Small tiles with almost nothing on them. *Wind’s up. Second crop looks poor. Saw a bird.*

And once a year, on no schedule at all, he comes down and does nothing useful whatsoever.

## Chapter 29: A Smaller Promise

It is a smaller *promise* than the one they had. It is not a smaller friendship. They are in each other’s way rather more than they were, now that neither of them is saving it all up for one day a season.

The old promise was enormous and it was about a job and it had *whatever else is happening* in it, and they made it when they were children and it lasted eleven years and it was slowly ruining a friendship neither of them wanted to lose.

This one is a bird, and a poor second crop, and one afternoon a year.

**It does not fix eleven years.** Nobody suggested it did. Sella still lived them and Bern still did not, and that is not a thing that gets settled.

But it is keepable, which the other one was not.


I stayed for a while after that.

There was riverstones at the line most evenings, which I had watched a dozen times and never played, and which I finally understood that week.

It is not really a game about stones. It is the one thing in that valley that both rates can do together without either of them having to pretend. A quick plays it slowly. A long plays it quickly. Both of them are doing something slightly unnatural, and the board sits in the middle, and for an hour or five hours nobody has to be the one making the accommodation.

Sella is very good at it. Bern is terrible and does not care.

They played twice that week, on opposite sides of the board at the line, and argued about the rules both times.

## Chapter 30: The Arithmetic Lying Down

I went up to the burial ground once, on the long side, because Ruck told me it was there and then would not come with me.

The stones are mixed. Quicks and longs in the same ground, because they lived in the same valley, and every stone has two dates on it in two columns the way everything there does.

You can read the whole shape of the world off that hillside. The quick stones come in dense little runs, four and five generations of a family in the space a single long takes up. The long stones sit far apart with decades of grass between them.

Nobody there finds it sad. It is simply the arithmetic, lying down.


The ferry runs the way it always did. Sella has six years of water written up on the boathouse wall, which is six years of records and not six years of anything else, and she is planning something with the landing that she explained to me twice. She is fifteen and she has a great deal of life ahead of her and she intends to have opinions about all of it.

Ruck is nearly grown and has taken work on the quick bank and is, I am told, extremely fast and quite bad at listening.

Tarr replaced two more posts.

The valley went on exactly as it had, which is the thing I have come to expect and which still surprises me every time.

## Chapter 31: The Real One

Bern walked me down to the ford on the last day, which took a long time and neither of us minded.

“You solved it,” he said.

“I didn’t.”

“You worked out what was wrong.”

“That’s not the same thing,” I said. “I worked out what was wrong and then I built you both a schedule and neither of you wanted it.”

Bern made a noise which I have decided was amusement.

“No,” he agreed. “But we’d not have got there without somebody standing between us saying numbers.” He looked out at the water. “You were wrong about the answer. You were right that there was a question.”

We went a little further down the track.

“Can I ask you something,” I said, “that isn’t about her?”

“Go on.”

“When you look at the far bank. At her side. Does it look wrong to you?”

Bern took a while over that, which with Bern means he was actually thinking rather than merely being slow.

“It looks *fast*,” he said. “It doesn’t look wrong. It looks like it’s going at the speed it goes at.” He shrugged, which on a long is an event. “Why?”

“Because I keep waiting for one of you to be the real one.”

“The real one.”

“The one whose year is the actual year. I’ve been here months and I still catch myself doing it, deciding that a year *really* means what it means to whichever of you I happen to be next to.”

“And which of us is it?”

“Whichever one I’m next to,” I said.

Bern laughed, properly, which took a while and was worth waiting for.

“Then you’ve got it,” he said. “That’s the whole of it. That’s what everybody here works out at about six.”

## Chapter 32: One More Time

Sella was on the boat.

“You’re going.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“You never do.” She coiled the rope. “Ruck says you’ve been asking Tarr about the posts.”

“I have.”

“Why?”

I thought about how to say it and could not, and said the true small part instead.

“Because somebody put them there.”

“Well, yes,” said Sella. “That’s what a post is.”

She pushed off, and took me across, and did not wait, and did not come back for anybody, which is what she says and which is not quite true, because she waited that once.

---

I crossed the line one more time on the way out.

It lurched, the way it did on the first day, the way it does for everybody. I know now what it is. I know exactly what it is, and what the number is, and which side is which.

I still cannot feel both of them at once.

Nobody can. That is not a failure of measurement and it is not something anybody there is doing wrong. It is the shape of the place.

Then the valley went small below me. The river ran at two speeds through the middle of it, and the line of posts went up over both hills, straight and evenly spaced, set there by somebody a very long time ago.

And I went forward.
