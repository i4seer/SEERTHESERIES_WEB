## Chapter 1: Nothing to Hold Down

I am a dot. I don’t know why.

I have been to ten worlds. On every single one of them there was something I could do.

On the last one, after the thing I could do was finished, I stayed for a while anyway. That was new.

I still don’t know how I get from one to the next.

This time I came out over a gorge.

---

It was a long way down and the bottom of it was a river, white in places, too far off to hear properly. The whole of one side of that gorge went up in steps.

Not steps you would walk on. Steps a field would stand on. Flat green shelves cut into the slope one above another all the way from the water to the ridge, and every single shelf was held up at the front by a wall of stacked stone. The walls were dry. No mortar in them anywhere, just stone laid on stone by somebody who had known what they were doing.

There were hundreds of them. There was more wall on that hillside than there was hillside.

I went along it in the light. It was early. Somebody a long way up was hitting something with something, unhurried, and the sound came across the gap and back off the far wall of the gorge a moment later.

I was pleased by it before I knew anything about it. I have learned to be careful with that.

---

They were up on the third shelf and there were four of them and they were taking a wall apart.

That was my first thought and it was wrong. They were putting it back together, which is a different operation conducted in the same direction.

They were upright and narrow, a long trunk, a small head with two eyes and a mouth in the ordinary places, and enormous flat feet that they set down on the stone with real deliberation, spread wide, gripping.

And their arms were too long.

They were not injuries. All four had them and they were all the same: long, jointed, reaching well past where you would expect an arm to stop. On two of them the effect was faintly ridiculous. On the other two it looked as though the arms were not going to be enough.

Nobody there thought anything of it. It is what a body looks like.

---

Here is what they were doing, and I watched the whole of it before I understood any of it.

One of them was up on the top of the wall, on the highest course, fast and light on the stone, lifting the old stones out and passing them down. That one talked continuously and moved constantly and got where it was going by the shortest route regardless of what was in the way.

Two were on the shelf behind, sorting what came down and leveling the bed with grit.

And the fourth was sitting on the course below the work, and doing nothing at all.

I watched it do nothing for a long time. It was not resting. Its whole attention was on the work above it, and it said things about the fit, and it did not get up and did not move and did not appear to be enjoying itself.

Then the wind came up the gorge.

---

It came up hard and all at once, the way wind does on a slope, and I felt it get under me and I went sideways about a body-length before I got hold of myself.

The one on the top course went sideways too. Not far. It grabbed the wall, said something short, and hung on with those long arms until the gust went through, and then said something considerably longer about the weather.

The one sitting on the lower course did not move.

I want to be exact: it did not brace, it did not lean, it did not hold on to anything. The wind arrived and went past it and the creature was in the same place afterward as before, and it had not been paying attention to the wind at all.

I thought: *those two are not the same weight.*

And then: *that is going to be the whole of this world.*

---

I got the arrangement out of the one on the top course, who was called Melly and who told me a great deal more than I asked for.

Every household on that hillside holds a weight. Not each person. The household, all together, has one quantity, and while the household stays the same, the quantity stays the same. They share it out.

“Fixed how?” I said.

“Fixed.” Melly was passing stones down while we talked and did not stop. “It’s what we’ve got. It doesn’t go up.”

“So if there are more of you...”

“Then there’s less each.” Melly said this the way you would say that if there is more water in the cup there is less in the jug. “There’s nine of us. I’m nothing. I can go anywhere and I can’t hold a thing down.”

“And that one?”

“Kesh?” Melly looked over the edge at the one sitting on the course. “Three of them. Kesh is *heavy*.”

Kesh, from below, said something about people who talk instead of working.

---

I spent the rest of that morning establishing the parts nobody explains.

**Heavy does not mean strong.** I got that wrong first and had it corrected twice. Kesh could not lift a stone Melly could not lift. Kesh could sit on a stone and not be moved off it, which is a different property and, on that hillside, a much more valuable one.

**Heavy does not mean slow.** Kesh walked along the shelf at exactly the speed Melly walked along the shelf. Nobody on Cadd trudges. On the flat they are all the same.

Where weight costs you is **up**.

I found that out by asking Kesh to come up to the fifth shelf with me, and being laughed at, and then being told the reason: going up is work against your own weight, and Kesh does not do more of it in a day than Kesh has to.

**Melly goes up.** Melly is on the top course, on the ladders, on the high shelves where nothing is anchored, because Melly is nine ways divided and the wind is the only real danger up there and Melly has been blown off two things already this year and finds this funny.

“You want the light ones high and the heavy ones low,” Melly said. “Everybody knows that.”

“What if you want somebody heavy high?”

Melly stopped what it was doing, for the first time all morning, and looked at me as though I had asked whether you could have a wall made out of water.

“Why would you want that,” it said.

---

By the middle of the day I had the world and had begun to be pleased with myself, and then I offered to help and found out what I was.

They were setting a stone. Kesh was on the course below, holding it still with the fact of being there, and the stone above needed a hand to steady it while the bed took, and one of the two on the shelf had gone off up the terrace for grit.

I said I would hold it.

There was a pause of a particular kind. I have had that pause on four worlds and I have never enjoyed it.

“You’d hold it,” said Melly.

“Yes.”

“With what?”

I explained, as I have explained on a number of worlds, that I do not have anything. No arms, no hands, nothing to grip with. What I have is that I can be somewhere and not leave.

“Right,” said Melly, kindly. “And how much do you weigh?”

I did not have an answer to that, and I hung there in the air over their wall while four people looked at me, and then Kesh said, without any unkindness whatsoever:

“There’s nothing you can do here.”

---

It was true, and it took me until the evening to accept how completely it was true.

Everything on that hillside is holding something up. Every field is standing on a wall and every wall wants a course under it that does not move while it is being built. That is the work. That is what there is. And the entire question, all day, every day, on every shelf, is **who is heavy enough to sit on the thing while somebody else does something to it.**

I have never been on a world where I could not find an angle. On the first one I could measure. On the third I could lie, because nobody there could read me. On the last one I could hear, which nobody else could.

Here I could look at things.

Nothing on Cadd needs looking at. They can all see perfectly well.

---

I went up in the evening and put myself over the middle of the Fetch, which is what the hillside is called, and watched the light go up the gorge.

Two days’ walk end to end. Four or five households on it and the walls of a thousand years. Somebody on the top shelf was still hitting something with something.

And down on the second shelf, in a doorway too narrow for two people to pass in, an extremely heavy person was standing in the way of two other people who wanted to get past, and could not be moved, and knew it.

The argument went on for a considerable time. Nobody laid a hand on anybody. Nobody needed to. One of them fetched a third person to take a view, and the third person took a view, and the heavy one stayed exactly where it was and continued the conversation from there.

It was the funniest thing I had seen in a month and I could not have contributed to it in any way at all.

## Chapter 2: The Arithmetic

Nobody on the Fetch says hello.

What they say is how many they are.

I had heard it a dozen times before I understood it, because it sounds like a greeting and is used like one. Two people meet on a shelf, the first says *four of us*, the second says *nine*, and then they talk about whatever they were going to talk about.

They are telling each other how many ways it divides, which is the same fact as what they weigh, arriving from the other end. Say *nine of us* and I know you are light, I know you can go high, I know not to ask you to sit on anything, and I know the shape of your household without one rude question.

It is the most efficient thing I have ever heard anybody say.

---

And once I had it, I could not stop hearing the other one.

*Four of us still.*

The *still* means we were more, and the four of us are heavier than we were, and everybody can see it. It is a thing you will now say to every person you meet for the rest of your life, every time you say hello.

The first time I heard it I asked, because I am like that. The person said *last spring*, and I said I was sorry, and she said thank you, and then told me a long story about a fence.

You cannot hide a death on Cadd. The survivors get heavier the same day and the whole hillside can see it, so there is no announcing and no concealing and no interval in which somebody does not yet know.

I have been on worlds where that would be a kindness. I could not work out whether it is one there.

---

I had the pronouns wrong for two days, which was entirely my own doing.

The Caddin do not use their bodies to tell you what they are. They tell you when they introduce themselves, in the same breath as the number, and I had not been listening for it because I was busy being impressed with the number. Melly had told me. Kesh had told me twice.

Sevil corrected me on my third morning. Not sharply. She corrected me as you would tell somebody their bag was open.

“She,” she said. “You’ve been doing it all morning.”

“I didn’t know.”

“I know you didn’t,” said Sevil. “That’s why I said.”

---

I met her because she was doing sums out loud and I was the only thing on the shelf that stopped.

She was sitting on a stone at the top of the second terrace with a stick and a patch of dust, and she was working through something with numbers in it, and every so often she rubbed the dust flat and started again.

I hung there and watched her do it three times.

“You’re the one they say has no weight.”

“Yes.”

“None at all?”

“None.”

Sevil put the stick down. It was the first time anybody on that hillside had looked at me with interest, and the interest was not friendly. It was the interest of somebody handed a number they have not seen before.

“Nothing?”

“Nothing at all.”

“How does that work? Are you...” She stopped and started again, which is what Sevil does when she has three questions and is choosing which. “Whose are you?”

“Nobody’s.”

“You’ve got no household.”

“No.”

“Then where’s your share come from?”

“I don’t have one.”

She looked at me for a while.

“I’ve never heard of that,” she said, and picked the stick back up.

---

The Ost are five.

Sevil, her brother Terrik, Terrik’s wife Dess, and two children: Tibb, who is about seven, and Oad, who is about four and who has not yet said a straight sentence to me.

They were eight, four years ago.

Nobody told me that. Tibb told me the whole thing on the second afternoon in the way children do, as a piece of interesting information about the world.

The Ost hold the same weight they held when there were eight, and now it divides five ways.

**They are the heaviest household on the Fetch.**

That is not a sad fact there. It is a fact with consequences.

---

The consequence is that the Ost hold the upper-terrace contracts.

Wall-work goes to whoever can sit on it. The upper terraces take the worst of the weather and want the most rebuilding, and the Ost are heavy, so the Ost have the work. Every season, all of it, more of it each year because Sevil is good at getting it.

And there are five of them, and two of those are children.

That is not about weight. Tibb is as heavy as anybody in that house. The share does not care how old you are. What children do not do is **stand**. A turn is four hours of not moving on a live course with somebody’s field above you, and nobody on the Fetch puts a child on one, and nobody has ever had to explain why not.

Dess works the stack, which is a full day and a walk up.

Which leaves Sevil and Terrik.

“He does two turns,” Sevil told me, in the middle of explaining something else entirely. “He’s done two turns since the year we were eight. Every wall, all season.”

“Is that a lot?”

“It’s twice,” said Sevil.

---

I asked Terrik about it that evening and got the flattest answer anybody has ever given me.

He was on the wall at the time, sitting on a course, not moving, in the last of the light, and he did not turn round.

“It’s sitting down,” he said.

“Sevil says it’s twice what anybody does.”

“Sevil says a lot of things.”

“Is she wrong?”

Terrik thought about that for the length of time it takes to watch somebody set a stone badly and decide not to say anything about it.

“No,” he said.

Then he said that what he would actually like is to work the stack, which is at the top of the Fetch where they square the stone, and which is loud and full of people and where all the news is. And that he cannot, because getting up there is an hour of climbing against his own weight and he is worth more down here, and that this is the arithmetic and there is nothing wrong with it.

He did not say it as a complaint. He said it the way you mention the weather in a place you have decided not to leave.

---

Tibb has a scheme.

Tibb has several. She is seven, has worked out the whole arithmetic, finds it outrageous, and proposes ways around it. They are all variations on one idea, which is that there should be fewer of them.

“If there were four of us,” she said, “we’d all be heavier.”

“There are five of you.”

“I *know* there are five of us.” She said it as though I had missed the point on purpose. “I’m saying if there were four.”

“Which four?”

Tibb opened her mouth and then did not say anything, and I watched a seven-year-old walk the whole length of a sum she had started for fun and arrive somewhere she had not intended to go.

She did not say it then. She said it at the table that evening, out loud, in front of everybody, because she had gone on thinking about it and had got to the bottom of it and it was, arithmetically, correct.

Dess went the way you go.

Tibb was told, at some length, that this is not a thing anybody says. She had meant nothing by it whatsoever and had simply followed the sum where it went. She was baffled and then embarrassed and then sulky in the ordinary order.

Nobody explained why. On Cadd you would not have to.

---

Oad sings.

He is four and he has one song and it is a counting song and every child on the Fetch has it. It goes through the numbers and it puts a household to each one, and the whole point of it is the last line, which comes back round to *and whose are you*, and which the child is supposed to answer with their own number.

Oad sang it at me eleven times in one afternoon. He wanted me to answer.

I could not, and he found this so funny that he made me not-answer it another four times.

It is a lovely song. Everybody there thinks so. Adults smile when they hear it, the smile you give a thing you have known since before you could talk, and nobody has ever once looked at it and heard what it says, which is that **the number you carry tells you whose you are.**

It says it plainly. It says it in the last line. It is the only piece of philosophy on that hillside and it is sung by four-year-olds to make adults smile.

---

There was a marriage being negotiated three terraces over the whole time I was there, and I went and watched some of it, because I was not doing anything else.

It was accountancy.

Two households, nine people between them, on the ground with dust and sticks. Not a word about affection. That was not in question and had been settled long before.

What was in question was **the pool**. When they marry, the two totals go together and re-divide across everybody in both households, and that is what a marriage *is* there. One side is six ways divided and light. The other is three ways and heavy. Afterward everybody is somewhere in the middle, and the three-way side is losing a great deal.

They argued about it for a month. Cheerfully. In public. With everybody on the Fetch offering opinions.

And in among all of it, the sticks, the dust, the numbers, the shouting, is a fact that nobody there has ever had cause to look at directly:

**The weight moves because they say it does.**

Two families sit down, agree a thing out loud, and the amount of world that pushes on nine bodies changes.

I watched the whole afternoon and thought it was the most interesting arrangement I had ever seen, and did not think anything else about it at all.

---

Sevil was still on her stone when I came back, doing the sums again.

“They’ve settled the Rennick marriage,” I said.

“They’ve settled nothing. That’s the Vessel girl and it’ll go another three weeks.” She rubbed the dust flat. “Say that again.”

“Which part?”

“About the weight.”

I had not said anything about the weight. What I had said, walking up the terrace, mostly to myself, was that it was strange to watch a quantity move because two people had agreed it should.

“It moves because they say it,” I said. “That’s all.”

Sevil sat and looked at her patch of dust for a length of time I did not interrupt.

Then she said, “Hm,” and rubbed it flat again, and did not tell me what she was thinking.

## Chapter 3: Spoken In

Here is how you rebuild a course, and I am going to set it out properly.

A wall on the Fetch holds up a field. No mortar. Stone laid on stone, and it works for eight or ten years and then the bottom of it starts to go, and if you leave it the field comes down into the gorge and takes the next three fields with it.

So you take the failing course out and put a new one in, and it goes like this.

**One.** The old stones come out, one at a time, working along. You do not take out more than you are going to put back that day.

**Two.** The bed is leveled. Grit and small stone, packed, and this is the fiddly part and the part everybody argues about.

**Three.** Somebody heavy sits on the course below the work and does not move.

**Four.** The new stone goes in on the leveled bed while that person sits there.

**Five.** You do it again, along the wall, all season.

Step three is the whole of it.

---

I did not understand step three until Sevil made me look at a wall built without it.

There is one on the fourth shelf, nine years old, put up by a household that was short that season and had nobody to sit. It failed at two years. They patched it. It failed again.

“Look at the line,” said Sevil.

It bellies. Not much. You would not see it from the shelf above, but along a hundred paces the courses have crept out and down, and every stone has moved a finger’s width from where it was put.

“An unweighted course walks,” she said. “You set a stone on it, and the course takes the stone, and the whole line shifts along by nothing at all. Do that four hundred times and you have got that.”

“Nobody can see it.”

“The gorge can see it,” said Sevil.

---

That is what a heavy person is for, and why the Ost have the upper terraces, and why Terrik does two turns.

A turn is four hours of not moving on a live course with somebody’s field above you. Not lifting anything. Sitting on a stone with your whole attention on the work above your head.

I watched Terrik do a full one and it was the dullest thing I have ever paid close attention to. At the end he got up, walked along the shelf at his ordinary speed, said the wall was going well, and went and did the second one.

---

I was there when Sevil said it, and she said it to Dess first, which I did not expect.

Dess had come down from the stack and was doing something to her feet, which after a day up there is necessary and agony, and Sevil came in and sat down and said:

“You know the Vessel marriage.”

“Everybody knows the Vessel marriage.”

“The weight moves because they say it does.”

Dess stopped what she was doing.

“They say it,” said Sevil, “in front of people, out loud, and it moves. Nobody does anything to anybody. There is nothing under it. Two households agree a thing out loud and the amount of world that pushes on nine people changes.”

“So?”

“So who says it has to be two households.”

---

Dess got there before I did, and Dess is the one everybody in that house treats as the pessimist.

“You want to put the floating thing in the count.”

“I want to know if we can.”

“And then what?”

“And then it can sit a course,” said Sevil, “and Terrik does one turn.”

“And then what?” said Dess again.

She was not repeating herself. She was asking the second half of the same question and everybody in the room, including me, heard it as the first half said twice.

Sevil said: “Then we get the season done.”

And Dess said, “That’s not what I asked,” and went back to her feet.

Terrik came in about four minutes later and said he did not mind, which is what Terrik says.

That was not the same as deciding it.

---

Sevil did the sum in front of everybody before anybody agreed to anything.

She did not persuade anybody. She got the dust and the stick and did the arithmetic out loud on the floor of her own house with all five of them there and me over the table.

“Five ways now,” she said. “Six ways after.”

“How much less?” said Dess.

“A sixth. Each. All of us.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. A sixth of an Ost share is not a small quantity, because an Ost share is not. They were about to be a sixth lighter each, every one of them, in a way the whole Fetch would see inside a week.

Terrik said: “How much can it sit?”

“That’s the question.” Sevil put two marks in the dust and then a third beside them. “Standing, we’re two. Me and you. Tibb doesn’t stand, Oad doesn’t stand, Dess is up the stack all day. Two carriers is what this house has got and two is what it’s had since the year we were eight.”

“And after?”

“Three. All three of us a sixth lighter than we are now.” She looked at the marks. “Three at five-sixths is more standing than two at whole. That’s the sum. That’s the whole of the sum, and it’s not a big one, and it’s the difference between the upper terrace in one season and the upper terrace in two.”

Dess said, from the other side of the room: “It’s a quarter more.”

“It’s a quarter more,” Sevil agreed.

“And if it can’t sit a turn?”

“Then we’ve made ourselves lighter for nothing,” said Sevil, “and I’ll have been an idiot in public.”

Sevil looked at Terrik.

“Yes,” he said.

Then she looked at Dess.

“I think it’s a bad idea,” Dess said.

Sevil waited.

“That’s not no,” Dess said.

“No.”

“It isn’t.”

That was the household’s answer.

---

Tibb wanted to know if it would work on a rock.

She had understood the proposal immediately and wanted to know whether, if you can speak a floating thing into a household, you can speak *a rock* into one, and get a member who weighs a great deal and eats nothing.

She was told no. She asked why not. Nobody could tell her.

“It has to be somebody,” said Dess.

“Why?”

“Because a rock isn’t anybody.”

“But *why*,” said Tibb, and got sent to bed, which is how that conversation ends on every world I have been to.

---

They did it the next morning at the stack, because that is where people are.

There is no ceremony and not even a form of words. What there is is the requirement that it be **said out loud in front of people who are paying attention**. A marriage has the same requirement. So does a separation. That is the entire legal apparatus of that world.

Sevil said what it was for. She stood at the stack with about twenty people listening and said the Ost were taking me into their count, that I would carry a share, and that the extra carrier would be used for wall-work on the upper terraces. The Ost would be a sixth lighter each from today.

Somebody asked whether that was wise.

Sevil said it was arithmetic and not wisdom, and that anybody who wanted to check her numbers was welcome to.

Then she looked at me.

“You’re taking the share?”

I thought about the upper walls and about Terrik doing two turns.

“Yes.”

“Right,” said Sevil.

Nobody said anything about family. Somebody made a joke about the Ost being desperate and somebody else said something about the price of a hand these days, and everybody laughed, including Sevil.

Then she said the thing, which was four words long, and the whole hillside got on with its morning.

---

I did not feel anything.

That is worth saying because I had expected to. I had been braced for the last hour without admitting it, and what happened was nothing: no shift, no arrival, no sensation of any kind.

Then I went to put myself over the stack the way I had been all morning, and I went down instead.

Not far. Not fast. I was where I had been and then I was a hand’s width lower, and I put myself back up and it took *effort*, which is a thing I have never once had to do.

I have floated on eleven worlds. It is not a skill and it is not work. It is the condition of being me.

Now there was something to hold up.

---

They put me on a course before the end of the day, because Sevil is not sentimental and there was a wall.

Here is the thing itself, and I am going to be exact about it.

You do not stand on a course. I have no legs. What I did was come down onto the stone until I was properly resting on it, in contact, the way a cup rests on a table, and then **stop holding myself up.**

That is all it is. You stop holding yourself up and the thing you have been given goes down through you into the stone.

The stone did not move.

Terrik set four stones above me in the next hour and the course did not walk a hair, and when he was done he got down off the wall and looked at it and then looked at me and said:

“Huh.”

---

Sevil had a word for it by the evening.

The Fetch’s word is *standing*, and everybody kept using it about me, and it kept being slightly wrong in a way that made Tibb correct people at length. So Sevil made one.

“Setting on,” she said. “You’re not standing. You’re setting on it.”

“Is that a word?”

“It is now,” said Sevil. “Say it enough and it’ll be one by the autumn.”

---

Here is what that day was.

For eleven worlds I have not been able to hold, push, lift, or steady one single thing. I have looked at things until I understood them and then told somebody else, and they have done it, or not, with their hands. On Sull I could not open a door. On Ebb I directed eleven people to seal a shed for a day and a half and did not touch any part of it.

On the Fetch, on my eleventh day, I set on a stone and the stone did not move, because I was there.

I did four hours the next morning and I would have done eight.

Oad sang at me that evening. He got to the last line and waited, as he always did.

“Six,” I said.

He laughed and started the song again.

It was the first time I had ever had an answer for him. I noticed the number. I did not notice the rest of the question.

---

And there was one more thing, and I found it on the second day, and it changed what I was worth.

The high shelves are the trouble on that hillside. They take the weather and want the most work, and the people who can get up there are light and the people who can hold a course are heavy, and those are never the same person. Kesh does not go above the third shelf. Terrik goes up twice a season and it costs him a day either side.

I went up in about a minute and a half.

I floated up the way I have always gone up. Weight makes it harder, but it does not prevent it. It prevents *climbing*, and I do not climb. I came down onto a course on the seventh shelf and stopped holding myself up, and I was heavy up there.

Nobody on Cadd can be heavy up there.

Sevil came up to see it. It took her most of an hour and she was not pleased about the hour, and she stood on that shelf getting her breath back and looked at me set on the top course of the highest wall on the Fetch, and I watched her do the sum.

She did not say anything about it at all. She got her breath back, and looked at the wall, and looked at the shelf above it, and went back down.

## Chapter 4: Take the North Wall

“Take the north wall this afternoon.”

That was the first one. Six words. Sevil said it on her way past with a bundle of grit on her shoulder and did not stop walking.

I said yes.

There was nothing in it to say anything else to. The north wall was open, the light was good, and I had spent eleven worlds being unable to do one useful thing and had just been handed an afternoon of doing one.

---

The north wall took four hours and I would have done eight.

Here is what four hours of standing is, from inside it: you come down onto the course, you stop holding yourself up, and then you do not do anything else at all. That is the entire job. Somebody works above you. The stone goes where it goes. Every so often the person above you says something and you say something back.

Three separate people told me this is the worst work on the Fetch.

I could not make that fit until I watched Terrik do one. For him it is four hours of his body being used as an object, in the cold, doing the one thing he cannot get out of, while people who can move about do work with some variety in it.

For me it was four hours of *being able to affect something*.

He was doing his second turn while I did my first. When he came off the wall he came along the shelf and looked at my course for a while, and then said:

“You’re better at it than me.”

“I’m not. It’s the same weight.”

“It’s not the weight,” said Terrik. “You *like* it.”

And he went home.

---

The second thing happened four days later and it was not really a request.

Sevil was setting a stone on the third shelf and the bed was not taking and she was down on the course herself, which she does not usually have time for, and she said, without turning round:

“Hold this while I set it.”

So I came down on the far end of the stone and stopped holding myself up, and she set it, and she said “good” and moved along and did the next one.

That is the whole of the second one. Three seconds. If you asked me to list the things that happened to me on Cadd I would not have put it in.

---

Every morning there was a wall. Somebody wanted a course held and I could hold it, and at the end of the day the wall was longer than it had been and part of the reason was me. Not my advice. Not something I had worked out and told somebody else to do. **Me, sitting on a stone.**

I ate nothing, I did not get cold, and I did not get bored, which turns out to be the thing nobody had thought to want. A Caddin sitting a turn is losing four hours of a life. I was not losing anything.

I got faster at coming down. There is a way of doing it where you arrive on the stone already stopped, and Melly said it looked like a bird landing, and Kesh said it looked like a sack going down, and I have decided that Melly is right.

---

Dess did not like it and would not say why.

She said things like *hm*, and *if it holds*, and once, when Sevil was going through what could be done with the season now, she said, “You’re building it into the sums.”

“Of course I’m building it into the sums.”

“That’s all,” said Dess, and went up to the stack.

---

The high shelves came about ten days in.

The seventh shelf had two hundred paces of wall that had not been touched in six years, because it is at the top and everything about it is expensive. To get a heavy person up there you spend a day on the climb, a day standing, and a day getting them down again and letting them recover, and you can do about a third of the work you would do in the same three days lower down.

I went up in a minute and a half.

Then I came down onto the course and stopped holding myself up and sat there being heavy on the highest wall on the Fetch, and Terrik set stone above me for a full turn, and at the end of it we had done a day’s work at a height where a day’s work costs three.

That was the thing that changed what I was.

Because I am not a third carrier. I am a third carrier **who can be heavy where nobody else can get to.** The Fetch has three or four shelves at the top that everybody has been managing rather than maintaining for as long as anybody has been alive, and all of them are wall, and all of that wall wants somebody sitting on it.

Sevil came up to look. It took her most of an hour and she arrived out of breath and did not say anything for a while.

Then she said: “How long can you sit up here?”

“As long as you like.”

“Days?”

“I don’t get tired.”

And she stood on the seventh shelf and looked out along two hundred paces of unrepaired wall, and I watched her stop thinking about this season.

---

The third one came at the end of that week and it was the easiest of the three, and I said yes to it faster than I said yes to either of the others.

Terrik was ill.

Not badly. He had something that had gone round the Fetch and everybody had it for four days and complained about it enormously, and he was in the house looking irritated and being told to sit still.

Sevil said: “Cover Terrik’s turn.”

I said yes before she had finished the sentence.

---

Here is exactly what I thought.

*Terrik is ill, and there is a wall, and I can set on it.*

I thought: *this is one afternoon.*

And then I did not think anything else about it at all, because there was nothing else in it. Somebody was ill. Somebody covered. That is what happens in a house.

The word she used was *turn*.

I heard *a turn*. Meaning one. Meaning this one. Meaning the afternoon in front of us.

I went and did it, and the wall got four stones longer, and it was a good afternoon.

## Chapter 5: Cover Terrik’s Turn

Terrik was better in three days and I did the next one anyway.

He had been ill. It seemed reasonable. He did not ask me to and neither did Sevil. I went up to the wall because it was the morning and there was a wall, and Terrik was on the shelf sorting stone and looked at me settling onto the course and did not say anything about it.

That was the fourth.

The fifth was two days after that, on the fifth shelf, and nobody asked me about it at all. There was a course open and no one on it and I was there, so I came down on it and stopped holding myself up, and somebody I did not know very well set stone above me for three hours and thanked me at the end and went home.

I did not think about either of them. There was nothing in them to think about.

---

The Rennicks split that month and the whole Fetch watched it.

Nine of them, in one house, and they had been nine for eleven years and had got to the point that households get to. Nobody died. Nobody did anything. What happened is that two of the Rennicks wanted to work the low ground and four of them did not, and it had been an argument for two years, and one evening it stopped being an argument and became a separation.

Which on Cadd is a negotiation.

---

A separation there is not what it is anywhere else I have been.

When a household splits, the total splits. Not evenly. **How it splits is the entire content of the separation**, and it is done in public, with sticks and dust, over weeks, in front of anybody who wants to come and watch. Everybody wants to come and watch.

There is no authority. There is nobody to appeal to. There are two sets of people, some sticks, a great deal of dust, and the requirement that whatever they arrive at be said out loud in front of witnesses.

They did it in nineteen days.

---

I went to most of it and I enjoyed it enormously.

It is the best entertainment on that hillside. People bring food. Old Caddin who have not left their shelves in a season come out for the good afternoons. There is a running commentary from about forty people, none of whom have any standing in the matter and all of whom have a position on it, and there are two or three moments in every separation where somebody who is not a Rennick at all says something so useful that both sides stop and consider it.

Sevil went every day and did not say much. She sat at the back and watched the arithmetic.

Tibb came once and was bored, which she found humiliating, because she had expected to enjoy it and everybody had promised her it was interesting and it was mostly four adults saying the same numbers to each other in different orders.

---

What they settled was two ways from nine.

Five keep the house and the upper ground and the larger part of the total. Four take the low ground and a smaller part, and will be lighter than they were, which is what they wanted. Low ground does not want holding. The work on it is reaching and carrying and going up and down a ladder forty times a day, all of which a light Caddin can do and a heavy one pays for.

Both sides said it out loud on the twentieth morning in front of about sixty people, and then everybody ate.

And that was the day the lower terrace came free.

---

Nobody did anything underhanded about it.

What came free was the *lower terrace*: a long stretch of good ground on the second shelf that the Rennicks had held the wall contracts on for a generation, and which the five who kept the house could no longer work. They were heavier after the split, and they were also fewer. The terrace wants two carriers through the season, and they had exactly two, both needed on their own ground.

So they gave it up, in public, at the same meeting, in the same breath as everything else, because that is how things are done there.

Everybody on that hillside knew within the hour that the lower terrace was going.

---

Sevil did not go and get it that day.

I watched her not do it. She went home. She did the sums for four days. She talked to Terrik about it twice that I heard and once that I did not, and both of the times I heard, Terrik said some version of *if you think so*.

She talked to Dess about it once and that one went differently.

“How many carriers has it got to have?”

“Two,” said Sevil.

“And how many have we got?”

“Three.”

“You’ve got two,” said Dess.

There was a pause of a particular kind.

“I’ve got three,” said Sevil.

“You’ve got two and a thing that helps.”

“It’s carrying a share, Dess. It’s in the count. That’s three.”

And Dess said, in what sounded to me like a remark about arithmetic: “It’s in the count for the wall season.”

“Yes.”

“Right,” said Dess, and went up to the stack.

---

I was in that room. I heard all of it.

I did not think it was about me in any way that required me to say something. They were doing sums about ground, and I was one of the numbers in the sums, and being a number in somebody’s sums was the most useful thing that had ever happened to me.

If you had asked me, that evening, whether I had made anybody a promise, I would have said no. And I would have been right.

Nobody had asked me for one. Nobody on that hillside asks anybody for one. It did not occur to me to wonder what the afternoons added up to for anybody else, because I did not know they added up at all.

---

The turns went on.

There is not a great deal to say about that month, which is the point. I did the north wall and the fifth shelf and the seventh twice. Terrik did one turn most days instead of two and started coming up to the stack in the afternoons, which he had not done in four years, and came back down with the news and told it badly.

Dess said the sitting had done something to his color. Terrik said it had not. Dess said it had.

Tibb had a new scheme, which was that the Ost should acquire a second floating thing, and she was extremely serious about it and wanted to know where I had come from so that they could get another one. I said I did not know. She took this as evasion and pursued it for two days.

Oad sang.

And the wall on the seventh shelf went two hundred paces along, in weeks, at a height where nobody had done more than patching in six years. Light people, who could get there, came up to look at it and stood about on the shelf saying that they had never seen the seventh done.

---

The only strange moment in the whole month was small and I did not do anything with it.

I was on a course on the sixth shelf, in the middle of a turn, not moving, with nothing to do but look at things.

And I thought: *this is a thing somebody said out loud.*

Not the wall. The weight. Sevil had stood at the stack and said four words, and the amount of world that pushes on me had changed, and it was still changed, and every stone I had held since had stayed where it was because of a sentence.

I turned that over for about half a turn and got nowhere with it, and then Terrik dropped a stone and swore at it and I stopped thinking about it entirely.

## Chapter 6: The Second Holding

The lower terrace changed hands eleven days after the Rennicks settled.

They did it at the stack in the middle of the day, because a holding is the largest thing most people ever take and it wants people to have seen it happen. There were fifty or sixty there. The Rennicks who were giving it up stood on one side, and Sevil stood on the other, and old Vessel witnessed it because Vessel witnesses everything and enjoys it.

I was over the back of the crowd where I could see.

---

There is a form to it and Sevil went through the whole thing.

She said what she was taking: the lower terrace, the second shelf, from the elbow to the Rennick corner, with the wall contracts that go with it.

She said what she was paying, and when, and out of what.

And then she said what she had, because that is the part that matters. A holding is not given to whoever wants it. It is given to whoever can work it, and the way you demonstrate that you can work it is that you stand up in front of sixty people and count.

“The terrace wants two on the wall through the season,” said Sevil. “I’ve got three carriers.”

Somebody said: “You’ve got two and the floating thing.”

“I’ve got three carriers,” said Sevil. “It’s in the count. It’s been sitting the seventh shelf since the spring, which none of you can do, and it has done two hundred paces up there in two months at a height where Kesh won’t go above the third. Anybody who thinks that’s two carriers can come up and look at the wall.”

Nobody came up to look at the wall. Several people said things. The general view, as far as I could tell, was that Sevil Ost had got herself something nobody had thought of and was going to do very well out of it, and that this was either enterprising or lucky depending on who was talking.

Nobody said she was wrong about the arithmetic.

**And she was not wrong about the arithmetic.**

---

Then she took it, and paid what was due that day, and Vessel said the words, and it was done in front of sixty people and could not be undone.

I want to set down exactly what she had done, because it was not a gamble.

She had a household with three carriers in it. She had two months of evidence. She had a wall on the seventh shelf that everybody could see and nobody else could have built. She had said, publicly, months earlier, exactly what the arrangement was for, and nobody had contradicted her then and nobody contradicted her now.

She took ground she could work with what she had.

Every part of that is true.

---

Dess was at the back and did not come forward.

She had come down from the stack for it, which she did not have to do. She stood at the edge of the crowd with her arms folded and watched the whole of it and did not say anything to anybody, and when it was finished she went back up.

She did not look at me. I noticed that she did not look at me, which is a thing you only notice about somebody who usually does.

---

Terrik was pleased.

He came over afterward and was as close to cheerful as Terrik gets, and he said that with the lower terrace they could put me on the seventh through the wet and take the low walls themselves, and that if it went the way Sevil thought it would go, next year he might get a season where he was not on a wall every day of it.

“Two years of that,” he said, “and I could go up the stack for good.”

He has wanted that since before I arrived. It has been the only thing he has ever wanted out loud.

And he said it to me, standing in the yard, on the day the ground was taken, as a thing that was now going to be possible.

---

It was nine days after that, on a Tuesday, and the weather was going to turn.

Sevil was standing in the yard with a list. She had the season on it: everybody’s days, the ground, which walls came first if the wet arrived early. She was going through it out loud the way she does, half to Terrik and half to herself, correcting as she went.

“Sixth shelf Monday if it holds. Then the north end. Dess, you’re at the stack all week, they want the long stone squared before the wet. Terrik, you’re on the sixth with me Monday and Tuesday.”

She turned a page over.

“Your turn’s Thursday.”

Then she said something about the long stone, and Terrik said something back about the long stone, and the two of them had a short argument about whether the stack had enough of it, and Dess came out of the house and joined in on Terrik’s side, and the three of them went off up the yard still talking about it.

---

I did not say anything.

I hung where I was, over the yard, looking at the wall opposite, and did not move for some time.

*Your turn’s Thursday.*

Not *would you take Thursday*. Not *there’s a wall on Thursday*. Not *could you*.

**Your turn.** Mine. A thing that exists and comes round and has a day attached to it, and which she was reading off a list, in order, along with Dess’s week at the stack and Terrik’s Monday on the sixth shelf.

She was not asking me. She had not been asking for some time. She was **telling me a day**, the way you tell somebody a day when the arrangement is already understood, and the reason she was telling me at all was that I needed to know which day.

---

I went back through it.

That is not the right way to say it. I did not go back through it; the whole of it arrived at once, the way a thing does when it has been sitting in front of you and you have been looking at the wrong part of it.

*Take the north wall this afternoon.* I said yes.

*Hold this while I set it.* I said yes.

*Cover Terrik’s turn.* I said yes.

And then the fourth one, which nobody asked. And the fifth, which nobody asked. And a month of them, which nobody asked, because by then there was nothing to ask.

I had thought I was saying yes to afternoons.

**Everybody else had watched me take a place in a rota.**

Not because anybody decided it. **Everybody on the Fetch understands a share that way.** You carry a household’s weight and the household’s work is your work, and to every single person on that hillside those are not two things that happen to go together. They are the same thing, said from two ends. Nobody has ever had to say otherwise, because nobody has ever carried a share who did not already understand it that way.

They had not been asking me because you do not ask. You tell a person which day.

---

The second thing arrived about a minute after the first and it was worse.

Nine days ago Sevil had stood at the stack in front of sixty people and counted three carriers, and taken ground on that count, and paid for it.

She had not been exaggerating. She had not been chancing it. **She had been counting me**, in the ordinary way, the way she counts Terrik, because as far as she knew, as far as anybody there knew, I had spent two months demonstrating exactly what I was.

I had watched her do it. I had been over the back of the crowd and had thought it was a fine piece of arithmetic.

---

Sevil had not misled me. She had told the whole hillside what she was doing, out loud, at the stack, in front of twenty people, and she had said what the share was for. I had heard her say it. I had been there.

What she had not said, what nobody had said and nobody would ever think to say, is that **to everybody there, the share and the work are the same object.**

That is not a term of an arrangement. To them it is what an arrangement is.

---

I did the Thursday.

Of course I did. It was Thursday and there was a wall, and whatever I had worked out on Tuesday did not change either of those, and I had not decided anything.

I set on the course on the sixth shelf for four hours and Terrik set stone above me and said the wet was coming in early, and at the end of it he got down and looked at the run of the course and said it was going well.

## Chapter 7: Not Mine to Keep Doing

I did four more days and then I did not do the fifth, and that is the whole of the event.

There was no scene. Sevil came out in the morning and said the lower terrace wanted starting and that I should take the elbow end, and I said no.

She stopped.

“Say that again.”

“I’m not going to take the elbow end.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Is it the height? Because it’s low ground, it’s the easiest...”

“It isn’t the height,” I said. “I’m not going to do the season.”

---

Sevil put down what she was carrying, which on Sevil is the equivalent of somebody else shouting.

“You’re in the count.”

“I know.”

“You’ve been in the count since the spring. You carry a share. Everybody on this hillside knows you carry a share.” She was not raising her voice at all, and I would have preferred it if she had. “What do you think a share *is*?”

“I think it’s weight.”

“It’s weight and it’s the work,” said Sevil. “There isn’t a...” Then she stopped, and I watched her understand that she was about to explain something that has never needed explaining, and that she did not have the words for it because nobody has ever had to make them.

She tried anyway. That is one of the things I liked about her.

“You take the weight of a house,” she said, “and you do the work of the house. That’s not two things I’ve asked you for. That’s one thing and it has been the one thing since before anybody was here to say so. I did not ask you for the season because there is no asking. There is only being in the count or not being in the count.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You were in it two months.”

“I know.”

“Then how could you not know?”

And I said: “Because nobody said it.”

---

Sevil looked at me for a long moment.

“No,” she said. “Nobody said it.”

---

Here is what I said, and I said it badly, and I have not thought of a better way to put it since.

I said that every single thing anybody had asked me to do, I had done. Three requests. Yes to all three. And after the third nobody had asked me anything, and I had gone on doing turns because there were turns to do and I liked doing them.

I said I had thought I was doing afternoons.

I said I had never once been asked for a season and had never once agreed to a season, and that when she stood at the stack and counted three carriers she had counted a thing I had not been told I was.

And then I said the part that mattered, which was not an argument and did not help:

**”It isn’t mine to keep doing.”**

“It’s a *share*.”

“It’s a share you gave me,” I said. “In a house I’m not in. To hold walls up on ground I’m not from. Nobody consulted me about the terrace and I would not have known what I was agreeing to if they had, and I am sorry, and it is still not mine.”

---

She never made the argument a personal one. That was not what she thought and it was not what she wanted.

What she said was:

**”You accepted the connection. You accepted the duty. They’re the same acceptance.”**

And by every standard on that hillside, she was right.

---

Terrik was not there.

He was on the sixth shelf that morning and did not come down until the middle of the afternoon, and by then it was finished.

He would have let me off. Sevil knows he would have let me off, and she did it in the morning, and I am not going to say what I think about the order of those two facts because I do not know.

Dess was there and did not say one word.

She stood in the narrow doorway, the one that has needed widening for six years, and listened to all of it and did not say *I asked what would happen* and did not look at Sevil and did not look at me.

Then she went to work.

---

The unspeaking took about a minute.

It has to be done the way the speaking was done: out loud, in front of people who are paying attention. So we went up to the stack in the afternoon, which was the worst part of the day for me and I imagine for her, and Sevil said that the Ost were taking the floating thing out of their count, and that the Ost were five again from today.

Somebody asked why.

Sevil said it was not going to sit the season.

That was all she said. She did not say I had refused. She did not say I had let her down, and she did not say what it meant for the terrace, and she stood there in front of about thirty people who could all do arithmetic and let them do it.

I would rather she had said something unkind. It would have cost me less.

---

And then I was weightless again.

Here is what that was, and then I am not going to mention it again.

The weight went out of me between one moment and the next, and I did not fall, because falling is not what happens. I simply stopped having anything to put down. I went to settle onto the top of the stack wall the way I had settled onto every course since the spring, and I arrived, and nothing happened. The stone did not know I was there.

I have been weightless for eleven worlds and it has never once been a loss. It was that afternoon.

---

The Ost were five again, which is heavier each than six was, and it does not help them at all.

I worked it out on the way down from the stack and Sevil had worked it out before she opened her mouth. Five shares from one total is more weight per person than six. Terrik is heavier this evening than he was this morning. So is she.

And the lower terrace wants **two carriers on the wall through the season**, and the Ost now have two, and both of them are needed on the upper ground they already held.

They got their weight back and lost the third body.

There is no arrangement on that hillside that turns two people into three, and Sevil had found the only one there had ever been, and it had lasted two months, and she had bought ground with it.

## Chapter 8: Short on the Upper Terrace

The wet came in early, which everybody had said it would.

That is the first thing that happened after, and it had nothing to do with me, and it is the reason the next part is as bad as it is. The season shortened by about three weeks. The lower terrace still wanted its walls before the water got into the beds, and the Ost still had the upper ground, and there were still two of them.

Sevil went and looked at what could be given back.

Nothing could. A holding is not a thing you return; you can sell it on, and she tried, and the Rennicks who had given it up were not going to take it back at what she had paid, and nobody else on the Fetch has two spare carriers either, because nobody on the Fetch has ever had a spare carrier in the whole of its history.

So they kept it and worked it short.

---

Working it short means what it sounds like.

You do the wall without somebody sitting on the course. You get about a third more done in a day and you build a wall that walks. It will hold for two years. It might hold for three. It will not hold for ten, and when it goes it will take the bed with it, and somebody will rebuild it, and that somebody will probably be Tibb.

Everybody knows this. Nobody on that hillside had to have it explained to them, and nobody pretended it was fine.

What they said, when they said anything, was that it was what there was.

---

Terrik went back to two turns.

He did it the way you would put on a coat. There was no conversation about it that I heard. The wet came in, the ground had to be walled, and on the first morning he did the upper terrace and then he went down and did the lower one. He came in at dark and ate and did not say anything and went to bed.

The next day he did it again.

He has stopped going up to the stack in the afternoons. He did that for nine weeks and he does not do it now, and when Dess said something about it he said there was not the time, which is true.

---

He never once said anything to me about any of it.

It would be easier if he had. He did not blame me and he did not tell me he did not blame me. He did not ask me to reconsider. He did not say *it’s all right*. He never said anything that would have required me to answer.

What he said, the last time I saw him, was that the wet had got into the third shelf beds and that this happened every four or five years and there was a way of dealing with it.

Then he asked if I was going.

I said yes.

“Right,” said Terrik.

---

Sevil and I did not stop speaking. That is worth putting down because I had expected we would.

She talked to me about the same things she had always talked to me about: the ground, the weather, what the Rennicks were doing now, the price of squared stone. She did it in the same voice, and she was not warm and she was not cold, and if you had come new to the Fetch that week you would not have been able to tell that anything had happened.

She did not forgive me. I want to be exact: not that she withheld it, but that forgiveness is not the shape of what was between us. She thought I had taken a duty and put it down. I thought I had never picked one up. Neither of us moved a finger’s width on that, and both of us knew the other had not moved, and we went on talking about squared stone.

Once, near the end, she said: “You’d do it again.”

“Yes.”

“Yes,” said Sevil. “So would I.”

---

Dess never said *I told you so*, and she had earned it, and she did not take it.

What she did say, on my last evening, was the only thing anybody on that hillside said about the whole business that was not about walls:

“You’d have been all right if you’d been from here.”

“Because I’d have known?”

“Because you’d have known,” said Dess, “and because you’d not have had anywhere else to go, and I don’t know which of those I mean.”

Then she asked me to tell Sevil about the doorway, which has needed widening for six years, and which is not going to be widened this year either.

---

Tibb was furious with me for four days and then stopped.

She was furious in the way a seven-year-old is furious, which is entirely and about everything. It was not really about the terrace. I had turned out not to be a permanent improvement in the arithmetic, and she had made plans.

Then she got interested in something else. On the last morning she asked me whether, where I was going, they had weight, and I said I did not know, and she said that if they did I should get into somebody’s count, because it was stupid to be nothing.

I said I would think about it.

“Don’t think about it,” said Tibb. “Just do it.”

---

Oad sang.

He sang the counting song on my last morning, which he sang most mornings, and got to the last line and looked at me and waited.

For two months I had an answer. Six.

That morning I did not.

He found this funny again and started the song over.

Nobody on the Fetch has thought about that song once in the whole of my time there. It says that the weight you carry tells you whose you are. It says it plainly, in the last line, to every child on that hillside before they can walk, and every adult who hears it smiles.

I know what the song says. I have known since Oad first sang it at me.

For two months I could answer it. I had not understood what the answer meant.

The song is being sung on that hillside this morning.

---

I left in the early part of the day, before the light was properly up the gorge.

Sevil was already out. She was at the elbow end of the lower terrace with a stick and a patch of wet dust, doing the arithmetic of a season that is three weeks shorter than it should be, with two carriers and four hundred paces of wall.

She said, “You’re off.”

“Yes.”

“Wind’s coming round by the middle of the day. You’ll want to be above it.”

That was the whole of it, and it was the correct thing to say.

---

I went up off the terrace, which is a thing I can do and nobody there can.

The Fetch went small underneath me the way places do. The gorge, the river a long way down, the shelves stepping up the slope one above another, every single one of them held up at the front by a wall that somebody sat on while somebody else built it.

You can see the seventh shelf from up there. Two hundred paces of new wall, at a height where nobody had done more than patching in six years, standing straight and true and built the way a wall is supposed to be built.

And below it, on the second shelf, the lower terrace, where a new wall is going in fast and unweighted and will walk.

Both of those are mine. Neither one cancels the other.

---

I was weightless the whole way up, which took no effort at all, which is what being me is.

The crossing is out past the ridge where the ground goes over toward the north road. The black sky, the white line, the shape that opens.

I went through it without stopping.

---
