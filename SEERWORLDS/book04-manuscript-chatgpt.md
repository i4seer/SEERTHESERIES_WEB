The Name in the Stone

# Part I: The Asking

## Chapter 1: A Question Before Hello

The first fact about Orrun was that I had arrived inside something.

Stone pressed in on every side. It made the floor under me, the walls beside me, and a ceiling high enough that four of me could have floated between it and the ground. I checked above twice because I was still expecting sky. There was only red rock, rough where nobody had bothered with it and smooth along the routes people used.

The second fact was the light. Thin amber growths ran through the walls at about the height of my middle. They branched, joined, and disappeared around both bends of the passage. I moved close to one. It gave off no heat I could detect, but it lit the red dust on the floor and showed me smaller openings cut into the stone ahead.

I had seen three worlds before Orrun. None had put its sky in the walls.

Something struck rock far below me. I did not hear the first blow. I felt a small hard tremor pass through the passage and into the air around my edge. Then came a second, followed by a pause of exactly the same length. Somebody was working. That helped. A place with work has reasons for some of its noises.

I turned toward the next blow, which meant I was facing the wrong way when a pale creature hurried around the bend.

It saw me and tried to stop. Its four short legs did not agree about how. The front pair planted while the back pair continued, and the creature folded in the middle and slid through the red dust until its nose stopped less than a hand from me.

“I’m all right,” it said before I had asked.

It stood by pushing with three legs, moving the fourth, changing its mind, and trying again. I could have described the process more kindly, but I would not have described it more clearly. When it was upright, it shook dust from skin the color of old palm flesh and looked at me with two large, dim eyes.

“Did I hit you?” it asked.

“No.”

“I nearly did.”

“You nearly did.”

The creature considered that. “Sorry.”

“You stopped in time.”

“I fell in time.”

That was true, and I liked it for saying so.

It came nearer, then began a full circuit around me. This took longer than you might think. Its legs were short even for its body, and it kept leaning inward as if a closer view would explain why I had no legs at all. I stayed where I was. I had learned that moving while somebody examines me only makes the examination last longer.

When it reached its starting place, Pell announced that I floated. I agreed. It asked how, then what I was made of, and I had the same answer for both.

“I don’t know.”

Pell looked pleased rather than disappointed. “Good. I thought I was missing something.”

The passage carried another blow from below. The pale creature did not react. It belonged to this place enough that the stone could shake under it without earning a glance.

“Were you here last warm?” it asked.

I did not know how long a warm was, but I understood the question. “No.”

“I knew it. I know everybody along these levels, and Sath knows everybody above them, and Ottu says he knows everybody below, but he doesn’t.” The creature turned toward the bend, took two steps, and looked back. “I’m Pell.”

“I’m the Dot.”

Pell waited.

“That was my answer,” I said.

“Oh.” Pell tested the words under its breath. “The Dot. All right. Are you coming?”

“Where?”

“You can’t stay in a traffic passage. A Carrier will put you on a shelf by mistake.”

I looked behind me. The passage was empty, but the dust held several broad tracks. One set had been made by something much wider than Pell.

I followed.

Pell talked while we moved. It told me the settlement was Underhand and the people were Orruk. It told me the amber lines were veins, the blows below came from the new cuts, and the passage we were using had been finished before Pell’s grandmother’s grandmother was soft. I asked what soft meant. Pell said it meant soft, which was not useful, then added that I looked fairly soft myself except for the floating.

Three side passages later, Pell stopped so suddenly that I passed it.

“I forgot,” it said.

“What?”

It had asked my name, where I came from, whether I could touch the ceiling, and whether I ate crawlers. I thought we had covered enough for one meeting.

Pell leaned forward with the serious interest it had not used for any of those questions.

“What are you going to take?”

“From whom?”

“Nobody.”

“Then why would I take it?”

Pell’s face changed. It had several more ways to do that than mine does, and this one appeared to mean that the problem was larger than expected.

“Your real name,” Pell said. “Which one are you going to choose?”

The blow from below came again. This time I felt it while I looked at Pell and understood that I had not yet learned what its first question meant.

## Chapter 2: A Body for a Word

Pell decided the fastest way to explain a name was to find one walking toward us.

The thing filling the passage did not look as if it had begun life shaped like Pell. Its back made a level platform wider than the distance between the walls of some side routes. Four thick legs moved beneath it with no wasted lift, and its neck was short enough that the head seemed fitted directly onto the load-bearing part. Eleven blocks of cut red stone sat across its back. I counted because ten would have been impressive and I wanted to be sure.

Pell pressed itself into a doorway. I moved up beside the amber vein, though the Carrier would have passed under me without trouble. Dust fell from the lowest block each time one of its feet touched down.

“Evening, Pell,” the Carrier said.

“It isn’t evening.”

“It is for me.” The Carrier glanced up at my shadow on the wall. “New soft?”

“Not an Orruk,” Pell said. “Probably.”

The Carrier accepted that answer more easily than I did and went on. Its load stayed level through the bend. I waited until the last broad foot disappeared, then looked at the marks it had left in the dust beside Pell’s small tracks.

“That was soft once?” I asked.

“Same as me.”

“Its bones changed.”

“Everything changes. Bones, skin, eyes, how much palm you need, where you can work without hurting, all of it.” Pell came out of the doorway and started walking again. “Carrier takes sixty days before you’re mostly settled. Longer before you stop knocking into doors.”

I followed, trying to put the large body over Pell’s. The result did not work. It was like trying to find a cup inside a table.

I asked who decided the change and what caused it. Pell said I was asking one question twice. The soft chose, then said the name. That was enough.

Pell was enjoying the lesson. It had discovered a person who did not know the first thing every Orruk learned, and it meant to spend that discovery carefully.

It explained that Pell was a call-name, useful for locating one particular soft in a settlement full of them. A chosen name was different. A soft went to the Name Chamber, stood in a worn place in the floor, and spoke the word it meant to become. After that, the body began its sixty-day work.

Nobody approved the choice. The gathered Orruk only listened. When I asked whether their refusal to hear could stop it, Pell said, “They can put their fingers in their ears if they want. It still works.”

Before I could ask how they knew, something scratched above us.

I moved back from the wall. An Orruk traveled along the ceiling with its body hanging beneath four long limbs. The hands at the ends found shallow cuts I had mistaken for tool marks. It crossed over me, released all four holds at once, and dropped.

There was time for me to think that it had made an error. Then it turned in the air and landed on narrow feet without making enough sound to wake somebody in the next chamber.

“Pell,” it said.

“Sath.”

Sath’s head sat on a long neck, and its bright eyes moved over me much faster than Pell’s had. “What did you take?”

“Nothing,” I said.

“What will you?”

“I have only just learned that those are different questions.”

Sath looked at Pell. “You found a slow one.”

Then it ran at the wall, went up without pausing, and vanished along the ceiling route.

“Wallwalker,” Pell said.

“I had worked that out.”

“Good. You’re improving.”

We came to a crossing where four amber lines met. Pell chose the left passage, then the right one, then returned to the crossing after admitting the left had been correct. During this, it gave me seven names. Deepdigger. Veintender. Stonesetter. Waterworks. Channelcutter. Speaker. Ringer. Each name changed a body toward certain work, though Pell insisted the work itself remained voluntary.

“If I said Carrier now,” I asked, “would I become like the one we passed?”

Pell stopped navigating badly and examined me with new caution. “You have to mean it.”

“What if I meant it?”

“Then I suppose we’d find out.”

That answer was less confident than the others. I stored it beside the fact that Pell had called my current name soft.

I asked what Pell planned to choose. It began moving again and said it was close to several names.

“That is not what close means.”

“It is when the Name Chamber is this way.” Pell pointed down both remaining passages at once.

For the next quarter of an hour, Pell listed nine possibilities and removed four of them. Then it restored two, added one I had not heard before, and blamed me for making the question harder.

I had been on Orrun less than an hour. I already knew two things that did not fit together easily: Pell could choose any life it wished, and Pell did not know which wish was its own.

## Chapter 3: Practice Bodies

The next chamber answered a question I had not asked yet: what do you do with a choice that follows you through your whole childhood?

You play with it.

Four soft Orruk had built themselves into a Carrier. Three lay across the back of the fourth, gripping one another wherever they could. The one underneath took two steps before its front legs spread. The stack leaned, shouted four different instructions, and fell against the wall.

Nobody was hurt. I know because everybody stood up at once to argue about whose balance had failed.

Pell walked past without slowing. “Ottu keeps putting the heaviest one on the top.”

“Which is Ottu?”

“The one on the bottom.”

This seemed unfair until Ottu crawled out and announced that the others had loaded themselves badly. Then it seemed like a group achievement.

The chamber had been cut wide for storage, though no stores remained. Soft Orruk had taken possession of the empty floor. One clung to a wall groove and practiced being a Wallwalker by letting go. Another stood on a stone block and delivered a Speaker's address to an audience that kept leaving. Near the back, two soft used chips of red rock to scrape a channel through packed dust while a third poured a cup of rockmilk into the high end. Most of it escaped sideways.

“Channelcutters keep the water routes open,” Pell told me. “Those three have flooded that corner every warm for a month.”

“Are they improving?”

Pell studied the spreading pale puddle. “They have more cloth now.”

The Carrier group rebuilt itself. Ottu saw Pell and called across the chamber, asking whether Pell had come to practice properly or only criticize. Pell said it could do both. Then it lowered itself beside the stack and showed me Deepdigger.

The full performance consisted of lying flat, pulling with all four legs, and making a rough scraping noise.

“That is digging?” I asked.

“Deep digging.” Pell made the noise again, lower this time.

“Have you watched a Deepdigger work?”

“Not at the face. Soft aren't allowed in the new cuts.”

“Then how do you know that noise is correct?”

“Everybody knows it.”

Ottu stopped rebuilding the Carrier long enough to say Pell sounded like a blocked water pipe. The next few minutes did not improve anybody's knowledge of digging.

I moved around the chamber and watched. The games were funny because the bodies were wrong. Short legs could not span the ceiling grips. Soft fingers could not strike stone apart or read a light vein by touch. A back built to bend could not keep three friends level. The children failed visibly, recovered quickly, and began again.

But the games were not only jokes. The one playing Wallwalker altered the angle of each fall. The dust Channelcutters changed the slope of their ditch after the spill. Ottu ordered a lighter soft to the top of the next Carrier stack and made it almost to the doorway. They were testing futures with the only bodies they had.

Pell joined every game. It carried two soft for half a passage, climbed until its back feet left the floor, shouted a Speaker's welcome from the block, and spent ten minutes pruning a dead scrap of palm as a Veintender. It was poor at Carrier, worse at Wallwalker, excellent at Speaker, and so careful with the scrap of fungus that I thought Veintender might be the answer.

Then Ottu challenged it to Stonesetter.

The soft used loose blocks about the size of Pell's head. Each player had to build a straight wall across a marked strip of floor. No mortar held the stones. Fit was everything. Pell placed the first two too close, corrected the second, and set the third so it rocked.

Ottu's first row did not move when another soft kicked it.

“You're good at that,” I said.

Ottu looked away too quickly to hide how much the words pleased it. “My mother's a Stonesetter.”

“Are you taking it?”

The pleasure went out of its face. Ottu checked the wall again, though nothing had shifted. “Maybe.”

Pell answered for itself before I could ask. It had been a fine Deepdigger at six, a certain Wallwalker at eight, and an obvious Veintender for eleven days the previous warm. That morning it had been leaning toward Channelcutter. Speaker remained possible, though its family claimed Underhand already contained enough speaking.

“How old are you?” I asked.

“Thirteen.”

“When do you have to choose?”

Pell picked up the loose third stone and turned it until it sat cleanly on the row. “I don't.”

That was the first time anybody told me an Orruk could refuse the choice. Pell said it as if the fact barely deserved space beside the more urgent question of whether the fourth stone would fit. Around us, every game continued. Each soft could keep its present body. Every one of them was spending the warm pretending it would not.

## Chapter 4: Four Hundred and Six

Pell found the Name Chamber on its second attempt.

We entered through a low passage and came into a room large enough to make Underhand feel open. The ceiling rose far beyond the height of the other passages. Amber veins crossed it in a net, brighter than any I had seen, and their light reached all the way to a floor worn smooth by feet.

The walls had been cut after the room was cut. Every face carried columns of marks. Straight strokes met short hooks and angled lines in patterns that repeated often enough for me to tell they were writing before Pell said so.

I moved toward the nearest column.

“Names,” Pell said. Its voice came back from the high stone and said the word again more quietly. “That side is the stock.”

There were more marks than I could take in at once. Near the entrance, the cuts were deep and clean from many hands tracing them. Farther along, dust softened the grooves. A few marks had small lamps set below them. Others stood in dim stretches where the wall light barely reached.

“How many?” I asked.

Pell gave the answer at once. “Four hundred and six that people use.”

“And the others?”

“Still names. Nobody takes them.”

I counted the first three columns and checked Pell's total against the spacing. It was possible. When I started toward the darker end to continue, Pell made a patient sound.

“Everyone counts them,” it said.

“Then somebody may have counted wrong.”

“All of us?”

“That would be unusual.”

Pell waited for me to admit it was impossible. I did not. Instead I crossed the chamber to the second wall.

The cuts there were smaller and packed close. Some names appeared in long runs. One pattern returned so often that I could follow it down the wall without knowing what it said. Others appeared once, then vanished among thousands of later choices.

“Taken names,” Pell said. “When somebody chooses, the Markmaker puts it here.”

“Every time?”

“Without exception.”

“How far back?”

Pell looked at the wall as if it might know. “Before anybody remembers.”

A hard tapping began near the middle of the room. I followed it to an adult Orruk crouched beside the taking-wall. She was taller than Pell but not large, with steady forelimbs and a face protected by clear shell. A narrow tool rested in one hand. She set its tip against stone, struck it with another tool, and lifted a white chip no bigger than dust.

The new cut joined an old mark so neatly that I lost the join.

“Suth,” Pell said. “This is the thing I found.”

The Markmaker kept her tool against the wall. “Does it need feeding?”

“It says no.”

“Everything says no before it tries crawler.” Suth finished the line, brushed the dust into a shallow tray, and looked at me through the clear shell. “Name?”

“The Dot.”

“Taken or called?”

“I don't know what that means for me.”

“Called, then.” She set the point for her next stroke. “Don't cross the pale lines on the floor. Don't touch a loose mark. Don't tell me that floating means you can't knock anything over.”

I looked down. Thin boundary lines had been scratched around two work areas. Inside one, pieces of carved stone rested in exact rows on cloth. “Why are the marks loose?”

“Repair.” The tool struck. “The wall lasts. Individual cuts don't unless somebody looks after them.”

That gave me a better way to understand the room. The stock wall was not a list somebody had finished. The taking-wall was not a monument left alone. Both required Orruk to keep choosing, cutting, cleaning, and recutting. The records were old because living hands had refused to let them become dust.

Pell had already grown bored. It walked the common-name end and read choices aloud, adding an opinion to each one. Carrier was useful but made narrow passages annoying. Wallwalker was excellent until you wanted furniture. Speaker depended on whether the family could survive you. Channelcutter was steady and necessary and, according to Pell, not exciting enough to decide anything today.

I stayed with the taking-wall.

Look at a thousand choices written close together and the first thing you see is repetition. Look longer and you notice the gaps, the brief fashions, the single old name that somebody chose once and nobody chose again. I could not read the marks yet. I could still see six hundred years of young Orruk standing in one room and placing one decision after another into stone.

The distant work blows reached the chamber through the floor. Suth's lighter tapping answered from the wall. For the first time since I arrived, I understood that Underhand was built from both sounds: bodies opening new stone below and one careful hand recording what those bodies had become.

Then Pell called from the entrance to say it had found food, and the history of Orrun lost to food without a contest.

## Chapter 5: The Price of Supper

Food in Underhand came on flat pieces of stone because wood did not grow there and clay was apparently a foolish thing to waste heat on. Pell told me this while collecting enough supper for three soft and claiming all of it was necessary for thought.

The eating chamber opened off a busy passage. Warm slabs set into the walls roasted small hard-shelled crawlers. Orruk moved between them with scoops, bowls, knives, and strong opinions. The room smelled sharp and nutty from the shells. I could not eat any of it, but smell is useful even when supper is not.

Pell built its meal from four things. Palm came first: a pale sheet of fungus cut into folded pieces. Rockmilk filled a heavy cup. Two amber glowfruits went into a bowl Pell guarded with both front legs. The crawlers came last, cracked open after roasting.

“Those are the good part,” Pell said. “My family cooks them better.”

The Orruk working the nearest hot slab heard this. “Your family burns them.”

“Only the outside.”

“The outside is where they live.”

Pell took its plate before the discussion could become more exact.

I floated beside it at a low table while the chamber filled. The different adult bodies required different arrangements. A Wallwalker folded long limbs under the table and still occupied two places. A Carrier ate from a ledge because its back did not bend far enough. Three Veintenders sat close together with small bowls and split one glowfruit among them. The food was not merely food. It was evidence of what each name continued to demand after the sixty days ended.

Then a Deepdigger arrived.

He was shorter than the Carrier I had seen, yet he used more of the room. Plates covered his chest and shoulders. His forelimbs ended in wide claws rubbed flat at the tips. He carried four slabs of palm in the bend of one arm and a bowl of crawlers in the other, with a rockmilk vessel hooked against his side.

“How many meals is that?” I asked Pell.

“One.”

The Deepdigger heard us. “A small one.”

He lowered himself onto a reinforced section of floor. The stone beneath him had been patched twice. He ate with the steady speed of someone doing another job before the next job began.

Pell told me a Deepdigger needed about four times the food of a Veintender. The Veintenders at the next table objected before Pell had finished. They claimed the number was nearer three if the digger did not count second supper. The Deepdigger claimed Veintenders survived on half a fruit and complaint. A Carrier passing behind him said everybody should count by weight and then refused to help with the counting.

There was no single correct answer. That pleased me more than it should have. After the Name Chamber, I had started to suspect all Orruk agreed on everything.

I asked why glowfruit went mostly to the soft. The slab worker showed me a tray where thin roots touched a bright wall vein. Each fruit had taken a long time and a great deal of careful tending to grow. Soft bodies needed something in it. Nobody could tell me the name of the something, but children without enough of it grew weak.

Pell pushed one fruit away after two bites.

Four adults noticed.

It pulled the bowl back and finished.

Choice had looked clean on the chamber wall. At supper it was less clean. A Carrier needed space built for a back that could not bend. A Deepdigger consumed enough palm to feed several smaller bodies. Veintenders lived in light that hurt other eyes. Soft were fed by everybody and watched by everybody. The body a name gave you solved certain problems by creating needs that lasted as long as you did.

I said this to Pell.

Pell licked crawler oil from one front digit and looked around the chamber. “Yes.”

“You say that as though it changes nothing.”

“What would it change?”

“Your list.”

“My list already has bodies on it.”

That answer was better than my question. I had imagined Pell choosing a word and receiving a shape afterward, as if the shape were a surprise hidden in the sound. Pell had grown up at these tables. It knew who needed the reinforced floor, who could not use the low bowl, who took more food, who slept in darker rooms, and who could cross the ceiling. Those facts were not hidden costs. They were ordinary life.

The Deepdigger finished his rockmilk and stood. Pell asked whether he was returning to the lower face.

“After another plate,” he said.

Pell gave me a look that meant its four-times estimate had been proved.

The Veintenders gave Pell a different look.

I did not choose between them. It was my first warm in Underhand, and I had already learned that supper could support several truths if everybody counted differently.

## Chapter 6: Testing the Edges

On my second warm in Underhand, I began trying to break the naming system.

That sounds worse than it felt. On the three worlds I had visited before Orrun, a shared certainty had hidden something important. I had found a road claimed by a person who did not own distance, doors treated as proof of welcome when one creature had never opened hers, and a careful way of speaking built around a danger much smaller than everybody believed. Each world had offered me a statement that sounded complete. Each statement had a loose edge.

So I looked for Orrun's edge.

I started with power. Could an adult name a soft by force? Pell stopped walking when I asked, and that told me the question was not ordinary. It took me to Suth instead of answering alone.

The Markmaker set down her striking tool. “No.”

“Has anybody tried?”

“Yes. It does nothing to the soft and gets the speaker put out of the chamber. If the speaker tries again, we close the chamber until they leave.”

“Why is that?”

“Because a name has to belong to the mouth saying it.” Suth picked up the tool. “And because we are not animals.”

I wanted the first answer. The second told me more about Underhand.

Next I tested access. Common names were near the entrance where the wall was bright, and rare names stood deeper in poorer light. That looked like a way to keep certain choices from certain people. I asked a soft, two Carriers, a Veintender, and Suth whether anybody needed permission to use an old name. All gave me the same answer. Any Orruk could take any mark in the stock. No payment, family standing, or work shortage changed that.

I tested labor after that. The bodies looked made for jobs, and I expected the job to become an order. Instead Pell took me to a crawler pen kept by a Deepdigger.

The keeper's claws could split shell with almost no effort. They were less helpful with the small latches on the pens, so the handles had been rebuilt thick enough for her grip. She had never worked a stone face. Nobody had made her. The only complaint she heard was that she charged too much for crawler feed, which she said was because everybody wanted to pay last warm's price.

“Would a Veintender be better at this?” I asked.

“At the latches,” she said. “Not at moving a full pen when some soft forgets to close the drain.”

Pell looked interested in the ceiling.

The keeper looked at Pell.

I learned why the corner of the practice chamber still smelled of rockmilk.

By midday I had tested whether families owned names, whether the same name could be taken by two soft on one warm, whether a soft could leave the chamber without speaking, and whether a changed Orruk could refuse the work everybody expected. The answers stayed open. Families advised, argued, boasted, and worried, but the word belonged to the soft. Two could take the same one. Silence produced no change. Work was not assigned.

The system was doing something I disliked in an investigation: it was surviving fair questions.

One question remained from Pell's wall game. I waited until we had left the crawler keeper, because I had begun to notice that Pell answered better while moving.

“You said you never have to choose.”

“You don't.”

“What happens to your body if you never do?”

“Nothing.” Pell stepped over a water groove. “You stay soft.”

“For how long?”

“For your life.”

The answer altered the picture more than Pell's voice suggested. A permanent choice was not the only permanent condition. Refusing it also shaped where you could go, what you could lift, and which tasks your body allowed.

“Has anybody done that?”

Pell took longer this time. “Three Orruk here.”

“Who?”

“I don't know them.”

Pell knew the names of adults four levels away, the family history of half the soft in the practice room, and which passage cooked crawlers correctly. I did not say that. The delay had become part of the answer.

“How old are they?”

“Old. One is very old.”

“What do they do?”

Pell's pace increased. “Soft things.”

Then it asked if I wanted to see the upper water channels. The change was obvious enough that I could have pushed. On earlier worlds, I would have. I told myself I did not because I had many warms left and could ask later.

That was partly true.

The rest was that I had spent a day searching for a door somebody had locked, and the first closed thing I found was a thirteen-year-old who did not want to discuss three neighbors. I followed Pell toward the water and left the question where it was.

For then.

# Part II: What Bodies Know

## Chapter 7: Below the Last Vein

I met Ganno coming up from the dark.

Pell and I were crossing the head of a stair when two plated forelimbs appeared over its top. They gripped the edge, pulled, and brought the rest of a Deepdigger into the light. He was the Orruk from supper. Up close, I could see red dust packed into every join in his shoulder plates. The flat ends of his claws were chipped in different places, and one carried a fresh white line.

He stood on the landing and blinked at the amber vein. His eyes were small and almost closed.

“You ate the small meal,” I said.

“Then I ate the other one.” He turned his head until he found me. “Floating soft.”

“Not an Orruk,” Pell corrected.

“That explains the floating.”

It did not, but he was already moving past us.

I asked his name. Ganno answered without stopping. I asked what lay below the stair, how far the new cut went, what kind of stone they removed, and whether I could see it. That made him stop.

“Can you get crushed?”

“I have never tested that.”

“Can you get out of the way?”

“Usually.”

Ganno looked at Pell. “Does it answer anything properly?”

“Not yet.”

He told me to meet him at the same stair four warms later. Pell objected that soft were not allowed at a working face. Ganno pointed out that I was not an Orruk, as Pell had just insisted. Rules become very interesting when the creature breaking them does not belong to the group named in the rule.

Four warms later, I waited at the stair before Ganno arrived.

He carried a low lamp fixed behind thick shell. Its light pointed down and did not shine into his eyes. He also carried a wedge, a water vessel, and enough food that I could now identify a full Deepdigger shift by supper alone.

“Stay above my shoulder,” he said. “If I say back, go back. If anybody taps three fast, get against a wall and wait.”

“Which wall?”

“The nearest one that isn't falling.”

That was not exact enough for me, but he had begun descending.

The upper stair was smooth and wide. Water grooves ran along both sides, carrying a thin flow upward by a method Pell had described twice and I still did not understand. After three landings, the amber growths became thinner. Their branches ended one by one until Ganno's covered lamp supplied most of what I could see.

The route changed beneath us. Finished steps gave way to a long slope scored for grip. Then the walls lost their polish. Tool marks crossed one another where workers had widened the way, and broken stone waited in heaps for a Carrier.

Ganno's breathing changed before the light ended. Each breath grew deeper, with more time between. I asked whether the air was bad.

“Not bad yet.”

“When does it become bad?”

“When I stop answering.”

We passed the last living vein. It was no longer than Pell's leg, a weak amber thread on the left wall. Somebody had built a little rim beneath it so water would not cross its root. Below that point, Underhand's light came only from what its workers carried.

The darkness did not bother me. The blows did.

At the surface, they had reached me as small tremors spaced by long pauses. Down there, each strike moved the air and floor together. Dust lifted from the slope. Loose chips clicked against one another. The next strike always arrived after enough quiet for me to wonder whether work had stopped.

Ganno led me through a low opening into a chamber that did not yet deserve the name. Six Deepdiggers occupied the far face. Lamps sat behind them, aimed so the light crossed the stone instead of meeting their eyes. Each worker stood with most of the body close to the ground and one claw resting on the rock.

They were listening through their hands.

Ganno joined them. He placed a claw on one point, shifted it the width of a digit, and waited through another worker's blow. Then he moved sideways, pressed again, and drew one short line in the dust.

He struck once.

A block taller than Pell separated along the line and leaned outward. Two Deepdiggers caught it before it fell, turned it, and lowered it onto woodenless rollers made from round stone.

I checked the face. There had been no crack I could see.

“How?” I asked.

Ganno pressed his claw to the new surface. “Grain.”

I moved as close as his shoulder and looked. The stone appeared the same above and below his line. I changed angles, used the lamp's edge, and watched dust settle into every shallow mark. Nothing showed me what his hand had found.

“I can't see it.”

“You aren't meant to.” Ganno chose another place. “You came with the wrong eyes.”

That should have sounded rude. It did not. He was stating a tool problem. I had come below the last vein believing careful attention could show me anything worth knowing. Ganno put one claw on a blank red wall and found a path my whole body could not detect.

## Chapter 8: The Answering Tap

The Deepdiggers did not work quickly. They worked without wasting anything.

A claw moved because it had found grain. A strike landed because the claw had chosen a place. Two bodies shifted because a block had begun to lean. Nobody hurried after loose stone or shouted for a tool left across the chamber. The whole face came apart at a pace that looked slow until I noticed how much wall had disappeared.

The air grew worse. I could not taste or breathe it, but dust began to cling to my edge and the Orruk opened their mouths wider between blows. Ganno's covered lamp turned brown. Every so often a worker carried a full dust tray up the slope and came back with the clear shell wiped clean.

One Deepdigger had a broken plate along her left shoulder. The old crack had healed with a raised seam. Between strikes, she tapped her claw twice against the wall.

Somebody to her right answered with three taps.

She gave one. Farther down the face, Ganno gave two.

The pattern moved around the chamber without stopping the work. I listened through several rounds before asking Ganno what information they were passing.

“Still here,” he said.

“All those taps mean only that?”

He touched the wall and waited. “Only that is plenty down here.”

The cracked-plate digger started again. Her two taps traveled left this time. Each worker answered in a different place, so the sound mapped the whole face. A missing reply would tell them where to look before a voice could cross the dust.

I began counting the rounds. On the fifth, one answer did not come.

Ganno stopped with his claw raised. The others stopped one after another. Nobody called a name. They waited through the space where the tap should have been, then the missing worker struck twice from behind a new block. The rock had hidden the first sound.

Six bodies returned to work.

I had misunderstood the pattern. It did more than prove everybody was alive. It gave each worker a small duty toward the others. You tapped because somebody needed your answer, and you listened because one day the missing answer might be yours.

Ganno called it an old foolish habit. He did not miss a round.

During the meal break, the Deepdiggers ate sitting against the unfinished side wall. I watched Ganno finish two palm slabs before another worker had opened her crawler bowl. He used the quiet to criticize work that was not present.

A channel two passages away had been cut across a seep and would require repair before ten years passed. An upper chamber had corners made by an Orruk who had died before Ganno's naming, and Ganno still visited them because the joins were nearly perfect. One narrow route had been widened in a hurry. He would not identify the digger responsible, which made the others identify three possible offenders for him.

“What makes a good corner?” I asked.

He put down his food, which told me the answer mattered. A corner had to bring load away from the ceiling without trapping water or wasting stone. Too sharp and the roof carried stress in one line. Too round and the workers had removed rock for no reason. Ganno drew two shapes in the dust, rejected both, and used one claw to mark the curve he meant.

The cracked-plate digger leaned over to improve it.

Ganno wiped out her line.

Their argument lasted through the rest of the meal and became too exact for me to follow. They discussed force, seep direction, tool reach, and a chamber built eighty warms earlier by somebody both claimed to have studied. Neither sounded angry. They sounded awake in a way they had not while eating.

When work resumed, I watched Ganno's corners. He built them with the care he had used to describe the dead digger's work. His claws found load I could not see. He changed a curve by less than the width of Pell's smallest digit, stepped back, and checked it through touch twice.

“Do you like doing this?” I asked when the next break came.

Ganno looked at the corner instead of me. “It's good stone.”

“That was not my question.”

“No.” He placed his claw on the face again. “It was my answer.”

The workers tapped. Ganno answered. Then he struck the wall exactly where his hand had told him, and another clean piece came free.

He was better at that work than anybody I had ever seen do anything. I still had not heard him say he wanted to be there.

## Chapter 9: Where the Light Is Kept

Ganno ate while we climbed.

This should not have been possible with his hands full of tools, but he had arranged the food across the upper plates of one forelimb. He pulled a folded piece of palm free with the other claw and swallowed it in two bites without changing pace. By the second landing, the first half of his shift meal was gone.

The weak final vein appeared ahead. Then came the first full branch, and Ganno lowered his lamp cover until almost no light escaped it.

“You don't need that now,” I said.

“I don't.”

He kept the cover closed anyway.

The glow grew at every landing. Dust turned from brown to red. The finished walls picked up warm color again, and the water channels showed thin bright lines. Ganno narrowed his eyes before we reached the main living level.

Then we entered a passage I had not seen.

The amber there was not a seam somebody had found. It was a living structure the Orruk had shaped for generations. Thick roots covered the lower walls. From them rose branching growths that crossed the ceiling and met above the center of the passage. The light was strong enough to show individual dust grains falling from Ganno's plates.

Frames stood under the largest branches. Veintenders moved along them with narrow tools, clear cups, lengths of wet cloth, and the kind of calm speed that only appears after years of work.

One tender braced three digits around a growth no wider than mine. She turned it toward the light, studied the color near its base, and cut a thread from the far side. The remaining branch brightened so slowly I almost decided it had not. Then the light spread through the next joint.

“Why that piece?” I asked.

She did not look down. “It was turning early.”

“I can't see a difference.”

“You aren't looking for one.”

This was the second time that warm somebody had explained my failure as a problem with my body. It was becoming difficult to argue.

The Veintender climbed lower and let me look from her place on the frame. She pointed to three branches. To me they shared one amber color. To her, the left needed cutting in three days, the center could stay eleven, and the right had suffered too much water and must be kept warm before anybody touched it.

“What happens if you cut the wrong one?”

“We lose the growth.” She touched the thick root below with two fingertips. “This part may live forty years if we don't behave foolishly.”

Her hands went back to work. One narrow tool slid under a strand, lifted it away from the wall, and allowed another finger to place a small pad beneath it. The whole movement took less time than my question had.

Ganno had stopped in the center of the passage.

He no longer carried food. Both forelimbs hung beside him, dusty and still. He tilted his head upward until the amber crossed his face.

Deepdigger eyes were made for the new cuts. I had seen how little lamp light Ganno used below. Here, his lids narrowed to dark lines within moments. Water gathered at their edges, ran along the dust on his cheeks, and left two clean tracks.

He remained where he was.

A Carrier came through and adjusted course around him. Two soft ran past. A Veintender dropped a cloth, climbed down for it, and returned to the frame. Ganno watched the work until his eyes could not have shown him much at all.

“Does it hurt?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“Then why don't you move?”

He did not answer until the tender above us cut another fine thread. “I will.”

He stayed for three more cuts. Then he lowered his head, blinked hard, and continued up the passage.

I followed until the light thinned enough for his eyes to open. The question I had asked at the work face remained with me, along with his answer that had not been an answer. I tried a different one.

“If you could choose now, would you still take Deepdigger?”

Ganno shifted the empty food wrap from one claw to the other. “You don't ask an adult that.”

“Why?”

“Because the adult can't do anything useful with it.”

He did not sound angry. He turned into an eating passage, bought another bowl of crawlers, and spent the next ten minutes explaining that his sister roasted them correctly while everyone else was wasting heat.

I listened. I also counted how many times he rubbed water from his eyes before the food arrived.

## Chapter 10: A Second Cut

I waited two warms before asking Suth whether an adult could take another name.

I would like to say I waited out of respect for Ganno's boundary. The truth is that I spent those two warms asking six other Orruk first. Four said no. One said probably not. A Speaker gave me a long answer that became less useful with every sentence. I went to Suth because disagreement meant there might be a record.

She was recutting a name near the bottom of the taking-wall. Her clear face shell had collected so much dust that she had pushed it up onto her head. “Yes,” she said.

I moved closer. “It works?”

“I said they can take one.” She struck the tool. “I didn't say the body listens.”

Suth finished the line before leading me toward the far end of the wall. The common takings thinned as we went. Lamps became fewer. At the last column she knelt and wiped red dust from a set of marks cut smaller than the others.

Each entry held two names joined by a short line. Some had a third below.

“Second takings,” she said. “First name, then what they tried later. Dates beside both.”

“How many?”

“Count them if you intend to stand there.”

I did. There were hundreds. They reached farther back than Suth could identify by memory. I had expected an unusual act performed by one or two desperate Orruk. The wall held generation after generation of them.

I tested the result every way I could without repeating the same question. No body had changed, not even partly. A close second name made no difference: Suth showed me a Carrier who had tried Stonesetter, a Wallwalker who had tried Highledge, and a Waterworks who had tried Channelcutter. They had stood on the same worn ground as before and could have filled the chamber with witnesses. Nothing followed the second word.

Suth's answers did not slow. Mine did.

One old entry carried three later names. The cuts were not equally deep. The second had been carved with a hand that pressed hard enough to chip the edge of the first. The fourth mark was shallow and narrow.

“Were you here for that one?” I asked.

Suth looked. “No. My teacher's teacher may have been.”

“Why record something that never works?”

She gave me the same look she had given my called name. “Because it happened.”

I followed the rows. Some second takings came only days after the first change had finished. Others waited decades. A few adult Orruk had returned three times. Their bodies had remained the first word they spoke as children, but the wall carried every later word too.

“Did any of them know it would fail?”

“All of them. We don't hide this end.”

“Then why try?”

Suth settled her tool against the next worn groove. “Ask them.”

“Some are dead.”

“Then you waited too long.”

It was not kindness, but it was fair. Suth recorded choices. She did not own their reasons merely because her hand preserved the marks.

She began cutting. The sharp sound carried farther in that dim end of the chamber. I stood among the second names and thought of Ganno closing his eyes under the veins. I had a fact now: he could speak Veintender in the right place with everybody listening, and Underhand would record that he had said it.

His hands would remain claws. His eyes would still hurt.

I did not ask Suth whether Ganno's name appeared there. It was the first question I had stopped before somebody else told me to.

On the way out, I passed the worn place in the floor where soft Orruk made the first choice. It was only a shallow hollow made by many feet. I tried to decide why the ground would listen once and never again.

The stone offered no opinion.

## Chapter 11: Halfofboth

Suth did not invite me back the next warm. I returned anyway.

She had moved to a ladder near the common-name end and was cleaning old dust from the hook marks. I stayed below the pale work line and waited until she looked down.

“I have three questions,” I said.

“You have always had more than three.”

“These belong together.”

“That will be new.”

My first question concerned compound names. If one word made one body, perhaps joining words could keep more than one future open. Suth climbed down, selected a narrow ledger stone from a shelf, and showed me a page of recorded compounds.

They worked. Nobody knew exactly how.

The body did not become half of one common shape and half of another in a clean division. Sometimes one trait dominated. Sometimes the new form did something neither ordinary name suggested. Orruk took compounds knowing the outcome might not match any living example.

I asked whether a compound body could change later. Suth said no. Uncertainty did not keep two futures open; it only hid which permanent future would arrive.

Suth returned the ledger to its place.

My second question was about the dim end of the stock. Names remained there that nobody had spoken for hundreds of years. Some had no outcome record. An Orruk could still choose one. The chamber would accept it, and the body would become whatever that old word meant, whether Underhand had use for the result or not.

That answer opened enough danger to make Pell's ordinary list seem sensible.

Someone had chosen such a name recently. Suth would only tell me that I might meet her after the change finished, if the Orruk herself agreed.

The boundary was becoming familiar. I could ask what the rule allowed. I could not turn another Orruk's choice into public material merely because I was curious.

My third question had seemed better before I began.

“Is there a name that means no name?”

Suth froze with one foot on the ladder.

“I am trying to find a word that would preserve the soft body as a chosen form,” I explained. “If a soft took Unnamed, it might choose without changing.”

“Or it might become something nobody has ever seen.”

“There may be a record.”

“There isn't.”

“Could somebody make the name?”

Suth came back down. She set her tools on the shelf in a row, which worried me more than if she had waved them.

“A soft once asked whether two names spoken together would make it good at both,” she said. “It had been choosing between Carrier and Wallwalker. I told it to take Halfofboth and report what happened.”

“Did it?”

“It took Channelcutter. It has been sensible near water for thirty-seven years.”

“Then my question is still open.”

“Your question is a hole wearing a name.” Suth tapped the stock wall with one digit. “These marks do not obey jokes. If you say a word you made this morning, the chamber may do nothing. It may do what the word means to you. It may do something that was meant when the name existed before you found it. I don't know. That is why we keep outcomes.”

I had treated language as a latch. If the naming system closed a door, a clever enough word might lift it again. But the words did not command stone in ways Suth could predict. They carried bodies with them, including bodies no living Orruk remembered.

“You dislike my question,” I said.

“I dislike you offering it to somebody else.”

That was fair too.

Suth climbed the ladder and resumed cleaning. I stayed until the next line was clear, then left the chamber without testing Halfofboth, Unnamed, or anything I had invented on the way in.

Freedom in Underhand extended all the way to foolishness. The wall did not protect you from the word you chose.

## Chapter 12: What Was in the Room

The taking-wall contained a pattern Suth called the Highledge years.

She showed it to me after I asked why one mark appeared forty-one times inside a narrow section and almost nowhere before or after. The first Highledge in the run belonged to a popular Speaker's child. The Speaker had made a work song about bright upper chambers, and the child had taken Highledge in front of nearly all Underhand.

Within four years, forty more soft chose the same body.

“Did the settlement need them?” I asked.

“The settlement had nine good high ledges,” Suth said. “It still has nine.”

“What did the others do?”

“Whatever work they found. Crawler keeping. Frame hauling. Storage counts. One rings now and is very good.”

The Highledge body was tall, narrow, and made to stand safely along steep shelf routes. It was useful where passage walls opened into upper spaces. On ordinary floors, the long legs had to fold awkwardly. Most of the forty-one had chosen an admired picture of life rather than an available task.

Nobody stopped them. Nobody even asked them to stop.

“Then the choice was free,” I said.

Suth cleaned dust from the forty-first mark. “Were you expecting me to argue?”

I was. I had learned to test force by looking for a creature who could say no. The Highledges had been allowed to say yes. The problem, if it was a problem, lived earlier than permission.

Suth showed me other runs. A Deepdigger family had placed the same name on the wall for six generations. A Waterworks period followed a flood that shut three living passages for a warm. Ringer appeared more often after a famous performance. One compound vanished from use after a badly shaped adult spent forty years warning soft away from it.

“Families do tell children what to take,” I said.

“Families tell children everything.”

“Pell said naming another person is forbidden.”

“It is.” Suth pointed at the worn floor. “Advice isn't a taking. Pride isn't a taking. A song isn't a taking. The soft still stands there alone.”

That was true at the final moment. It was also incomplete, and I think Suth knew it was incomplete because she put the tool down.

“When I was soft,” she said, “my mother thought I should take Veintender. My eyes were good and I could sit still. My father thought Markmaker because his sister had been one. Neither told me. I heard them tell everybody else.”

“What did you want?”

“For them to stop being careful where I could hear it.”

She had chosen Markmaker. She would not tell me whether her aunt's work or her parents' careful silence had mattered more. That question belonged to her, and by then I could see it before asking.

We went to find Pell. It was in the practice chamber building a water channel through dust, though that warm's list had Channelcutter ranked fifth. I asked what its family hoped it would take.

“They don't care.”

Ottu, working beside it, laughed hard enough to collapse one side of the channel.

Pell corrected itself. One parent admired Carriers. The other had suggested Waterworks often enough that the suggestion had become part of supper. A grandparent believed Speaker was not a real body's work. Pell's older cousin sent new arguments for Wallwalker every few warms. Nobody had ordered anything.

“And your friends?” I asked.

Ottu said Pell should take Quietkeeper, which did not exist but ought to.

Pell threw channel dust at it.

The two chased each other out of the chamber, leaving the broken water route behind. I looked at the abandoned slope. The choice was Pell's. So were the songs, jokes, family bodies, useful jobs, admired adults, childhood games, and every look at a showing. None forced the final word. All of them entered the room with the soft who spoke it.

I went back to Suth and said I understood.

“No, you don't,” she said. “Neither do I. That's why I only cut what they say.”

Then she lifted her tool and placed one clean line beside the Highledge years, preserving another choice without pretending the wall could explain it.

# Part III: The Crack I Wanted

## Chapter 13: Finished Changing

Rin's family began arranging her showing before the change was finished.

Pell took me to the passage three times during the final warm. On the first visit, six relatives were arguing about food. On the second, somebody had moved every bench and nobody admitted it. On the third, Rin herself was trying to repair a cup she had dropped.

Her new hands were webbed.

The thin blue skin between her digits caught the pieces when she tried to pinch them. She changed grips, dropped one again, and said a word Pell refused to explain to me. The cup had broken on the first week of her change. She had kept it because she wanted to repair something with the finished hands.

“Are they finished?” I asked.

Rin spread them under the amber vein. “They were different when I woke, so probably.”

The rest of her Waterworks body lay close to the floor. Her back had widened, her skin had darkened along the wet channels, and the edges of her feet carried the same webbing as her hands. She could hold her breath much longer than most Orruk. I did not ask her to prove that in a stone passage.

By the time the showing began, nearly everybody living nearby had arrived. Rin walked into the cleared center while her family formed an uneven line along one wall. She was laughing until the crowd became quiet. Then she saw how many faces were turned toward her and stopped.

Nobody gave a speech. They simply looked.

The silence lasted long enough for Rin to shift her weight twice. Then an older Waterworks said the webbing had come in clean. Somebody behind me said her back might stay too narrow. Three relatives answered at once that narrow backs ran in the family and were excellent near old drains. The passage filled with talk.

Rin began crying.

Nobody rushed to hide it. A family member put a food bowl beside her and another adjusted a damp cloth under one foot. The comments continued: the color along her sides, the reach of her front digits, the way the last joint bent. It sounded like an inspection because it was one. It also sounded like welcome.

The two things did not cancel each other.

Afterward, Rin sat beside a water channel with the repaired cup in both hands. The join was rough but held. I asked what the sixty days had felt like.

“Busy,” she said. “My bones never asked whether I was using them.”

Every morning had brought some small loss of skill. One day her old grip worked. The next day it did not. Food slipped. Sleep broke into short pieces because the growing joints ached. Her balance changed faster than she could learn it. Family helped, but help did not prevent the body from hurting.

“Would you do it again?” I asked.

Rin laughed into the cup. “That's like asking if I'd finish falling a second time.”

“You could regret the choice and still be glad the changing stopped.”

She looked at me more carefully. “I don't regret it.”

“How do you know yet?”

“I don't know what it will feel like in thirty years. I know what it feels like today.” She turned one webbed hand over. “Today nobody asks what I'm taking.”

That had been worth sixty days of pain to her. Perhaps it would be worth the body after thirty years. I could not measure either part from the finished hand.

The crowd thinned. Rin's family opened food trays, and people stayed to argue about crawler heat. The soft who had attended did not leave. They gathered near Rin in a line that pretended not to be one, each asking to see her hands.

I watched the adults watch them.

The glances were quick. A parent checked a child's face while Rin flexed the webbing. A grandparent pulled one soft forward for a better view. Nobody said, *See how well this ends.* They did not have to. A showing gave the finished adult a welcome, and it gave every undecided child a picture of relief.

Pell stood closest to Rin. It asked six questions about the first shoulder ache and three about sleeping. It did not add Waterworks to its list. It did not remove it either.

When we left, Pell said Rin had come out well.

I agreed. I also looked back at the soft still waiting to touch her finished hands and understood that success could press on somebody without meaning to.

## Chapter 14: The Mark I Knew

I returned to the Name Chamber after Rin's showing because the soft had made me think about the unused end of the stock.

The bright common names were pictures they had seen all their lives. The older marks stood where the light failed, attached to no living body they could examine. I wanted to know what choosing looked like when the picture was gone.

Suth was working behind three pale lines instead of one. A section of the old wall had been opened. More than a hundred carved pieces lay on dark cloth in rows, each placed in the order it had come out. The hollow spaces behind them showed older cuts beneath the present surface.

“Stay at this end,” Suth said when I entered.

“I see the lines.”

“Seeing has not always protected my work from visitors.”

I remained outside the boundary and moved along the dim wall. Most marks meant nothing to me. Some repeated shapes I had learned from Pell, though the old forms used different hooks. I passed them slowly, comparing cuts, until one stopped me.

A circle held a single point at its center.

I had seen it cut beside a broken ledge on the first world I remembered. No local hand had been able to read it there. Later, in the place between worlds, I had found the same shape so faint in the dark that I might have invented it if the first carving had not existed.

Now it stood among Orruk names.

I moved nearer. The mark was part of a column whose lower stones had been lifted for repair. Its left curve disappeared behind one of Suth's cloth rows. I shifted sideways to see the hidden edge.

My lower edge crossed the pale line.

Suth shouted one word. “Out.”

The sound struck the chamber harder than her tool. I stopped.

“I only need to see—”

“Out of the work.”

“I am not touching anything.”

Suth set down her hammer. The care she used frightened me more than the shout. “Your air moves dust. Your body blocks my lamp. I have one hundred and nine marks off that wall, and if a row shifts I have to prove every place again. Move.”

I moved backward across the line.

“Farther.”

I obeyed until I reached the main chamber. Suth followed, took a length of cord from her shelf, and fixed it across the entrance to the old stock.

“When can I come back?”

“When the wall is whole.”

“How long?”

“Ten warms if nobody makes more work for me.” She tightened the cord while looking directly at me. “Perhaps twelve.”

I wanted to ask what the mark meant. I also wanted to explain that it had appeared on another world and between worlds, and that no fact on Orrun could matter more to me. Both wants were about me. The pieces on Suth's cloth carried other people's choices, and I had crossed the only line she asked me to respect.

“I am sorry,” I said. “I entered your work and could have moved the record.”

Suth checked the cord. “Yes.”

That was the whole answer I had earned.

She went back to the lifted wall. I stayed in the public part of the chamber long enough to hear her recount the first row. Each piece clicked softly when she checked it against the next. At number thirty-two she stopped, returned to the beginning, and counted again.

I left before learning whether I had caused the doubt.

For the next eleven warms, the cord remained across the passage. I visited twice and did not cross it. On the second visit, Suth had restored most of the lower stones, but the circle and point were hidden behind a rack of tools.

The clue had waited across three worlds. It could wait until the person caring for this wall was finished.

## Chapter 15: The Longest Minute

The cord was still up when Ottu took Stonesetter.

Underhand filled the Name Chamber from the bright end to the repair boundary. Orruk packed the entrances and stood in both side passages. Carriers remained outside because their backs blocked too much space, so families lifted soft onto them for a view. The amber veins shone through dust kicked up by hundreds of feet.

Ottu waited near the wall with its family. It had built the best practice wall I had seen, but now all four legs shook. Its mother, a Stonesetter with heavy gripping feet and broad hands, stood close enough to touch and did not.

Pell moved beside me through gaps nobody else considered gaps. “It was going to take Wallwalker last warm.”

“Ottu is poor at Wallwalker.”

“That's what I said.”

“Did that help?”

“It did not.”

The room quieted without anybody ordering it. Ottu walked to the worn hollow at the center. It faced the taking-wall. Suth stood ready with a clean tool and a space already measured for the new entry.

Ottu opened its mouth.

Nothing came out.

The silence lasted. Somewhere in the left passage, a crawler shell cracked under a foot. Ottu closed its mouth, looked toward its family, and opened it again.

“Stonesetter.”

The word was not loud. It reached the walls anyway.

Suth stepped forward at once. She placed the tool and began cutting while Ottu remained on the worn floor. The mark required twelve strokes. I counted. Between the sixth and seventh, Suth changed the angle of her wrist. Between the tenth and eleventh, Ottu's mother began crying.

Nobody spoke until the twelfth stroke ended.

Then the room made one sound out of hundreds of voices, feet, hands, and tools striking stone. Ottu turned. Its family reached it before Pell did, though Pell had placed itself well for speed. Food appeared from three directions. An aunt covered Ottu's back with a cloth that fell off twice because soft bodies were not made for ceremonies.

I remained near the edge until the crowd loosened. Ottu looked exactly as it had before. The change had begun somewhere nobody could see.

Pell climbed onto a low Carrier to find me. “It nearly went home.”

“Could it?”

“Of course.”

“After standing there?”

“After anything except saying it.”

Pell explained that soft often came to the center and left. Nobody laughed or recorded the attempt. Family brought them home, fed them, and returned on another warm if the soft wished.

“Some worlds would call that failure,” I said.

“Then they should improve their worlds.”

Ottu passed us beneath three relatives and enough food for a Deepdigger. It heard Pell and shouted that it had not almost gone home. Pell shouted back that everyone had watched it almost go home. Ottu threw a folded palm piece. A Carrier caught it without turning and ate it.

The celebration moved into the passage because the Name Chamber could not hold supper. An old Stonesetter told me she had considered Waterworks for a full year before choosing. She remembered every reason from that year and claimed she had not needed any of them since. Around her, adults compared takings separated by decades as if they had happened in the same warm.

Ottu sat near the wall, receiving advice about aches that had not started. Its mother kept touching the cloth and taking her hand away.

“Wonderful?” I asked Pell.

“Yes, I think.”

“Terrible?”

Pell considered Ottu. “Ask me when it's mine.”

I had watched one child stand inside a choice, remain free to leave, and speak anyway. In sixty days, the soft body would be gone. In eighty years, if Ottu lived that long, those twelve cuts would still name the shape laid in the wall.

Nothing about the act was forced. Nothing about it could be undone.

I wanted one of those facts to settle the other. It did not.

## Chapter 16: A Habit with Good Results

Ganno came to Ottu's taking because Ottu was his sister's child.

I found him after the celebration carrying two empty food stones toward the washing channel. He had stayed near the back during the naming and left before the old Stonesetter finished her third story.

“You are related to Ottu,” I said.

“Most people are related to somebody.”

“Pell said—”

“Pell is related to everybody after supper.”

He put the stones under the flowing water and scrubbed crawler oil from one with the flat of a claw. I waited until both were clean. Then I told him I had been searching Underhand for a false thing.

Ganno placed the first stone on the drying ledge. “Why?”

I explained the three earlier worlds. I kept the explanation short, but not simple. On one, people treated distance as property until the markers failed. On another, creatures believed entry always meant welcome, though one resident had remained inside another without consent for years. On the third, a whole way of speaking had grown around a danger that was real but smaller than the fear built over it.

Ganno asked whether everybody had been wrong. I said only one important part, and that I had found it. He rinsed the second stone before asking whether they had been pleased.

I almost said no. That would also have been too simple.

Some had been relieved. Some had become angry. On the bright plain, an old person had spent most of a life making every sentence safe against a disaster. When younger Orruk—no, they had not been Orruk; I had been in Underhand long enough to make that mistake—when younger creatures proved the danger much smaller, the old one went home carrying fewer rules. I had taken weight away. I had also taken the structure of ninety-one years and offered nothing in its place.

“Finding truth did not make it kind,” I said.

Ganno set the clean stone beside the first and pointed out that I had kept doing it. I said the next world had needed it too. He asked whether this one did.

The washing channel ran between us. I watched oil turn the water cloudy, thin out, and disappear around the bend.

“I thought it would.”

“Because all worlds have a fault?”

“The first three did.”

“Three stones in a row have grain.” Ganno picked up the top plate and checked its surface. “That doesn't mean the fourth owes you a crack.”

I disliked the comparison because it fit.

I told him what I had tested: coercion, ownership, assigned work, retaking, compounds, old words, remaining soft. The Orruk were not mistaken about the process. They knew exactly where freedom ended. They recorded failure, allowed refusal, and cared for bodies that no longer matched available work. Even the pressure around a free choice was visible in the wall if you knew where to look.

“So there isn't a hidden wrong thing,” I said.

“Perhaps not.”

“Then why am I here?”

Ganno lifted both food stones against one side. He did not know how I reached any world, and neither did I. He said that question sounded larger than Underhand's wall.

He carried the stones toward the eating passage. I followed because the conversation had not ended for me.

“Finding the wrong thing is what I do,” I said.

“You float too.”

“That is not work.”

“You ask questions.”

“That is part of finding it.”

Ganno stopped where the amber from two passages met. “Or finding it is what happened after the questions.”

I knew the difference he meant. I did not like that one either.

“You have done it three times,” he said. “That may be a skill. It may be a habit with good results.”

“What is the difference?”

“A skill waits until it is useful.”

Then he went to return the food stones.

I remained at the crossing, where four passages gave me four possible directions and no reason to select one. Since arriving in Orrun, I had treated every answer as ground between me and the fault I expected to find. Ganno had asked whether the ground led there at all.

For the first time on that world, I stopped investigating Underhand and examined the creature doing the investigating.

## Chapter 17: The Sensible Choice

I went down with Ganno again two warms later.

He did not mention our talk at the washing channel. I did not ask whether he had meant skill or habit as the kinder answer. We worked around the silence by discussing a roof seam he expected to reach before the shift ended.

The seam arrived early.

One claw-width above his marked line, the stone changed from solid red to a thin gray layer. Ganno stopped the whole face, reset two lamps, and spent most of an hour testing the ceiling. The other diggers listened to his taps and changed their positions when he pointed. Nobody complained about the lost time.

He decided the seam would hold if they curved the roof lower than planned. That meant the chamber beneath would lose height. A Carrier route marked for the far side had to move. Ganno sent a worker up with the change before anybody removed another block.

“What if you ignored it?” I asked.

“The roof might ignore me for forty years.” He pressed a claw to the gray stone. “Then it would answer somebody else.”

He altered the chamber for people he would never meet. I thought about calling that proof he belonged to the work. After the washing channel, I was less eager to turn a true fact into a whole answer.

At the end of the shift, we climbed through the dark and entered the bright passage. Ganno stopped under the frames.

This time I stopped beside him without asking why.

A Veintender was binding a split branch. Her hands crossed thin strips of wet fiber over the break, each one placed between living threads I could barely see. Ganno watched with his eyes narrowed. He held out longer than before, perhaps because he was not explaining himself to me.

“My mother took Deepdigger,” he said.

The words came without a question from me.

Her brother had taken it too. So had both of Ganno's grandparents on that side. Their work filled lower chambers he had visited as a soft. At family takings, the Deepdiggers stood together near the best food because they came up hungry and everyone made room for them.

“Nobody ordered me,” Ganno said. “They would have been ashamed to try.”

At fifteen, he had wanted the body that made his family move aside with pride. He understood stone, liked the dark, and already knew the work needed doing. His choice had been informed, useful, and celebrated. He did not describe a trapped child. He described a sensible one.

“It was a good warm,” he said. “I won't make it ugly now so I can blame somebody.”

Above us, the Veintender finished the binding and moved to the next split.

Ganno blinked water from his eyes. “I should have taken Veintender.”

There was no anger in the sentence. That made it harder to answer. Anger would have given me something to resist or support. He had offered a fact about himself and left it in the passage between us.

I thought of the second-taking wall. I did not tell him about it. He had lived in Underhand for decades; he knew where that record stood.

“When did you know?” I asked.

“Which time?”

He had known at twenty-two, then argued himself out of it. He knew again at twenty-six and spent a year helping in the vein passages. He knew at thirty, when a Deepdigger he trained made a perfect first corner and Ganno felt pride so strong it seemed like an answer. He knew again the next warm when he passed under the frames.

“It doesn't stay solved,” he said.

The light forced his eyes closed. He turned away at last and continued toward the dimmer living passages.

At his door, he paused. A thin amber growth followed the wall beside the entrance. It leaned away from the darker room and had two dead tips.

“I want to ask you something,” Ganno said.

“Ask.”

“Not tonight.” He touched one dead tip with the outside of his claw. “I need to work out the question.”

Then he went inside. I stayed with the small failing vein until its light stopped changing under my attention, which was another way of saying I did not know how to help either of them.

## Chapter 18: Passage Talk

The next warm, Pell found me before Ganno did.

It was crouched with Ottu and two other soft at a crossing, each holding a different piece of stone. Pell struck three slow taps against the wall. The answer came from a passage I could not see: one fast, two slow, one fast.

All four soft laughed.

“What did it say?” I asked.

“Nothing you need.”

Ottu told me it meant Pell's head had gone soft. Pell said Ottu had translated badly. The other two disagreed with both, and within a minute the message had become either a warning about a parent or a detailed opinion of somebody's crawler stew.

The soft had built their own language inside the workers' taps. Adults knew the common patterns for food, danger, come here, and not now. Everything beyond that moved too quickly for them or was ignored on purpose.

Pell taught me the pattern for *somebody is coming, look useful*. I struck it against the wall by moving close and pulsing my whole body. The answer accused me of having terrible rhythm.

That answer required no translation.

We spent most of the morning sending messages across three passages. I learned the warning for a ridiculous Highledge, the call for a lost cup, and a pattern so long that I forgot its beginning before Pell reached the end. Pell claimed it meant that somebody's sister had fallen into a crawler pen.

“How often does that happen?”

“Once.”

“Then why keep the message?”

“In case she does it again.”

Ottu's shoulder had started aching. The change was too early to see, but every few minutes it rolled one front leg and tried not to react. Nobody asked whether Stonesetter had been correct. They tapped insults, practiced warnings, and let Ottu shift position whenever it needed.

I had arrived expecting to observe Underhand. That morning the soft included me in a conversation I could barely perform. They corrected my timing, argued over my translation, and sent my bad tap farther through the passages than any of theirs. Somewhere beyond two bends, children I had never met repeated it worse.

I kept that morning.

Near midday, Pell asked why I had been at Ganno's door after the lower shift.

“He showed me a vein,” I said.

This was true. It also left out what Ganno had trusted me to carry.

Pell wanted to know whether the vein was good. I said it was alive. Then I tapped the pattern for lost cup, badly, and Pell spent several minutes repairing the message before somebody came looking for a cup that had not been lost.

The lie would have been to deny I had spoken with Ganno. Telling everything would have been worse. I was beginning to understand that privacy often required an answer shaped closely enough to truth that another person could act without receiving what was not theirs.

When the soft left for food, I remained at the crossing. The passages sent back taps for another hour: workers checking routes, families calling children, jokes moving farther than their reason. Each sound belonged to the person who struck it until somebody answered. Then it became part of Underhand.

Ganno's words were different. He had given them to me, not to the walls.

I do not sleep. Through the long quiet period, I floated beside the amber vein near my resting chamber and tried to guess the question he was forming. Perhaps he wanted to know whether choosing well excused regret. Perhaps he wanted proof that the Veintender self still existed somewhere inside the Deepdigger body. Perhaps he wanted me to find a flaw in the chamber after all.

Every guess turned him into a problem I already knew how to answer.

By the time the passages grew busy again, I had decided to prepare facts instead. I would learn what work he could do in the light, which tools might fit his claws, how long his eyes could bear the glow, and whether another body could supply what his lacked.

It was a thorough plan.

That should have warned me.

# Part IV: Permission to Visit

## Chapter 19: Five Good Ideas

I spent three warms building an answer before Ganno asked his question.

The first idea was time. Deepdiggers worked long lower shifts because travel to the face took so much of the warm. I checked the shift board and found no rule requiring a full one. Ganno could cut stone in the morning, eat, and spend the later hours in the vein passages.

The second idea was partnership. A Veintender had the eyes and hands for fine growth. Ganno had strength, reach, and a body that could hold a frame steady. They could divide the work by what each body did well.

I tested this with a tender named Hesk. She listened while threading support fiber through a damaged root.

“Would you work with a Deepdigger?” I asked.

“I have.”

“Ganno?”

Hesk's hand stopped. “Ask Ganno.”

That was another privacy boundary. I kept the useful half of the answer and left the rest.

The third idea was tools. Every pruning knife I had seen used a narrow handle shaped for Veintender fingers. Ganno's claws could not close around one. Sath's mother made tool grips near the upper storage route. She said a broad haft could be fitted around an ordinary blade within two warms.

“For a Deepdigger?” she asked.

“If he wants one.”

She turned a blank handle in her long Wallwalker hands. “It will be ugly.”

“Will it work?”

“Those are often different questions.”

I ordered nothing because the choice and the cost were Ganno's. Sath's mother said she would need the width around his closed claw. That measurement and any price were things to ask him about after I heard his question.

The fourth idea was shade. Ganno's eyes watered before he could study the veins. Crawler pens used thin shell screens to keep strong amber away from young crawlers. I borrowed one and held it between Ganno's door vein and the nearest lamp. The growth dimmed but remained visible.

The shell keeper warned me that the color would change through the screen. A Veintender might reject it for fine reading. Ganno was not reading fine changes yet. Seeing longer seemed worth seeing less clearly.

The fifth idea was work already suited to him. Old thick growths near living passages required bulk cutting. Their roots were established, their branches broad, and the work depended more on controlled force than delicate sight. Ganno could do it without pretending to have Veintender fingers.

I did not guess at this one. I watched a crew remove a dead limb the width of Pell. Two Veintenders spent half the warm sawing it free. A Deepdigger could have made the cut quickly if somebody marked the exact place.

By the third warm, I had facts, names, costs, and limits. I knew which shift keeper could alter Ganno's hours, which Veintender needed frame help, where the handle could be made, how much shell a shade required, and which old roots were due for cutting.

I found Ganno outside his room tending the small vein.

He used a piece of wet cloth held between two claws. Most of the water squeezed out before it reached the root. He adjusted his grip, tried again, and managed to wrap the cloth around one dead tip.

“You wanted to ask me something,” I said.

Ganno looked at the vein. “I did.”

“I have been working on it.”

He glanced toward me then. “You don't know the question.”

“I know the problem.”

I began with time. He listened without interruption. I described half-shifts, then partnership with a Veintender. At tools, he lowered the wet cloth. At the shell screen, he looked toward the amber branch above the passage. I finished with bulk cutting and the crew that had spent half a warm on one limb.

Five practical changes. None broke a law. None asked the Name Chamber to reverse itself. Each could begin within a few warms.

I had done good work.

Ganno waited after I stopped, perhaps to make sure there was not a sixth idea hiding behind me.

“The shade is new,” he said. “That may help.”

I was pleased. I wish I had noticed it was the only part of my answer he had accepted.

## Chapter 20: Permission to Visit

Ganno put the wet cloth back on the dead tip.

“Half-shifts,” he said. “I worked them for two years.”

My pleasure ended.

At twenty-two, when the wanting first stayed long enough to frighten him, he asked the lower shift keeper for mornings only. Nobody refused. He spent each afternoon in the bright passages until the light pain followed him into the dark.

“Then I changed the order,” he said. “Veins first, digging later. That was worse. My eyes were finished before I reached work.”

Partnership had been Hesk. Ganno had held frames, moved root beds, carried water, and cut the thick branches she marked. Hesk had been patient. She had explained colors more than once and never laughed when he missed them.

“Why did you stop?” I asked.

“Because she was kind.”

I waited. That answer needed more.

Ganno tightened the cloth. “Every time she checked my cut, I could see her making room for what I couldn't see. She gave me the useful part because she wanted me to have it. I began hating her for being good to me.”

He had left before that hatred became something he acted on. He still greeted Hesk. He still admired her work. The kindness had not been wrong. Neither had leaving it.

The thick-handled tool had existed too. Ganno showed it to me inside his room. The haft was wrapped around a pruning blade and worn smooth where his claw pressed. It had worked well enough for broad cuts. It sat on a shelf under dust.

“Sath's mother made this?” I asked.

“Her teacher.”

“Then the tool is older than Sath.”

“Most useful things are.”

He had done bulk pruning for years. The old vein beside his door was his, as much as any living growth could belong to a person. He watered it, cut dead branches, fed the root paste, and failed to understand why it leaned away from his dark room. Veintenders offered advice. Some helped. The growth remained poor.

Of my five ideas, four had arrived decades late.

“The shade may still help,” Ganno said.

It was kind of him to repeat that. I wished he had not.

“Why didn't you tell me?” I asked.

“You were enjoying yourself.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It was a reason for about five minutes.” He removed the cloth again. “Then I wanted to know how many you would find.”

I had treated him like an untested system. Ask enough Orruk, collect enough tools, remove every obstacle. I had been proud that each answer respected his body. I had not asked what he had already tried with that body during thirty years of wanting.

“You said you had a question.”

“I do.”

“Was it how to work in the veins?”

Ganno set the cloth in a bowl. Water ran from one edge and crossed the floor toward a drain. “I know how to work in the veins.”

He had proved that. The chamber allowed him. Hesk had welcomed him. The shift keeper changed his hours. Toolmakers adapted grips. No person and no rule kept Ganno from standing in the brightest passage every warm.

“I can go there whenever I want,” he said. “I can stay until I cannot open my eyes. I can hold a frame. I can carry their water. I can cut where somebody else points.”

“Then what did you want?”

He turned one broad claw under the amber from the door vein. The light reached its worn flat tip.

“I wanted to know the color before Hesk told me. I wanted hands that touched a branch and knew where the living thread ran. I wanted eyes that opened wider when the passage got bright.”

He lowered the claw.

“The work was never the whole of it. The work was the part everybody could offer. I wanted the body that belonged there.”

I had built five bridges into the vein passages. Ganno did not need another bridge. Underhand had never forbidden him to cross.

It had given him permission to visit in a body that could never arrive.

## Chapter 21: The Wrong True Things

“Then there is nothing I can change,” I said.

Ganno looked at the bowl of wet cloth. “I did not ask you to change it.”

The words left an empty place where all my work had been. I wanted to defend the five ideas, especially the shade, which was still good. I wanted to explain that making a life less painful was worth doing even if the body remained wrong. Both responses would have asked Ganno to comfort me for misunderstanding him.

I said nothing.

He picked up the bowl and carried it inside. His room was low and dim, built for a Deepdigger body. A reinforced sleeping ledge ran along one wall. Stone-reading tools rested in fitted slots near the door. The old pruning knife occupied a shelf above them where it did not fit anything around it.

Ganno set the bowl down. “I want you to tell me what you see.”

I looked at the failing vein outside.

“Not that.” He stood in the center of his room. “Me.”

“I see a Deepdigger.”

“Everybody does.”

He explained the problem slowly. Every Orruk in Underhand had known him after the choice. Even those old enough to remember soft Ganno also remembered the taking and the sixty days that made his present body. When they looked at his claws, they saw the word he had spoken. They saw excellent work, family resemblance, useful strength, and the life that followed.

I had not been in the room when he chose.

“Tell me what you see without that day,” he said.

It should have been an easy request. Observation was the thing I trusted most.

I began with his stone reading. I described the first clean block I watched him free, the gray roof seam he caught before anybody cut under it, and the corners other diggers came to study. I told him he never missed the safety tap. I mentioned the meal he finished before another worker opened her bowl, because a true picture of Ganno had to include hunger.

He listened without moving.

I told him his family made room for Deepdiggers at the good end of the table and that he had become a person worth making room for. I told him he carried more than most Orruk, changed plans before a future roof could harm a stranger, and knew when a passage had been cut by somebody in a hurry.

The list kept growing. Each fact was accurate. Together they made a strong account of the Deepdigger everybody already saw.

I heard that before I stopped speaking.

I could have changed course. I might have spoken about the way he watched Hesk's hands, the three extra cuts he endured before leaving the light, or the old tool kept above the proper ones. Instead I finished the answer I had prepared because the facts were good and I did not want to admit, while giving them, that they were wrong.

Ganno waited through the last sentence.

“Thank you,” he said.

The words were polite and final. He did not argue, because nothing I had said was false. He did not ask again, because he had already asked as clearly as he could.

I looked around the room. Tools in fitted slots. Low ledge. Covered lamp. A body with heavy plates standing where every object expected it. I had described the fit and mistaken it for the person.

“That did not answer you,” I said.

“It failed.”

“I knew before I finished.”

Ganno turned toward the door vein. “Yes.”

That answer cost more than anger would have.

I left. In the passage, the amber from the stronger wall growth reached to the edge of his door and stopped. I could have gone back with a better sentence. A sentence offered quickly would only have been a sixth idea.

So I went away carrying eleven or twelve true things, none of which had done the work he trusted me to do.

## Chapter 22: Wend's Two Piles

I met Wend because I took a wrong passage and did not ask Pell to correct me.

The route narrowed, bent twice, and ended at a chamber warm enough for crawler eggs. An Orruk sat on the floor sorting empty shells. She placed smooth halves to her left and cracked ones to her right. Three baskets waited behind her, though I could not tell what would go in the third.

She looked like Pell at the other end of a lifetime: the same small body, pale skin, broad dim eyes, and flexible hands that had not changed toward any work. Fine folds around her eyes and across her leg joints showed the years between them.

“You're the floating visitor,” she said.

“That is me.”

“You can hover there if you like. Sitting seems unlikely.”

I hovered.

Her name was Wend. She was sixty years old and had never taken another one.

I had imagined meeting a lifelong soft several times after Pell told me three lived in Underhand. In one version the adult was ashamed. In another, brave. In a third, angry at a settlement built for bodies that chose. Those versions would have given me an obvious question.

Wend was busy with shells.

The smooth halves went to young crawlers as feed dishes. The cracked ones would be ground into shade paste. When I asked about the empty third basket, she said it was for pieces she had not decided about, which explained why it contained nothing.

She asked where I had arrived and whether I liked wall light. I asked how long she had lived in that chamber. We discussed a seep that made one corner damp, a family down the route that argued through every meal, and the proper way to roast crawlers. Wend agreed with Ganno's sister, though she described the heat differently.

Nobody entered to feed or check her. During the afternoon, a Carrier left a basket of palm at the doorway and Wend called thanks without stopping work. Later, a soft brought news about Ottu's shoulder. Wend traded two pieces of glowfruit for a repaired shell scoop. She could not carry a full pen or reach upper storage, but the chamber did not wait around her like a place caring for somebody helpless. Her day had routes through it.

“Do people expect you to choose now?” I asked.

“At sixty?”

“The chamber would still allow it.”

“The chamber allows many things I won't be doing today.”

That was not the question I had come toward, though I had not known I was coming toward one.

“Why did you stay soft?”

Wend picked up a shell with a split near its hinge. She turned it once and placed it in the empty third basket.

“Have you tried the crawlers Ganno's sister makes?” she asked.

“I have eaten them.”

“Then you know why everybody else should stop trying.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.” Wend selected another shell. “That one was easy to sort.”

The refusal was gentle and complete. I could have asked again in a new shape. I was good at that. I could have asked whether she once wanted a name, whether fear stopped her, whether she regretted the limits, or whether the blank niche waiting at the end mattered.

I did not.

We talked until the warm changed. Wend told me which passage families had the worst crawler recipes and why Highledges should never be allowed to arrange low shelves. She taught me how to recognize a shell that looked whole but would split under weight. I found four. She found nine in the same basket.

When I left, the third basket held six pieces. Wend had created a place for uncertainty and did not seem offended when it remained partly empty.

I reached the main passage before another fact caught up with me.

Wend had not asked what I planned to take.

Pell had asked before learning where I came from. Sath asked before landing properly. Suth had asked whether my name was called or taken. Adults at meals, children in games, workers passing in passages—everybody had found a way to place that question near me.

Wend had spent most of a warm at my side and left it alone.

I could not know why. That was the point I had failed to understand with Ganno: not knowing did not always create a debt somebody else had to pay me.

## Chapter 23: The Blank Ones

The passage to the old end of the Name Chamber crossed a lower gallery I had used often. I had treated it as distance between places.

That evening I had no answer to bring Ganno and no wish to invent another one, so I moved slowly. The wall light thinned as the route descended. Long openings appeared on both sides, each large enough to hold an Orruk lying straight. Stone lips covered most of them. A name had been cut above every lip I noticed.

I stopped beside a fresh Channelcutter mark. Pale grit still clung inside the lowest stroke.

The opening below it was closed.

I knew about the burial passages. Pell had mentioned them while explaining why the deep routes stayed warmer than the upper chambers, and Suth had said that her hands served both ends of an Orruk life. I had let both facts pass because I was occupied with the wall of choices.

The gallery made those facts physical.

Named Orruk lay behind me, ahead of me, and around the bend. The nearer cuts had hard edges. Older marks had softened until wall light pooled in them. Some had been renewed by hands that followed the worn grooves with more care than perfect accuracy. No official record told families to do it. They came when a mark grew shallow and made it deep again.

I traveled farther down.

Carriers appeared often. Three Highledge marks stood together, their upper hooks repeated with small differences. A Veintender niche had a row of polished stones along its lip. Nobody had told me whether those stones belonged to the dead Orruk or to somebody still visiting, so I did not touch them.

Eleven Deepdiggers occupied one stretch of wall. The fourth mark was Ganno's mother's.

I counted them twice. That was a useless action, but it gave my attention somewhere to stand. I had eaten food she helped provide. I knew what her child had taken and what it had cost him. I did not know whether she had approved, worried, celebrated, or done all three. Her mark could not answer and Ganno had not offered.

I moved on.

Five niches later, a sealed opening had bare stone above it.

I searched the surrounding surface for a faded line. The wall was smooth except for tool ripples left when the niche had been made. I brought myself closer and changed my angle. Nothing.

The next nine had names. Then came another blank.

I found six before Pell caught up with me.

"There you are," Pell said. "I checked Wend's chamber, the eating room, Ganno's door, and three places you have never been. It was a very badly ordered search."

I pointed at the blank stone.

Pell looked where I indicated. "Yes."

"Whose niche is that?"

"No one remembers."

"Why is the name gone?"

Pell's hands folded under the front of her body. She did this when she had to work out why my question existed.

"It isn't gone," she said. "There wasn't one."

I looked back at the stone. "They stayed soft."

"Until they died."

"And they were buried here?"

"Where else would they be buried?"

Pell's answer held no challenge. I had supplied one from my own expectation. Somewhere inside me, without permission, I had built a second place: a smaller passage, perhaps, or the edge of this one, where the people who never named themselves would be put after death. Underhand had given me no reason to imagine it.

"How do you know who they were?" I asked.

"Their families know. Other people knew while they lived."

"Later."

"Later, some don't."

Pell touched the stone lip, not the blank space. "That happens to named people too. A mark says Stonesetter. It doesn't say who stole roasted palm from a cooling tray or who sang badly or who always gave away the sweet crawler legs."

I had no reply to that.

We continued together. Once I knew to look, the blanks appeared throughout the gallery. They did not occupy one period or one family stretch. Sharp-edged niches and ancient ones both held them. One stood between a pair of Carriers with matching side cuts. Another interrupted four generations of Channelcutters. A very old blank had been polished by so many passing bodies that the tool ripples were gone.

"How many blanks are there?" I asked.

Pell did not know. She offered to help me count, then warned that the galleries forked and that neither of us had eaten. I started anyway.

At twenty-three, I lost my number because two softs hurried past us arguing about whose turn it was to fetch palm. One slapped the message for liar against the wall. The other answered with the tap for an exaggerated insult I had not learned. Their noise traveled through named and blank niches alike.

I did not begin again.

For a month I had heard names discussed at meals, at work, after showings, during games, and in the quiet before sleep. People asked softs what they would become. Adults compared choices and bodies. Suth preserved six hundred years of takings so that an answer could remain available.

Nobody had pointed out the lives that supplied no answer.

They had not hidden them either. The blank spaces ran through the center of the same gallery, receiving the same stone, depth, and passage. I had crossed them again and again. My attention had gone to the writing.

Wend's reason was still hers. The wall did not solve her into courage, fear, contentment, regret, or indecision. It showed only that she belonged here without solving.

Pell had moved several niches ahead. She waited beneath an old Stonesetter mark without calling me.

I joined her.

As we left, I tried to remember the first blank I had passed on my first journey to the Name Chamber. I could not. I remembered hundreds of cut names, their patterns and numbers and changing fashion. The empty places had been present inside that memory all along, and I had made them disappear by deciding that only an answer counted as information.

## Chapter 24: That Is a Name

The cord was gone from the old end of the Name Chamber.

Suth had finished the repaired section. Every lifted piece sat in the wall again, the joins narrow and clean. Fresh stone showed around the old cuts like pale borders. The dark cloth had been folded and returned to its shelf. Only dust in the floor grooves proved that a hundred and nine choices had recently lain loose in the room.

Suth was recutting a worn mark farther along. She saw me stop where the cord had been.

"The floor won't attack you," she said.

"May I enter?"

She rested her tool against her lower arm. "You may."

I crossed the place where the boundary had stood. Nothing moved except dust under my air. I kept to the middle of the passage until Suth lifted her lamp and followed.

The circle and its central point had been returned to their column. The recut edges around it made the mark easier to see, though Suth had not deepened the mark itself. Its curve was slightly uneven. The point sat a little high. Those small differences matched the cuts I remembered beside the broken ledge and in the dark between worlds.

I had spent eleven warms arranging my first question. I had considered how to explain the cliff, the long fall, the second faint carving, and the white line where one world released me toward another. I had planned to begin with proof so Suth would understand that the mark was important.

Instead I pointed. "What does that mean?"

Suth looked.

She did not lean close. She did not adjust the lamp. Her eyes passed over the mark with the ease of eyes finding a familiar tool.

"It's a name," she said.

For several breaths, I could not make my next question fit.

The same shape had waited at the edge of my first remembered world. I had treated it as a sign meant for a traveler, or a warning nobody could translate, or evidence of whoever had built the crossings. Each idea placed the mark outside ordinary life.

Here it stood in the same columns as Carrier and Veintender.

"Which name?" I asked.

"That one."

"I mean, what does it make?"

"Nobody knows now."

Suth raised the lamp at last. The circle became a thin shadow around light.

The outcome record for that section had been lost, she explained. Some marks were older than the surviving records; others had outlasted the people who still knew what changes followed them. Nobody in her fifty years at the wall had spoken the circle-and-point name. Her own teacher had never seen a taking of it either.

"So it might make an Orruk into something no longer needed," I said.

"It could."

"Or something dangerous."

"It might."

"Or useful."

"That too."

Suth lowered the lamp. "Unknown is a large shelf. You can put many things on it."

I looked along the old column. Marks crowded one another, each a possible body without a living example. "Why keep them if you don't know the outcomes?"

Suth tapped the wall with one knuckle. "Because they're names."

She said this as if I had asked why stone was kept in a mountain.

I tried again. "Has anybody taken this one? Ever?"

"The mark wouldn't be on the taking side if nobody had."

"Recently enough to know who?"

"Not recently."

"Could you find the oldest niche with it?"

Suth's expression did not change, but my question sounded different after the gallery. A burial mark might prove that an Orruk had spoken the name. It could not restore the body, the outcome, or the reason. I would be asking Suth to search miles of dead so I could confirm what the wall already said.

"Never mind," I said.

"Good."

She returned the lamp to its hook and inspected the cut she had left. The tool settled into her hand, its working edge precisely against the worn groove.

I remained with the circle and point.

On the cliff world, the mark had been separate from any visible language. Here it belonged to a system of choices. That connection was true. It was also smaller than every conclusion I wanted to build from it.

I could not claim the same people had cut both. I did not know whether names traveled between worlds, whether the mark preceded Underhand, or whether the resemblance meant what I thought it meant. Even exact shapes could gather different meanings in different places. Suth had given me the Orruk meaning and the edge of Orruk knowledge. Beyond that edge, the unknown remained large enough for any favorite answer.

Her first strike sounded. The old chamber returned a softer copy.

A question formed behind the ones Suth had answered. It concerned the mark, but it was also personal, and asking it would have required her to reach beyond anything she knew.

Eleven warms earlier, I would have tried to make her do that. I had crossed her line because my need felt more important than her work. Since then Ganno had asked me to see him without replacing him with an answer, and Wend had shown me what a refusal looked like when it was allowed to remain whole.

Suth set the tool for another stroke. She did not ask what else I wanted.

The next impact joined the echoes of every other mark in the chamber. The circle and point stayed silent.

I left without asking my last question.

# Part V: What the Wall Cannot Say

## Chapter 25: The Thing Properly

I found Ganno beside his door vein on the next warm.

The borrowed shell shade stood against the passage lamp. It reduced the amber enough that Ganno could face the growth without closing his eyes. It also turned every living thread the color of old cooking fat. He had tied one edge to a frame and weighted the other with a digging wedge.

One of my five ideas had become real. It looked ugly and helped.

Ganno was scraping dead paste from the root with the back of his worn pruning knife. The broad handle fit his claw. The blade did not fit the work. Each stroke removed some paste and left some stone dust behind.

"You came back," he said.

"I answered the wrong question."

"You answered a question."

"I knew it was wrong before I stopped."

That made him put down the knife.

The apology I had practiced was accurate. It included my investigation, my assumption, his request, the moment I recognized my mistake, and my decision to continue anyway. It explained everything except why I had returned.

I let the practiced version go.

"May I try again?" I asked.

Ganno drew one claw through the bowl of root paste. "I didn't ask twice."

"You didn't."

"And you may still be bad at it."

"I may be."

He returned the knife to the root. He did not grant permission in words, but he did not leave. I treated that as permission to begin and nothing larger.

I started with the bright passages.

Ganno paused there after every lower shift. He could have taken a darker route home. He could have passed through quickly enough to spare his eyes. Instead he stood among the frames until water gathered at their edges, watching Veintenders touch branches and know where growth had changed.

I said he did not watch the light. He watched the people whose bodies received information from it.

The knife stopped moving.

I spoke about his door vein next. Twenty-six years of weak growth leaned away from the room. Advice had not corrected it. Water, paste, tools, partnerships, changed shifts, and stubborn attention had not corrected it. Ganno could have pulled the root out and filled the groove. He renewed it anyway.

"That may only mean I dislike losing," he said.

"You do dislike losing."

"Strongly."

"You don't keep every failed digging cut beside your bed."

Ganno looked through his door at the shelf where the old broad-handled knife had gathered dust. "No."

I told him what I had heard when he described Hesk. He had not spoken as if failed work were the injury. He spoke as if the worst part were becoming a kindness project inside the work he wanted to share. He could bear being corrected. He could not bear needing another person's pity before he was allowed to be useful.

Ganno pressed the knife flat against the stone.

The sentences came slowly. I had no clean name for the person those actions made visible. I could not turn longing into a craft title, and I would not call him a Veintender when his body and work were not those things. He had asked for what I saw before the choice. I could only describe somebody who kept approaching beauty that hurt him, who preserved the tools of an impossible life, and who still wanted to know with his own body rather than borrow certainty from another.

"You have been doing that for longer than I have known anything," I said. "I don't know a word large enough for it."

The passage carried a distant sequence of work taps. Ganno listened until the all-clear arrived, then scraped another strip of dead paste from the root.

"That is worse than your first answer," he said.

"I thought it might be."

"Less organized."

"Much less."

"You repeated yourself."

"I noticed."

He worked around a knot in the root. The shell shade shifted when the weighted edge slipped, and amber struck his face. He turned away at once. I moved the digging wedge back onto the shell without asking whether that was the kind of help he wanted; his watering bowl occupied both claws, and the shade was my borrowed object blocking a shared passage.

When the light dimmed again, Ganno looked at the vein.

"You think I watch them," he said.

"That is what I see."

"Not the growth."

"I think you watch both. I think one tells you what the other refuses you."

Ganno sat with that. I resisted the urge to improve it.

After a while he lifted the pruning knife and used its point on a blackened thread. The cut was too deep. A pale bead of sap appeared, and Ganno covered it with paste.

"Terrible vein," he said.

"It is one of the worst I have inspected."

"You have inspected very few."

"That protects the claim."

His lower plates shifted in the way they did when he was trying not to laugh. He went on repairing the damage he had just made.

Nothing about his body changed. In the next warm he would descend to the digging face, read stone better than anybody near him, carry twice what Hesk could carry, and come home through light built for different eyes. The broad knife would remain a bad tool for fine roots. The shade would help without becoming an answer.

I stayed beside the door until Ganno finished with the paste.

He never told me the second description was right. When he spoke about a new chamber planned below the last stair, he talked through the roof angles and load seams, then stopped once to point out where the door vein had produced a living tip.

It was a small green-brown hook under the ugly shade. I could not tell whether it had grown that warm or had been there for days.

Ganno did not ask me to decide what it meant.

## Chapter 26: Channelcutter

Eleven warms after I returned to Ganno, Pell sent three softs to find me.

The first said Pell was taking a name. The second said I had to hurry. The third said there was plenty of time because Pell's family had blocked the Name Chamber entrance while arguing about where everybody should stand.

All three reports were true.

The common chamber could hold Underhand for meetings, but a taking made it feel smaller. Adults filled the floor and pressed along the walls. Wallwalkers occupied the upper stone. Deepdiggers stayed near reinforced sections without being asked. Softs moved through gaps no adult body could use, carrying food, messages, and contradictory news about whether Pell had been sick once or twice.

Pell's family had brought two versions of the same palm dish. Each side claimed theirs was the traditional one.

"It cannot be traditional twice," one aunt said.

"Then yours has to stop," another answered.

Pell was not with them.

I found it near the stock wall with Sath and Ottu. Pell's skin looked paler than usual. Its flexible hands kept folding together, releasing, and folding again.

"They told everybody I was sick," Pell said.

"Were you?"

"That is private."

Sath made a choking sound that was probably laughter.

Pell turned on him. "Your showing isn't for forty warms. I will be there telling people everything."

Suth crossed the floor carrying her cutting tools. Conversation weakened around her, then rose again after she passed. She checked the worn center place, counted her blades, and adjusted a lamp. Nobody directed Pell toward her. When Pell was ready, it would move.

I asked whether the list had shortened.

"One," Pell said.

"From six?"

"From eight. It went up again."

"Do you want to tell me?"

Pell looked at the center of the room. "You can hear it when everybody else does."

That was the first privacy it requested from me all morning. I kept it.

The families finished placing themselves. Food disappeared into side passages until after the cutting. Softs came down from the wall. Somebody moved a bowl from the center at the last moment, and Suth gave its owner a look that prevented any second bowl.

Pell walked out.

The worn patch had seemed small when Ottu stood there. Around Pell, it looked enormous. Pell placed all six feet inside it, then moved the back two, then returned them almost exactly to their first positions.

Underhand became quiet.

Pell opened its mouth and closed it.

Nobody encouraged it. Nobody complained. The name could begin only when Pell made it begin, and six hundred witnesses could do nothing useful with the silence.

Someone coughed in the outer passage.

Pell said, "Channelcutter."

The word was not loud. It reached the high wall, came back once, and vanished among older echoes.

Suth began to cut.

Channelcutter was one of the most repeated marks on the taking wall. Water touched every chamber, pen, kitchen, and work route. The Orruk who kept it moving did work so constant that most people discussed it only when something clogged.

Pell had spent a year moving between rare names, admired names, difficult names, and combinations nobody had tried. It had woken me twice to compare futures. Now it stood under the ordinary word while Suth copied it into six hundred years of other ordinary choices.

At the second stroke, Suth stopped.

Pell's hands locked together.

Suth inspected the groove, selected a narrower tool, and struck the same place again. Then she continued.

Nobody explained the correction during the cutting. That was one of the rules Underhand had without naming it: the wall and the word received the room's full attention until Suth finished both.

The last stroke traveled through the floor.

Pell turned.

Relief changed its whole face before any body change could begin. For the past month, every question about Pell had pointed forward. In that moment it had an answer and was astonished to hear itself own it.

Its family reached it first. Two people cried. Three tried to feed it. The palm argument resumed when both dishes arrived together. Pell ate from both and called that the oldest tradition.

I stayed against the stock wall until the crush loosened. Ganno stood across the chamber with his family, near the passage where the light was weaker. He watched Pell's celebration. I could not tell whether he thought of his own taking, Ottu's, or only the soft in front of him. I did not go over and make him explain his face.

Pell found me after the food had traveled halfway around the room.

"Did everybody hear?" it asked.

"They did."

"Sath said I spoke into the floor."

"The floor heard too."

"That is not helpful."

"You were loud enough."

Pell released a breath. "Say it."

"Channelcutter."

Its feet moved against the stone as if the word had sent a current through them. "Again."

I repeated it.

Pell asked seven more times during the next hour. Once it made Sath say the name because my version had become unreliable through use. Once it asked Suth, who replied that she had already said it with a hammer.

Nothing visible had changed yet. During the next sixty days, pain would arrive and Pell's unfinished body would begin reorganizing itself. Its hands would find a permanent shape. Its appetite and routes and sleeping space would alter. In a year, it would work among channels. Decades later, it might know how much of this happiness had endured.

Nobody in the chamber could know that morning.

Pell knew what it wanted said now.

"Channelcutter," I told it once more.

## Chapter 27: The Room Plays Back

Underhand rang for Pell that night.

I had heard ringing three times before. The sound traveled through the red stone and reached other passages as a broad pulse followed by smaller returns. From far away, it resembled the work taps below, except nobody changed course and nobody answered.

Inside the Name Chamber, it was a different thing.

The food was gone. Families had cleared bowls and cloths from the floor. Six hundred Orruk returned and sat among the names, close enough that plates and feet touched. Pell lasted until everybody had settled before falling asleep against Ottu's new Stonesetter shoulder.

An old Ringer waited in the middle with a short mallet.

Underhand had two Ringers. People talked about both with the admiration usually reserved for excellent roofs and unusually sweet glowfruit. The name changed the body less obviously than Deepdigger or Wallwalker, but the work demanded another kind of map: every stone place that answered, the strength it tolerated, the echoes a chamber could carry, and the order that turned separate blows into one event.

The Ringer began behind the stock wall.

Her first strike was soft enough that I thought the mallet had missed. The stone answered from high above us. A second sound came from the entrance passage, thinner and later. Then a third appeared beneath my body, though the mallet had not moved.

Nobody spoke.

The Ringer waited until the last return disappeared. She crossed to a darker section beside the old names and struck again. This tone opened low, split against the carved columns, and came back carrying hundreds of shallow interruptions. I could hear that the wall was cut without hearing any single mark.

I expected a pattern I could count. After six blows I had three possible patterns and confidence in none of them.

The Ringer used gaps as carefully as impacts. Sometimes she stood with the mallet lowered while the room held a fading vibration. Orruk waited without shifting. A child near the entrance whispered once and was quieted by four relatives at the same time.

I moved upward so my air would not disturb anyone's skin. From near the ceiling I could see the full arrangement: small softs folded between adult bodies, Wallwalkers clinging where the floor crowd ended, Carriers occupying wide spaces, Veintenders with their narrow hands still, Deepdiggers placed where stone carried their weight.

Every named body changed how the room held sound.

The Ringer knew that too. She adjusted when a Deepdigger coughed. She allowed Pell's sleeping foot to slide off Ottu without losing the return she was following. Once she struck an apparently ordinary patch near Suth's tools, and the floor sent a hum through every contact point. Orruk felt it through feet, plates, tails, hands, and whatever else their choices had given them.

I felt it through the air around me.

The sound did not belong only to the mallet. Wall height lengthened it. Passage mouths thinned it. Four hundred years of cuts broke clean tones into rougher ones. The stock side and the taking side answered differently, although both were made from the same red stone.

Suth sat near her restored section. She watched the Ringer's working hand instead of the wall. I wondered whether she heard each old cut she had repaired. I did not ask.

The ringing continued for two hours.

Partway through, the Ringer entered the worn center where Pell had stood. She raised the mallet but did not strike the floor. Instead she faced the taking wall and hit a point just above the newest Channelcutter mark.

The answer reached us from the old end first.

Pell woke, listened for half a breath, and went back to sleep. Ottu adjusted its shoulder beneath Pell with a wince and then held still.

Across the chamber, Ganno rested against the back wall.

The amber light there was bright enough to make his eyes water. They were closed. With sight removed, his face had none of the tightening I saw when he stood among the vein frames. Sound reached him through the wall and the heavy plates of his chosen body. He did not need Hesk to tell him which color had changed. He did not need another person to point out the living thread.

I could have decided the ringing gave him what the light withheld. That would have been another solution built while watching from across a room.

What I saw was smaller. Ganno stayed in a lit chamber without pain showing on his face. His eyes remained closed. When the Ringer delayed one expected return, his front claw shifted against the floor before the sound arrived.

The movement came before the sound.

The final blow landed near the chamber entrance. Its first answer rose behind the takings. The second came from the floor. A faint third return passed through the wall of unused names and reached the old end last.

The Ringer lowered her mallet.

Nobody broke the silence immediately. The room seemed to be waiting for one more response from itself.

Then Pell woke and asked whether the ringing meant it was definitely a Channelcutter.

Half the chamber laughed. The other half began moving, which sounded much larger.

Ganno opened his eyes only after somebody shaded the nearest lamp to clear the wall. He found me above the crowd and lifted one claw.

I did not go down to tell him what the ringing meant.

I lifted my edge back.

## Chapter 28: The Quiet Showing

Two warms after the ringing, Underhand gathered again.

This time no food argument reached the corridor. Nobody called down the passages with last-minute news. Orruk arrived steadily and took places along a living chamber near the upper routes, where the floor was wide and the amber growth had been trimmed back.

The showing belonged to Merrow.

I had seen her taking record in Suth's deep section. Merrow had joined two old names whose separate outcomes were imperfectly known and whose compound outcome had no living example. The last Orruk recorded under that combined mark had spoken it three centuries earlier.

Merrow knew the risk when she chose.

Knowledge did not make the finished body useful.

She waited in a side passage until her family signaled. The crowd opened a path, and Merrow entered on narrow limbs with too many bending places. Her body was long enough that the rear half remained outside after her head reached the center. She folded one joint, then another, testing where the weight could settle. The adult shape looked complete. Nothing had failed in the change.

Underhand simply had no work prepared for it.

The limbs could not brace widely enough to move stone. Their joints bent poorly for wall climbing and placed the body too high for channel work. Merrow's reach exceeded a Carrier's, but the thin structure could not hold a full load. The head turned far enough to inspect veins, while the eyes lacked the fine response of a Veintender's.

I knew those facts because Merrow demonstrated them.

She crossed to a low block, set three limbs around it, and lifted. The block rose a finger's height before two joints shook. Merrow returned it carefully. Next she reached toward a trimmed amber branch. The upper limb arrived easily, but the gripping end closed on empty air beside the stem.

Nobody told her to try again.

At Rin's showing, relatives had called out details as soon as she entered: useful webbing, good back width, a familiar shoulder line. Praise had filled every change until the body became a list of things it could do.

Nobody offered Merrow a false list.

I expected the quiet to turn cruel as it lengthened. Merrow stood in the center with her forward joints drawn close. Six hundred Orruk looked at the body she had chosen and found no needed craft inside it.

A small soft walked out of the crowd carrying a bowl of glowfruit.

I saw nobody send the child. It stopped beneath Merrow's front limb, discovered that Merrow could not lower the gripping end without folding most of her body, and placed the bowl on the floor instead.

Merrow bent. It took time. One narrow joint settled, the next locked, and a rear limb slid outward until two adults moved their feet. Nobody hurried her. When she reached the bowl, the soft took one piece too.

That ended the showing without anybody announcing it.

Food entered from every passage. Orruk moved from their viewing places and formed smaller groups across the chamber. Merrow's family spread a cloth along the open center rather than near a wall, because her body could fold there without blocking an entrance. People brought dishes to that cloth, ate, left, and returned with others.

Nobody pretended the body suited a task. Nobody asked Merrow to defend choosing the compound. Nobody explained that it might become useful someday. The risk had been real before she spoke and remained real after everybody saw what it had cost.

They ate with her anyway.

Pell reached the cloth carrying both disputed palm dishes from its taking. Merrow's longest limb knocked one bowl sideways. Pell caught it against its chest and announced that Channelcutter reflexes had begun early.

"You have not changed," Sath said.

"Then I was always excellent."

Merrow made a sound that might have been a laugh. Her family laughed after it, and soon Pell was retelling the bowl rescue with a larger spill and a more dangerous angle.

I moved around the meal looking for its rule.

There was none I could identify. No elder directed the visits. Suth remained only long enough to eat one piece of fruit, then returned to work. Hesk brought root paste and spoke about the chamber trimming, not Merrow's joints. Ganno arrived late from below, left his tools near the entrance, and took a place where Merrow could see him without turning the long body.

The meal lasted most of a warm.

Merrow sometimes joined a conversation and sometimes watched. Once she attempted to shift closer to a new group and discovered that three bodies occupied the space her rear joints needed. The three moved. No apology passed in either direction.

This did not erase the cost. When the food was gone, Merrow still had no established work, no route designed for her shape, and no other adult body to teach her its balance. Tomorrow would require practical decisions the meal did not make.

The gathering gave her one thing only: a first hour in the completed body that did not require a defense or a lie.

I understood that much. I did not know whether Merrow felt comforted, exposed, exhausted, or all of them in turns. Nobody explained the gathering to me, and I had learned enough to leave that absence alone.

When Merrow rose to go, the chamber opened a route around her before she chose one.

## Chapter 29: A Space on the Wall

The warm before I left, Suth offered me the stock.

I had come to the Name Chamber to look once more at the circle and point. Suth was cutting a common Carrier mark near the bright end. The strikes were quick because she had made that shape hundreds of times.

"You have spent four weeks staring at my wall," she said.

"I have also listened to it."

"That was somebody else's work."

Suth finished the hook, brushed grit from the groove, and inspected the line. "Choose one."

I thought she was telling me to choose a mark for study.

"Which section?"

"The stock. Take a name."

Her tool returned to the Carrier stroke.

The offer had no ceremony. Suth did not challenge me to prove courage or imply that I had waited too long. She had watched a young-looking creature wander between names for a month. Recommending one was as practical as telling me which passage served food.

That made it harder to dismiss.

I moved toward the stock wall. The common names began where the light was strongest, with outcomes visible in every passage. Older shapes continued into shadow. Somewhere beyond the lamp's best reach waited the circle with its point.

"I may not be able to," I said.

"Every soft can."

"I am not an Orruk."

"You've mentioned that."

"I don't know whether I have the part a name changes."

Suth stopped cutting.

During my first warm, Pell had called me soft because I looked unfinished. Other Orruk had treated the description as a puzzle, a mistake, or a temporary condition. Suth now looked at me without fitting me into one of those versions. Her eyes traveled across my edge, the open space below me, and the body that had remained the same across every world I remembered.

"Hm," she said.

It was the longest answer Underhand possessed for facts that would not fit yet.

Suth resumed the Carrier mark. She completed one side, changed tools, and began deepening the center. I assumed the offer had ended.

"I cut the burial marks," she said after several strokes.

"I saw the blanks."

"Then you finally looked at that wall too."

She used the same hand and most of the same tools for both chambers. At a taking, she placed the chosen name among earlier choices. After death, she repeated it above a sealed niche. For some Orruk, her hand made the same name twice across one lifetime: once after the choice and once at its end. She had cut the Deepdigger mark above Ganno's mother.

Suth checked the Carrier line with one fingertip.

"Sometimes I have no mark to make at the niches," she said. "The place is still there."

The hammer settled again.

Wend would receive that space. So would every soft who never spoke from the worn floor. Their families might remember their call-names and the specific ways they stole food, sang badly, repaired shells, or refused questions. The wall would carry none of it.

"Does leaving it blank bother you?" I asked.

Suth considered this longer than she had considered the ancient name.

"It bothers my hand," she said. "A finished row with nothing cut looks unfinished. The people never complained."

"They were dead by then."

"They also did not complain before."

I could have told her about Wend's third basket. I did not know whether Wend would want her reason carried into the Name Chamber, and the shell story would not answer Suth's trade problem anyway.

"The blank will remain," Suth said. "Stone is good at that."

She returned to work.

I faced the stock.

On three worlds, I had searched for a fact that would explain what I was. I expected the answer to exist somewhere outside me: in a broken machine, a remembered warning, a repeated mark, or a person who recognized my shape. If I investigated well enough, the hidden fact would become visible and I would finally match it.

Underhand offered a different process. Orruk did not uncover names buried inside themselves. They spoke one choice, changed toward it, and spent the rest of life discovering what the choice had made possible and what it had cost.

The wall held both the freedom and its danger. Ganno had chosen reasonably and wanted another body. Pell had chosen happily without knowing the next thirty years. Merrow had accepted an old risk and entered a future with no useful shape waiting for her. Wend had chosen no name I could see and still possessed a whole life.

None of them gave me an instruction.

I did not step into the worn center. I did not test the circle-and-point name or select a safer one. My refusal was not proof that I lacked the changing part. It was not proof that I possessed it.

I was not ready to learn through my body in front of Suth's wall.

Suth finished the Carrier name and moved to the next unfinished mark. She neither repeated her offer nor withdrew it.

Before Orrun, I had asked what answer the worlds were keeping from me. The question waiting as I left the chamber was less generous: whether there had ever been an answer to find, or only a choice I had not made.

I disliked that question.

I trusted it more.

## Chapter 30: The Way Down

I told three people I was leaving.

Wend received the news while sorting crawler shells. Her third basket contained four pieces that morning, none of them the six I remembered. She had decided those and found new ones that resisted her.

"Do you know where you're going?" she asked.

"I have no destination."

"Do you know when?"

"Today."

"Half a plan."

She handed me a shell whose outer curve looked smooth. A hairline crack opened along the inner hinge, visible only when I turned it toward the wall light.

"Cracked," I said.

"Eventually."

I placed it in the third basket.

Wend nodded as if I had understood enough. She told me again that Ganno's sister was the only person in Underhand who roasted crawlers correctly. I told her this was the fourth time she had made the claim.

"Then you have had four chances to learn."

Wend did not ask whether I would take a name somewhere else. She did not ask what answer I would carry from Orrun. I thanked her for the afternoon, the shells, and the question she had refused.

"You are welcome for two of those," she said.

I left while she was testing another hinge.

Pell found me before I could find it.

The first bodily change had started in its shoulders. Nothing looked different, but Pell reported each ache with the precision of a mining alarm. The right side hurt when it lifted a bowl. The left had produced three separate pains during sleep. A place between them had begun something Pell described as pressure, stretching, or perhaps greatness.

"Channelcutters need excellent shoulders," it said.

"You learned that after choosing?"

"I knew generally."

"That means no."

Pell walked with me through the upper eating passage. Its front feet moved carefully, though it denied doing anything differently. It had begun keeping a list of foods that tasted wrong during the change. The list contained one item, and Pell had disliked that item before.

At the stair mouth, it stopped.

"Will you come back?" Pell asked.

I did not know whether any crossing returned me to a world. I did not know what chose the next opening or whether Orrun could appear again. Promising would have comforted both of us for the length of the stair and become false afterward.

"I will try," I said. "I cannot tell you what that means."

Pell rubbed one shoulder with two hands. "Then if you come back, I will show you the channels."

"All of them?"

"The important ones."

"Which are those?"

Pell looked offended. "I have sixty days to find out."

It asked me to say the name once more. I did. Pell listened as if I had not repeated it nine times already.

I continued down.

Ganno waited at the first deep landing with a work lamp covered for his eyes. He had his cutting tools, food load, and the small gray wedge he used to test cracks. Nothing in his equipment suggested a farewell. He looked prepared for an ordinary lower shift.

"Pell said you were leaving," he said.

"Pell sent three versions of the news ahead of me."

"One of them said you had already gone."

We descended together.

Ganno described the chamber his crew would begin in the next warm, four levels below his current work. Another digger had planned a broad roof with a center support. Ganno intended to narrow the north edge, carry the load into two natural seams, and leave the center open for future movement.

He drew the shape with a claw whenever we reached a dusty ledge. The first drawing put the entrance on the wrong side because we were facing uphill. Ganno erased it and began again without changing the story.

"The old plan would hold," he said. "It would also make every Carrier swear at us for eighty years."

"They could take a narrower name."

"Tell them that from a safe passage."

He sounded cheerful. The new room interested him. Twenty years from now, he expected to be correcting somebody else's roof there or explaining why his own had been better. He would still require dark routes and heavy meals. He would still pass through the vein light with eyes that narrowed against it.

Knowing his longing had not made his Deepdigger work false. The grief and the skill occupied the same body without resolving each other.

At the lowest finished stair, the amber wall lines stopped.

Ganno set down his lamp. Beyond us, work light appeared in scattered pools: covered bowls near the cutting faces, pale guide marks, occasional sparks where metal met stone. Deeper still, the mountain held complete dark.

"This is where I stop," Ganno said.

He meant where he stopped walking with me. He might also have meant where Underhand stopped. I did not ask him to make one sentence carry both.

"Right," I said.

"Right."

We stood there for several breaths. No apology remained to give. No promise could improve the vein outside his door or replace the body that knew stone with one that knew living color. Ganno had trusted me with something he had not said aloud before, and I had answered badly, then less badly. That was the size of what existed between us.

He picked up his lamp.

"The left wall past the second cutting face sheds," he said. "Don't float close."

"I won't."

Ganno turned uphill toward his crew. I waited until his covered light disappeared around the bend, then entered the dark.

Six Deepdiggers were working at the active face. Their blows arrived in sequence, spaced so each worker could hear the stone's response. I crossed behind the tool line only after the safety tap. One digger noticed me, pointed down, and returned to the cut when I moved that way.

Below them lay the newest completed chamber. Its corners remained sharp and pale. The next opening had only a roof and half a floor. Tool grooves ended abruptly along its right side, each one waiting for another warm.

I passed the last place anybody had finished.

I passed the last place anybody had begun.

The stair became rough footholds, then isolated cuts, then unworked slope. Ganno's warning proved accurate: a thin layer peeled from the left wall and broke through my lower air. I moved to the center.

Behind me, the work rhythm weakened. Individual blows joined into one distant pulse. Far above that, too faint to hear, were the eating rooms, crawler pens, Name Chamber, burial galleries, Wend's baskets, Pell's aching shoulders, and Ganno's shaded door vein.

Ahead, the red stone continued without mark or lamp.

The descent ended against a wall.

I searched it because searching is what I do. The surface carried no tool cut, no door edge, no circle and point. Air did not move through a seam. The mountain was closed and had never needed to be otherwise.

It was an ordinary dead end.

Then it opened.

Red stone vanished from in front of me. Black sky spread beyond it, divided by a white line. The shape waited there, the same impossible boundary that had carried me to four worlds and explained none of them.

I did not know why it had appeared at the bottom of Underhand. I did not know whether the ancient name had anything to do with it. Suth's wall had given me a connection and kept the explanation.

For once, I did not feel cheated by that.

I turned before crossing.

The route upward held layers of light. Closest was the black where untouched stone met the new cuts. Above it lay the dim brown of work lamps, then the faint amber line of settled passages. Higher than I could see, vein rooms would be bright enough to hurt Ganno when he returned from the next shift. He would probably stop there anyway. Pell would wake with a new ache and announce it. Wend would decide one shell and place another in the uncertain basket.

Those were predictions, not knowledge. The world would continue without arranging itself into an answer for me.

The white line remained open.

I faced it carrying no name and no proof that a name could hold me. I still wanted to know what I was. Orrun had changed the direction of that wanting. Somewhere ahead there might be another fact, another mark, or another person who saw something I missed. I would look.

I would also ask whether the answer was mine to demand, whether the person before me wanted a solution, and whether an empty place had been visible all along.

Then I went through.
