SEER WORLDS: THE OPEN DOOR

# Part I: Doors

## Chapter 1: Underwater

The first fact was water. I arrived inside it with no warning, the way I arrive everywhere, and waited to see whether that was going to be a problem. It was warm. It held me up. I could look through it and hear through it, and I did not need to breathe, so after a moment I decided I was probably safe. Probably is useful when you do not know anything else.

Below me lay a floor of pale stone. The grain ran in long gray marks, all pointing toward a darker stretch in the distance. Above me, a bright surface moved without going anywhere. It broke the light into pieces and sent them sliding over the bottom. One moment a patch of stone was white. The next it was the color of old bone. I watched the change happen six times before I remembered that counting the light would not tell me where I was.

I moved lower. That gave me another fact. The water near the floor carried fine grit, and my movement lifted it. The cloud drifted toward the dark stretch instead of spreading evenly. There was a direction here, even when the water looked still.

Then I heard two people arguing about a basket. They were far enough away that I could not find them. One voice claimed the basket had been left beside yellow weed. The other said the weed had grown beside the basket, which was apparently a different matter and worth becoming angry about. Their words traveled cleanly through the water while their bodies remained hidden.

I listened until both voices began repeating themselves. People do that when they have reached the part of an argument where the argument has become more important than the answer. That was familiar. The large shapes on the floor were not.

At first I took them for rocks. They rose from the pale stone in uneven rows, each one about the size of a chair, though a chair would not have been useful underwater. Their curved backs carried bands of brown, green, and yellow. Soft growth waved from some of them. One had a broken ridge along its side. Another had three small baskets set beside it.

There were too many to be natural and too many to be abandoned. I went to the nearest one and stopped a safe distance away. Nothing happened. I moved around it once. Its lower edge joined the floor so closely that I could not see a gap. The growth on its back bent with the water. The shape itself did not shift.

“Hello?” I said. No answer came from the shape. The basket argument continued somewhere beyond it.

I was deciding whether to touch the ridge when something pale crossed between us so quickly that I lost it. The grit below me rose in a curl. I turned toward the movement. The small creature had already circled back and was hanging in the water close enough to examine me.

It was soft and no larger than a hand. Short limbs ran along both sides of its body and never all moved in the same direction. Four held a tight bundle against its underside. The others paddled, pointed, folded, and began some new movement before the first was finished. I could not find a face, but I knew exactly when its attention settled on me.

“I don't know you,” it said.

I told it that we shared that problem.

“I know everyone.” It passed behind me, then above, completing a full inspection before I could turn halfway. “So you must be new. Where did you come from?”

I looked up at the shifting brightness. I looked across the stone. There was no opening, mark, or trail that could answer for me.

“Somewhere else,” I said.

It said that was not a place. I said it had been one while I was there.

The creature paused. Its side limbs gave a quick ripple that I later learned was laughter. “What have you brought?”

I had brought nothing. It made another circuit, slower this time, as if the missing cargo might appear from a better angle. “Then why did anyone send you?”

That was the second question I could not answer. The first was how I had arrived.

“Nobody sent me.”

The creature tucked the bundle closer. For the first time, most of its limbs became still. I had managed to say something stranger than my appearance.

It made me confirm that I had come without cargo or instructions. Then it asked, “What are you supposed to do?”

“I find out where I am.”

“You are on Sull.” The reply came at once, and the paddling began again. “There. Now what?”

I had no idea. If you had been beside me, you might have said that learning a world's name should count for more than one breath. I agreed, but Sull had already become impatient with me.

The creature turned and sped between two of the great shells. I called after it and asked where it was going.

“To finish this delivery. You can come if you keep out of the silt.”

It did not check whether I followed. I did, because I knew one word about that world, and it knew everything else.

## Chapter 2: The Delivery

Keeping out of the silt was harder than the creature made it sound. It traveled in curves, sharp drops, and sudden climbs, while I needed a moment to change direction. Every time I corrected toward it, the current carried the cloud from my last correction across its path. After the third cloud it accused me of doing that on purpose. When I said I was not, it told me that made it worse.

It told me its name was Nit only after it had told me seven other things: that the bundle was for Olla; Olla disliked late deliveries; the basket argument belonged to Coll and Marr; Marr was wrong; Coll had also been wrong yesterday; and neither fact would have the smallest effect on the argument. Nit spoke while going around the shells instead of over them, although going over would have been shorter.

I asked why it turned so often. Nit pointed three limbs toward three separate shells. “Because people need things. If I go straight, I miss everyone.”

That answer taught me more than the name Sull had. We passed a brown shell with a ridge worn smooth. Two long arms came out from under its forward curve and sorted strips of weed into a basket. No body followed them. A voice from inside called a greeting, and Nit struck one of the arms with a side limb as it passed.

“That's Coll,” Nit said. “He gives away the good weed and complains when he runs short.” Coll lifted an arm toward me and asked whether I was new. Nit answered, “Very,” for me, and we were already gone.

After that I knew where to look. Every great shell had two arms working near the floor. Some raked grit into shallow lines. Some cut weed or tied bundles. One pair of arms held the basket that two distant voices were still arguing about. Whenever Nit approached, the arms reached toward it with an object, a request, or both. Nit accepted three messages, refused a piece of weed, changed the destination of one basket, and somehow kept Olla's bundle under the same four limbs.

I asked whether all the people were inside the shells. Nit turned backward while still traveling forward and made me explain what I meant. “They aren't inside them. Those are their homes.”

I looked back at Coll. Only the working arms showed beyond his curved wall. When I asked where the rest of him was, Nit gave its ripple-laugh again. “There is no rest. That's Coll.”

That was when I stopped thinking the shells were buildings. They were bodies and homes together, or they were bodies that made homes of themselves. I did not yet know which wording the Kelt would accept. Nit did not stay still long enough for the question.

We came to a dark green shell set among taller weed. Its arms were folded close to the rim. Nit slowed for the first time. All the loose movement drew in, and it held the wrapped bundle carefully before a flat plate fitted into the upper shell.

“Olla,” Nit called. “I brought what Sennet tied.”

The plate lifted on one side. The opening beneath it was black until a weak warm light reached the gap. An arm came up from inside, felt along the bundle, and took it. Nit said, “Carried whole, kept dry, nobody opened it,” in a quick formal run. The plate settled back against its rim.

Then Nit burst away from Olla so quickly that I remained looking at the closed plate.

I called Nit back and asked about the moving part. It first told me I had seen a delivery, then understood which part of the delivery I meant.

“Olla's door.” Nit had the strained patience of somebody dealing with a difficult child, though Nit was the one I would later learn counted as young. “You open it. Things go through. You shut it.”

I asked whether Olla could come through it. Every limb on Nit stopped.

The working arms around us continued. Water combed the tall weed. Somewhere behind us, Marr shouted that a basket could not own the plant that grew beside it. Nit stayed still long enough that I checked whether I had asked a question at all or made a noise that meant something terrible on Sull.

“Olla is set,” Nit said at last. “She doesn't go anywhere.” When I asked whether that meant never, Nit asked why she would need to. Her home was here.

I looked along the bank. Hundreds of arms reached from hundreds of shells, but nothing large moved between them. The only bodies crossing the open floor were Nit and the other small pale creatures. Baskets did not carry themselves. Messages had no other route.

Nit had already resumed paddling. “I have six more stops.” I reminded it that it had started with one. “I did when I met you.”

Now there were strips of weed looped around two limbs and three spoken messages to keep straight. Nit seemed pleased by the increase. I followed again, this time watching who moved and who could not.

## Chapter 3: A Place Chosen

By the next light I had divided the bank into two kinds of Kelt.

The set ones could turn a little against the stone. Their arms reached, sorted, cut, traded, and sometimes pointed with great force at a neighbor. Everything else about them stayed where it had grown. The loose ones went everywhere. They carried food and tools, but those were the least interesting things they carried. A warning about rough water crossed the whole bank through them. So did news that Marr had finally given Coll his basket and had done it in a way that proved she remained right.

I counted thirty-two loose Kelt before Nit told me I had counted one of them twice. I objected that the one I meant had a white mark along its side. Nit said two of them did. That was unreasonable, but it did lower the count to thirty-one.

Nit had collected me that morning as if I were another delivery. It took me through the warm shallows, where the weed grew high and the set Kelt stood close enough for their arms to overlap. Those neighbors cleaned growth from one another's shells while they talked. Further down the bank, there was more open stone between homes, and a loose Kelt had to cross the gap even to pass a tool.

I asked why the Kelt had chosen such different distances. “They chose places.” Nit made it sound as though distance had nothing to do with it.

At a patch of yellow weed, Nit slowed and made two careful circles. No shell stood there. The floor was level, the water warm, and three established homes were within an easy swim. One of the set Kelt called Nit over and offered a strip of food. Nit accepted, then came back to the bare stone.

“Here,” it said. I looked for whatever I was meant to see. The pale floor had been brushed clear of loose grit. Six small stones formed a ring. The yellow weed grew just beyond it, bending its narrow tips with the current. I asked what was there.

“My place.” Nit settled inside the ring without touching the bottom. Its limbs, usually incapable of agreement, quieted together. “When I set.”

I had seen enough of the bank to know what that meant, and not enough to believe Nit had used the word correctly. When I asked whether it would become like the shells, Nit gave one strong paddle toward the nearest homes. “A person, yes.” I told Nit it was already a person. Nit answered that it was loose. I said that did not change the first fact. Nit went around the stone ring once, checking it. “Not finished.”

The answer bothered me. I followed Nit out of the ring and asked when this finishing would happen. There was no date, no required size, and no keeper who decided. A loose Kelt felt ready, chose a place, and set. Nit had been returning to the yellow weed for a year. It expected the feeling soon.

“What does ready feel like?” I asked.

“Like not wanting to leave.”

That seemed clearer until I looked at the shells. I pointed out that after setting, Nit could not leave, and asked what happened if the feeling changed.

Nit laughed hard enough to disturb its six stones. It darted down, replaced the one that had rolled, and told me I had the order backward. A Kelt set only after the wish to move had ended. Then the shell grew, the home formed, and arms replaced the little paddling limbs. The place that had been chosen became the place that held everything else.

I asked who would make Nit’s deliveries, carry its messages, and show it things outside the reach of its arms. Nit answered each question as though nothing would be lost. Younger Kelt would carry. Neighbors would bring news. “People tell you.”

It had an answer for every loss because Nit did not count them as losses. Settling meant a voice that stayed in one place, a home with a door, arms strong enough for grown work, and a position among neighbors who could reach you. Motion was childhood. Nit liked motion, but it liked the yellow-weed ring more.

I went through the facts again, because one of them should have changed when I put them in order. None did. Look at the bank from Nit’s side and stillness was not a trap. It was arrival.

Then Nit asked where I would settle. I said I would not. It corrected me: I had not chosen yet. “I do not stay on worlds.”

Nit moved close and examined me the way it had on the first day. “Everyone stays somewhere.”

I could have told it about the black place between worlds, the pale line, and the shapes that let me through. None of that would explain why I left. I did not know why. Saying so would only give Nit another problem to deliver around the bank.

“Not me,” I said. Nit returned to its ring and nudged the last stone into place. “You will know when you are ready.”

There are answers that cannot be argued with because the person giving them has placed your disagreement in the future. Nit had done that to me. I left the yellow weed knowing that every Kelt on Sull would see my motion as unfinished, and I was beginning to suspect that Nit felt sorry for me.

## Chapter 4: The Last Shell

The warm water ended gradually. I found that out by following the grain in the stone after Nit left me for its afternoon work. The gray marks ran away from the crowded center, past wider gaps between shells, and down a slope so slight I would have missed it if I had been walking. The water cooled one distance at a time.

Fewer voices reached me there. The nearest Kelt still called across to one another, but each call had farther to travel. Their homes carried heavier growth. Several doors looked like narrow lines fitted into the upper shell instead of the broad plates I had seen near the yellow weed.

I decided age explained it. Older shells sat farther out. Old material closes around moving parts. That happens in many places, and I was pleased to have one answer without asking Nit.

Then the stone fell away. It did not form a cliff I could measure from above. The pale bottom tilted into dark water and disappeared before I could see where it ended. Cold rose along the slope. Fine grit rolled down it in small threads, each thread vanishing at the same distance. I stayed on the pale side and looked. Being able to move is not the same as needing to test every direction.

One shell stood beyond all the others.

It sat low at the start of the slope, dark bands nearly hidden under crusted growth. No neighbor could have reached it with an arm. The nearest home lay far enough inland that two loose Kelt passed through the gap without going near either one. I might have taken the last shell for empty if I had not heard its voice.

“Eight. Nine. Broken. Ten.” The speaker was quiet and seemed to be addressing the water. I moved around the shell until I found two broad arms working at a strip of tough weed. They were larger than the arms near the shallows, with pale worn patches where they bent. I asked what was broken.

The arms stopped. One remained stretched across the weed. The other folded halfway back toward the shell.

“Nothing of yours.” I pointed out that I owned nothing there. “Then nothing is broken.”

The answer had a useful shape, although it did not answer me. I looked in the direction the shell faced. Pieces of driftweed came over the shallows, passed us, and slid toward the dark slope. One was split near the middle.

I said she had been counting the driftweed. She praised my hearing. When I admitted I had been close, she corrected the praise: perhaps my hearing was ordinary. I liked her before I knew her name.

The shell went back to cutting. She used one arm to hold the weed against the stone and the other to pull a sharp piece of shell through it. The strip resisted at a hard joint. She changed the angle twice, found the soft line beside it, and finished the cut. Nothing in the movement was wasted.

I asked whether ten was many. She said it was ten. When I asked for yesterday's count, the sharp tool paused. “Twelve.”

That was an answer I could use. I turned toward the shell and saw the door clearly for the first time. It lay near the top, closed so tightly that grit had collected along one edge. The opening could not have been much wider than Nit's body. Every other door I had inspected would have admitted a basket.

My age explanation became stronger. This shell looked older than most. A moving plate would wear, catch growth, and close down over years. I had not yet learned that a reasonable answer can be more dangerous than a foolish one because it gives you permission to stop noticing.

“Does your door still open?” I asked.

Both arms came home. The tool settled beside the cut weed. The door did not move, but the whole front of the shell changed. I cannot tell you how. Kelt have no face to tighten and no shoulders to lift. Still, one moment I had been speaking with someone, and the next I was facing a wall that wanted me gone.

I tried to improve the question. “The others are wider. I thought perhaps yours had—”

The rim pressed down with a faint scrape. Whatever voice had reached me came through no longer.

I waited. Light moved over the shell and climbed away. Three pieces of weed passed toward the slope. Then four. Counting them felt rude, so I stopped at seven.

“I asked because I didn't understand,” I said. No arm returned.

The water cooled further as the light thinned. I could hear the distant bank, baskets moving, voices calling names, Nit somewhere laughing too loudly. None of it came this far in a form that required an answer. The shell at the edge had found the one way a set Kelt could leave a conversation.

“I will go now,” I said. After a moment, because I did not want the silence to decide everything for me, I added, “I may come back.”

She did not give me permission. She also had no way to stop me. I understood only the second fact, and that was my first mistake with Rell.

## Chapter 5: Said Before Everyone

Nit found me near Coll the next morning and knew I had gone to the edge before I told it. Cold grit still clung beside me. Nit brushed off one dark grain and said nothing was out there. I told it about the last shell. “Rell.” Nit said the name quickly and moved on from it faster. “You don't bother her.”

I asked whether Rell had told it that. Nit said no. No one had needed to. That was the kind of answer Sull produced often: a rule supported by everybody's obedience and nobody's memory of hearing it.

Before I could ask more, Nit caught my attention with three limbs and pointed toward the crowded middle. Loose Kelt were traveling there from every part of the bank. Some carried food. One dragged a line of six little baskets. Voices crossed over one another until the water seemed busy before anything moved through it.

“Sennet is being welcomed,” Nit said. “Come now or Marr will put you where she wants you.”

Marr put me where she wanted me anyway. She had an ochre shell polished clean by several reachable neighbors and a door broad enough for two loose Kelt together. Her arms caught Nit, shifted it to one side of the gathering, and waved me lower so I would not block anyone's view.

Marr identified me as the creature without a door and told me where to stand. “Nobody will know where to place you otherwise.”

I stayed there. Marr had solved a problem I had not known I caused.

The gathering formed around a large shell with old brown bands. A loose Kelt hovered over it, moving constantly within a very small space. I recognized Sennet's name from Nit’s first delivery. The bundle had gone to Olla, and Olla was the shell below her now.

The bank did not become silent. A place where everyone speaks across fixed distances may not have true silence. The noise lowered enough for Olla's voice to carry.

She named Sennet. She said Sennet belonged in her home and would keep a place there. Then Olla spoke the welcome itself, slowly enough that every listener received the same words.

All around us, Kelt repeated what had happened. The closest set voices carried it outward. Loose Kelt took it across the larger gaps. I heard Olla's welcome leave us, break into dozens of voices, and reach homes too far away to see the door.

Olla opened. Her plate rose much farther than it had for Nit’s bundle, revealing warm dimness below. Sennet stopped moving. For the length of one breath, the young Kelt held itself at the edge. Then it went down through the opening.

The door remained open. Olla's arms lay along either side of it. No one spoke. I had spent enough time on Sull to know that the stillness of so many loose limbs was an action, not an absence.

Sennet came back out.

The bank burst into movement. Baskets opened. Weed changed hands. Young Kelt went through the water in loops that made Nit seem calm. Sennet stayed close to Olla's shell while people came to speak to both of them.

I found Nit carrying food toward Marr and asked why Sennet had left the home. Nit almost dropped a strip before it understood my question.

Nit made me move aside while three others passed. It explained that Sennet did not need to remain behind the door. She had entered, accepted the place, and come out to continue her life. If she settled by the cold weed or crossed to the far shallows, if she quarreled with Olla, if one of them died before the other, the place inside Olla's home would still be Sennet's.

I asked whether Olla could take the place back. The food strip stopped between Nit’s limbs. “No.” Even if Sennet did something terrible, Nit said, the place remained hers. The public words made sure the whole bank knew.

I looked toward the homes spreading in every direction. Olla could not go to any of them. Most could not come to her. What they knew together had to travel by voices or loose bodies, and a fact important enough to last needed every possible route at once.

Marr reached toward Nit for the food, missed, and accused Nit of moving the food on purpose. Nit said Marr's arm had become slow. The welcome had changed something permanent, and ordinary life had returned quickly enough to step on its ending. I preferred that to a speech.

One question remained. I waited until Nit had surrendered the strip, then asked how Olla decided who had a place. Nit said a person entered the home permanently only through welcome. I asked what happened if someone went inside without one. Nit was already chasing a basket before it answered. “They come out again.”

It was a simple answer. On that morning, with Sennet moving freely above her mother's open door, I had no reason to hear a warning inside it.

## Chapter 6: Shelter

I spent the following morning measuring doors without telling anyone I was doing it.

The broadest belonged to Kelt in the warm center. Their neighbors could reach the rims, and visitors crossed above them all day. I saw doors opened for bundles, food, greetings, arguments, and one loose Kelt who went through Marr's home because going over it would have required a turn. Toward the edge, openings narrowed while the gaps between shells grew.

My age answer still worked. The outer homes looked older. Yet the pattern of visits was as exact as the pattern of age, and I wrote both down in the only place I keep records.

Marr caught me circling her before I finished the second measure. She told me to ask how wide her door was. When I did, she said, “Wider than Coll's.”

That was not a measure. It was, however, the answer Marr wanted recorded. She rested one arm across the door and used the other to sort pale grit from dark. Before I could leave, she asked whether Nit had explained welcomes properly. Marr then explained them herself without waiting to learn what Nit had said.

According to Marr, nobody entered a home permanently by mistake. The owner chose, spoke before witnesses, and opened. The welcomed Kelt accepted by going inside. Every part protected every other part. A private welcome would be no welcome because the bank could not remember what it had not heard.

I asked what happened if no welcome occurred. “Then no place is made.” Marr dropped one dark grain into the pale pile, noticed, and removed it. “Simple.”

I turned toward the edge. From there Rell's shell was only a dark rise against darker water.

The light changed before I decided whether to go.

At first it became thin, as if the bright surface had moved farther away. Then a low pressure traveled through the water and pulled every strand of weed in the same direction. Marr's arms stopped sorting. Across the bank, other arms withdrew. Voices shifted from argument to instruction.

“Small bad water,” Marr said. “Stay low if you mean to stay out.”

Grit lifted from the floor in curling lines. The loose Kelt did not panic. They picked a nearby shell and went through its door with the speed of a practiced act. Marr opened before the first reached her. Three slipped past one arm and disappeared into the warm space below. Two entered Coll. Nit hesitated between Olla and a gray shell, chose the gray one, and squeezed through as its plate came down.

No owner declared a welcome. No witnesses repeated names. The loose ones went inside because the water had become difficult and an open door was near.

I stayed outside. The moving grit could not harm me as it harmed the Kelt, and I wanted to see what the rule looked like during its exception.

Brown water covered the bank. I could no longer see Coll, though I heard him complain that someone had brought loose weed into his home. Marr answered from inside herself that bad water was not improved by good manners. The pull lasted long enough for grit to collect on my far side. Then it weakened. The floor returned by degrees.

Doors opened across the bank. Loose Kelt came out coughing silt and talking at once. Nit emerged from the gray shell already carrying a complaint from its owner to someone three homes away. Marr released her three, passed the misplaced dark grain to one as if that had been its reason for entering, and continued sorting.

I pointed out that the sheltered Kelt had not received places. Marr agreed that shelter was different. When I asked what made it different, her arm swept toward the young Kelt spreading back across the water. “They leave.”

There were three stages in that answer: they entered, the bad water ended, and they left. Nobody had counted them aloud. Nobody checked Marr, Coll, or the gray shell after. The bank treated the final stage as surely as it treated the water clearing.

I went to the edge near evening. Rell was cutting weed beside the same tool. Fresh grit lay across the upper side of her shell, more than I had seen on any home in the center. She worked without acknowledging me. I stayed farther from the rim than before and observed that the bad water had left more silt there. “It always does.” When I asked why, she said the floor sloped and she was last.

The explanation fit the threads I had watched descend toward the drop. Rell had to clear everything the water carried past the bank. I asked whether anyone helped after stronger water. One arm folded over the tool.

“Are you asking about my door again?”

I said I was not. “Then do not reach it by walking around the other side of the question.”

I had been doing exactly that without knowing it. I moved farther from the rim.

I told her I had returned because I said I might. “That is not the same as being asked.” I agreed.

She cut another strip. I waited for her to close, but she did not. The light left the far water first and worked its way toward us. Four pieces of driftweed passed. Rell counted under her voice, perhaps forgetting I could hear.

After the fourth, she said, “You may stay until the light goes. Do not ask about the door.”

That was not friendship. It was a limit, clearly given, and the first thing Rell had offered me that I could either respect or break.

# Part II: What Rell Keeps

## Chapter 7: The Count Between Us

I returned the next day before the light began to lower. Rell did not remind me that she had invited me only until dark on the previous day. She also did not speak.

I stayed beyond her door, where she could see every movement I made. Seven strips of driftweed came past. She counted none of them aloud. When the light reached the top of her shell, I left.

That became our arrangement for several days, although only one of us knew it was an arrangement. I arrived. Rell worked. I kept away from the rim and did not mention the door. Sometimes she closed before I got there and gave me nothing but shell. Sometimes one arm remained outside. I learned that an outside arm did not mean she wished to talk. I learned it by trying.

Marr gave me Rell's name on the fourth day, after pretending surprise that I still went to the edge. While splitting a bundle between baskets, she said Rell disliked company. I asked whether Rell had said so. Marr moved one strip from the full basket to the empty one. “She has arranged herself where company cannot reach her.”

That was an answer to a different question. On Sull, people often used distance as speech because set Kelt could not create distance after they chose it. Rell's position had become an answer she gave forever, whether she still meant it each day or not.

I carried the name back and did not use it. If Rell had wanted me to know, she could tell me herself.

On the sixth day, I noticed that she had changed her cutting angle. The edge of her shell tool was worn down, so she pulled it through the weed on a slant. That worked near the tip and tore near the root. I watched three cuts fail in the same place.

I mentioned Coll's sharper tool-shell. Rell said he could enjoy it. When I told her he gave tools away, she answered, “He gives everything away and complains afterward.”

She knew Coll well enough to copy Nit’s description. I almost asked when she had last spoken to him. The question reached me in time, and I let it go.

On the eighth day, she observed that I was still there and asked whether I had nowhere else.

I considered that. There were hundreds of shells, thirty-one loose Kelt unless the two white-marked ones had fooled me again, and a whole upward distance I had not explored. I had many other places. None contained a person who could not leave if I became tiresome.

“I have everywhere else,” I said. Her tool stopped. “That is not an answer.” I admitted it was only the true part.

One arm made a small loose movement against the floor. I had seen set Kelt laugh with both arms and enough noise to disturb baskets. Rell's movement was smaller. If I had not spent eight days watching her work, I might have missed it.

After that, she sometimes spoke first. She told me when I blocked the light across the weed. She corrected my count when two pieces passed together. Once she asked what Nit was shouting about, and I carried the basket dispute to the edge in a shorter form. Rell said Marr had been wrong from the beginning. I told her Nit agreed.

Rell said Nit agreed with whoever had spoken last. I pointed out that I had spoken last. “Whoever spoke last who has food,” she corrected. That was fairer.

On the eleventh day, four pieces of weed passed close together. I counted them under my voice. Rell's arms continued trimming a tough growth along her lower rim.

“Six,” she said. I said four. She corrected me: six for the day.

I had missed the first two before arriving. Rell had not. The count belonged to the whole day, not merely the part I witnessed.

“Six,” I agreed. She put the tool down. “Rell.”

I knew what she meant, but I let her finish it.

“My name is Rell.”

I told her I knew. Her arms lifted at once, and she demanded to know why I had let her tell me. “Because Marr gave it to me. You hadn't.”

Rell stayed still long enough for the seventh piece of weed to pass without either of us saying the number. Then she made the small movement again. “Seven,” she said.

I had not earned much. A name, a corrected count, and the knowledge that Rell laughed so quietly another person had to be waiting for it. It was enough to bring me back the next day.

## Chapter 8: The Part She Could Not Reach

Rell's shell looked different once I knew where work stopped.

The front and both sides were dark but clean. She cut the weed there every few days and scraped grit from the rim after rough water. Across the upper back, beyond the farthest bend of either arm, a hard green crust had grown thick enough to form shelves. New weed took hold on those shelves. Small pale creatures hid under them when my movement changed the current.

In the middle of the bank, no set Kelt carried growth like that. Neighbors cleaned the places one another could not reach. I had watched Coll work along Marr's back while arguing about the basket, and Marr had corrected his angle without pausing her side of the argument. The task did not count as a favor. It was what arms within reach did.

Rell had no arm within reach except her own.

She had made a tool for the problem. A curved piece of shell was tied to a long strip of tough weed. She hooked the strip over her back, pulled it forward with one arm, caught the other end with the other arm, and dragged the shell edge across the growth. The tool slipped more often than it cut. Each pull bent both arms farther behind her than they wanted to go.

I watched her do it twice. On the third pull, I moved closer and said I could reach the growth. The tool stopped above the shell. Rell said I could also remain where I was. When I agreed that both were true, she told me to choose the second.

I did. Respecting a boundary does not feel like doing something. It feels like the part before doing something, extended until the other person decides otherwise. Rell made seven more pulls and freed one piece no larger than a basket tie.

For four days I watched the same work. I did not offer again. That became difficult on the fourth day when a cracked shelf lifted from the shell but remained attached along its far edge. The water caught it with every change of current and pulled. Rell could feel the pull. Her arms searched behind her, found nothing, and tried again.

The shelf tore halfway. A line of fresh pale shell showed beneath it. Rell laid both arms flat, and I could hear the hard edge tapping against her back. I asked her to tell me to remove it. She reminded me that I had already asked. I reminded her that the earlier answer belonged to another day and another piece. Rell said it would come off by itself.

The current pulled and the broken shelf struck her again. I said it was coming off badly.

She was silent for a long while. I did not move nearer. At last one arm lifted and pointed to the tool on the stone.

“Use the flat end. Not the sharp one.” That was permission for the tool, the shelf, and nothing else. I moved the shell piece behind her and wedged its flat side beneath the hanging crust. My first angle pulled against the fresh line. Rell stopped me and directed me lower, with the edge turned away from her. If I could not see the join, I was to feel where the water caught.

I followed each direction. The current pressed harder against the loose shelf than against the solid growth. I moved along that difference until the tool found the last attachment. One push broke it cleanly. The shelf turned once in the water and dropped toward the slope.

Rell told me to catch the broken shelf. I reached it before it vanished over the dark. When I brought it back, small pale creatures abandoned the underside and scattered. Rell examined the edge and said Coll could sharpen it into a scraper. I reminded her that Coll could enjoy his own tools. “He can enjoy this one too.”

I set it beside her existing tool. The clean patch on her back was smaller than I had expected. Around it, years of growth remained. I asked whether she wanted me to continue. She said no, and I went back to my usual place.

Rell tested the exposed shell by moving one arm as far over herself as it would go. She could touch the nearest edge now. “Better,” she said. Then her arm dropped. “This does not put you in my debt.”

I said I thought I had helped her. She agreed. That was why she needed to say it.

I understood the shape of that. Debt would give me a reason to return and ask for something. Gratitude could become the same reason if I decided it did. Rell was closing both paths before I used them.

“Then we owe nothing,” I said. Rell answered that we had not owed anything before. The correction was exact. I kept it.

## Chapter 9: Exact Accounts

The scraper blank gave me my first reason to carry something for Rell.

She asked me to take it to Coll and have one edge sharpened, with the inner curve left dull. She described the edge twice and made me repeat it. Then she marked the side Coll must not cut with three scratches from her old tool.

Rell told me to bring back every piece if Coll ruined it. When I asked what use ruined pieces had, she said, “I will know exactly what he ruined.”

Coll was pleased to receive new material and insulted by the instructions. He sharpened the correct edge anyway. While he worked, he asked where I had found the piece. I said it had come from old shell growth near the edge.

Coll asked whether the growth had come from Rell. I did not answer.

Coll's tool paused once, then continued. He did not ask again. When he finished, he wrapped the cutting edge in soft weed and returned the three-scratched side untouched.

Rell tested the scraper against the growth she could reach. The edge bit cleanly. She asked what Coll had charged. I said nothing. “Coll charges in complaining. How much?”

I counted six complaints, though two concerned Marr and one concerned the quality of weed this season. Rell accepted three as the price of the work and said the rest belonged to Coll's ordinary day.

After that she let me carry other things, but every trip began and ended with an account. I brought tough weed from the shallows and received two narrow strips from the edge to take back. I carried a small basket of pale grit from Marr and returned the empty basket before dark. Rell never asked Nit. Asking Nit meant Nit would announce the destination while crossing half the bank, arrive with three extra objects, and tell everyone what Rell had looked like when receiving them.

I was safer because I was already going to her.

That should have made me examine why I went. Instead I treated usefulness as an answer.

One afternoon I brought a bundle of the soft weed that grew only near Olla. Rell had offered a piece of hard edge-weed in exchange, but Olla refused it.

I reported that Olla had called the soft weed a gift. Rell pushed it away and told me to return it. When I warned that Olla would send it again, Rell said I would become excellent at carrying one bundle.

I did carry it back. Olla added another strip and told me Rell had once brought food to her after an illness, before Sennet was born. That fact belonged to Olla, but the pressure to use it belonged to me. I could have carried the old kindness to the edge like a key: You helped her once, so you must accept this now.

I left the second strip with Olla and returned empty.

Rell asked whether the bundle had been accepted. I said no. She asked what Olla had told me. I said something about an old exchange that was not mine to spend.

The arms became quiet. “That is an inconvenient answer.” I agreed that carrying the weed twice had been inconvenient. Rell advised me to settle so nobody could send me anywhere. When I said Nit gave the same advice, she said Nit wanted everyone else to make Nit less strange.

We counted eleven pieces of driftweed that day. Rell said the number after each piece. I answered only when two passed close together and our totals might split. At the end she asked what the first day's count had been.

I gave her the first count: ten, including the broken piece. Rell noticed that I remembered. I said she had spoken it aloud. “I say many things aloud.”

That was untrue. Rell said very little when anyone could hear, and until recently nobody had been near enough to hear what escaped. I did not correct her.

The next day she counted without waiting to see whether I had arrived. I heard “three” while I was still crossing the last open stretch. When “four” came past, I gave the number from where I was.

Rell did not stop. That was more trust than the errands. She could price grit, tools, and weed. A count shared between two people had no way to be paid back, which meant she had permitted something between us that could not be kept exact.

## Chapter 10: After the Light

I stayed later than I said I would.

There was no single reason. Rell's new scraper had loosened a strip along her upper back, and she wanted the piece caught if the current took it. The driftweed count had reached thirteen, which was the highest I had heard. Nit had promised to bring a narrow basket before dark and had not appeared. Each reason was true. Together they let me avoid saying that I did not want to leave.

The light withdrew across the bank. Voices from the warm center became shapes of sound rather than words. Rell finished cutting and pulled both arms close.

Rell said Nit was not coming. I suggested Nit might only be late. “Nit is late while traveling. Nit is never late arriving with news.”

She told me to go before the cold strengthened. I moved away from her shell, but instead of crossing toward the bank I followed a slow piece of weed out above the slope. The piece turned in the downward current and disappeared. I remained where I was, about forty body-lengths beyond Rell.

From there the bank was behind me and the dark was below. The water changed when full light left it. During the day, the drop looked like an unfinished part of the world. At night, it felt occupied by distance. Cold came upward in steady bands. I could hear the small scrape of Rell's tool being set down.

I should have announced that I had not gone. I knew it then. Saying nothing allowed Rell to believe she was alone, and I accepted that false belief because it gave me one more moment near her without having to ask.

Her door moved.

The sound came first: a rough pull, then a halt. I turned. A line of dim warmth had appeared across the upper shell. The plate lifted another small distance. Rell made a low sound through the opening. I had never heard the sound before and understood at once that opening hurt her.

The gap widened farther than I thought the fitted rim allowed. Warm light reached one of her arms. It rested beside the door, not working, as though all her strength had gone into holding the plate.

Something pale rose from inside.

At first I saw only several small limbs touching the lower edge. More followed. A soft body came close to the opening but remained behind it. The shape was the size of Nit, perhaps smaller. It had no shell around it. Rell's arm moved until its outer edge rested beside the pale limbs.

Neither one spoke.

The pale shape stayed at the gap while cold water moved over the shell. It did not push against the opening or try to cross. After a while, it withdrew. The last small limb slipped below the warm line. Rell held the door for a count of six longer, then lowered it in slow stages. Each stage made the rough sound again.

When the rim sealed, the light vanished.

I remained over the drop. If I moved toward her, she would know I had watched. If I left, I would carry the fact away without her consent. Those seemed like different choices, but both began with the same act already completed.

Someone was inside Rell's home.

I tested what I knew. The shape was loose, like Nit. It had stayed behind the door. Rell had touched the rim beside it. None of those facts told me a name, a relationship, or a reason. They did tell me that every account I had heard on Sull was incomplete.

Olla had said her welcome where the whole bank could repeat it. Sennet had entered and left. Marr had insisted permanent places required public words. Nit had said a person who sheltered came out when the water cleared.

No public words belonged to Rell. Even Marr, who knew everything available to be carried, spoke of Rell as alone.

I left without calling to her. The decision did not protect Rell. It protected me from the moment when she would learn what I had done.

On the way inward, I passed Nit coming fast with the promised basket.

“You are going the wrong way,” Nit said.

“So are you.”

Nit waved the basket and continued toward the edge. I almost stopped it. A delivery would make Rell open an arm, perhaps speak, perhaps reveal that she knew someone had been outside. I let Nit go because stopping it would require an explanation.

That was another choice made for myself. By the time I reached the warm water, I had collected enough facts to ask a hundred questions and no right to ask the first one.

## Chapter 11: The Only Way to Leave

Rell knew when I returned.

The promised basket sat beside her, still tied shut. Nit had made the delivery and gone. Rell's arms rested on the stone. She did not count the two pieces of driftweed that passed while I crossed the open stretch.

“You stayed,” she said. I could have asked what she meant. There were several places I had stayed the night before, and asking might have bought me another sentence in which nothing was decided. I said yes. Rell made me confirm that I had stayed after she told me to go.

The rim of her door did not move. I watched it anyway.

“How much?” she asked. I knew she did not mean time. “I saw you open. I saw someone come to the gap. I did not see inside beyond that.”

One arm pulled in. The other remained flat for a moment and then followed. I began to say I had told no one. Rell said, “Go.” When I tried to explain, she cut across me. “Go away from my home.”

The plate pressed against its rim. Rell had shut me out before, but this time I understood the difference. On my first visit she had protected a question. Now she was protecting herself from the person who had ignored an answer.

I went. Near Marr's shell, Nit caught me and asked whether Rell liked the basket. I said the tie was still closed. Nit wanted to know whether she was ill. I said I did not know.

That was true, but it left out the fact Nit needed. Rell had withdrawn because of me. I did not tell Nit because Nit would ask what I had done, and the answer would place the hidden shape into open water. Even an honest account of my mistake would repeat it.

I went back two days later. Rell's arms were gone. The basket remained where Nit had left it, though the current had rolled it against the shell. Growth lifted from her back. Nothing else moved.

“I came to say I am sorry,” I told the closed plate. “You told me to leave. I stayed where you could not see me, and I watched you when you believed you were alone. That was mine to prevent. I did not.”

No answer came. I wanted to add that I had meant no harm, that I had not known she would open, that I had been interested and had lost the moment when interest became intrusion. All of it was true. None belonged in the apology. Those facts explained me. They did not repair what I had done to her.

I remained long enough to count five pieces of weed, then left without saying I would return.

Four days after the confrontation, I carried the still-sealed basket back to Nit. The tie was softening where it had rubbed the stone. Nit touched it with three limbs.

Nit asked whether Rell had returned the basket. I admitted I had removed it because it was being damaged, without her instruction. Nit pulled it out of my reach. “Then you should have left it.”

Nit was right. I had removed an object from beside Rell's shell because I could see damage and could not bear doing nothing. That was the same reason I had stayed to watch. A useful reason can still be a way of taking another person's choice.

On the sixth day I went again and stopped farther out than usual. The door stayed flat. No arms emerged. I said nothing.

I finally understood what I should have understood the first time Rell closed. A set Kelt could not turn away, cross the room, or choose a different path home. Rell had one way to end my access: pull every living part inside and become a shell.

My presence forced her to keep doing it. I left at once. For the first time since meeting her, I did not decide when I would come back.

## Chapter 12: No Quiet Question

Leaving Rell alone did not make the fact leave me alone.

I carried it through the center of the bank and tried to reduce it to what I had seen. A loose Kelt lived behind a door that had not been named in any welcome. The Kelt did not come out after the door opened. Rell wanted no one to see. Every word beyond those would be invention.

I began with Nit because Nit answered before considering why a question had been asked.

We were moving a basket of grit from Marr to a set Kelt near the shallows. Nit pulled from the front while I kept the basket from turning. I asked how people knew every member of a home.

Nit listed Olla's people, then Coll's, then Marr's. Marr had welcomed two from her brood, a cousin, and someone from so long ago that Nit knew the place but not the name. The gap bothered Nit for half the route. It considered going back to ask. I stopped it, saying I did not need the name, though Nit objected that Marr enjoyed questions.

Nit said that not needing a piece of news was a poor reason not to collect it. I changed the question. Suppose someone entered during bad water and remained after the water cleared. Would that person belong to the home?

Nit pulled the basket crooked. “They would leave.” When I insisted they had not, Nit said the owner must have stopped them. I asked for the case where the owner did not. Nit stopped pulling. The basket tipped toward the stone until I moved beneath its near edge. “Those are all the choices.”

We finished the delivery without speaking about it again. Nit had not answered because the world it knew provided no third condition.

Marr was harder. She answered the pressure beneath questions, and mine carried too much. I waited until she was separating soft weed from tough, then asked whether anyone kept a written account of welcomes.

It took me a while to explain writing. Marr disliked the idea. Marks could be wrong, she said, and nobody could question a mark until it admitted the truth. People could be questioned. Witnesses could correct one another. A whole bank remembering was stronger than one object.

I pointed out that a whole bank could not remember what it had never seen. Marr's sorting slowed. “Never saw what?”

I should have stopped. Instead I tried the question sideways, the way Rell had told me not to do. I asked how the bank would understand a loose Kelt who remained behind a door without a welcome.

Both of Marr's arms settled on the pile. She wanted to know who had told me, then who I had seen. I answered only the first: no one had told me.

I had not named Rell. I had not said the edge, a narrow door, or twenty years. Marr had supplied a person anyway, or at least knew there were few possible people. The question itself had carried more than its words.

I claimed I was asking about the rule. Marr gave it to me: people left after shelter; if they could not, the owner called for help; if the owner remained silent, the owner wanted them hidden. She pushed the two weed piles apart as though distance might make the choices clearer. “There is no innocent secret behind a door.” When I objected that there could be, Marr said secrecy would end the innocence.

That answer was wrong about Rell. It was also the answer everyone else would reach, because every safe action on Sull happened in public. A welcome had witnesses. Bad water took many Kelt at once. A keeper judged in hearing of the bank. Privacy around a home did not read as safety. It read as something done without witnesses for a reason.

Marr leaned one arm closer and warned me not to ask where others could hear. The reason was simple. “They will wonder whose door taught you the question.”

I left the sorting place without taking a basket. On the way out, I tested every safer version I could make. I could ask about all homes, but then the bank would examine all homes. I could ask Vesh only about accidents, but he would ask what accident I meant. I could tell him no name, but the direction from which I arrived each day already gave him one.

There were no records to inspect without waking suspicion. There was no private path to a keeper fixed in the busiest part of the bank. To prove that Rell had never spoken a welcome, somebody would have to ask everyone whether they had heard one.

Rell had not merely hidden a person. For years, she had protected herself from the question that hiding would make other people ask. I reached the start of the open stretch and stopped. Her shell stood at the far end with no arms visible. I wanted to go to her and say I finally understood.

Going there would prove that I did not.

# Part III: The Harm Done Carefully

## Chapter 13: Every Route Carries It

I spent the next day finding ways to reach Vesh without reaching him.

Vesh sat near the middle because a keeper needed to be heard. Twelve homes lay close enough to receive his ordinary voice. Loose Kelt crossed above him on nearly every route between the shallows and the lower bank. His door opened for messages, disputes, and visitors so often that I could not finish a count. There was no moment when he was alone.

I tried routes instead. If I approached from the shallows, three Kelt would see me cross. If I came from below, Marr would notice. I could arrive at night, but Vesh would be closed, and asking him to open for a secret visitor would make the secrecy visible. Nit offered to carry a message. I said I only wanted to ask Vesh something. Nit called that a message with a question inside, so I moved away before it improved the definition.

The safest plan seemed to be a broad question. I would ask Vesh whether any door on Sull might hold someone who had never been welcomed. He would not know I meant Rell. Then I imagined his first reply: Which door?

I could refuse the name. That would narrow his choices. Most homes opened daily and could not hide a loose Kelt. The few narrow doors belonged to isolated Kelt. Of those, only one sat where I had been going every afternoon.

I tried making the question larger. Vesh could ask every set Kelt to open for inspection after bad water. Rell's home would then be one among hundreds. But the inspection itself would announce that someone might be trapped or hidden. Every owner would ask why. Every loose Kelt would carry a different answer. By the time Vesh reached the edge, Rell would already know the bank was coming for her door.

I tried removing the home. Could a loose Kelt remain loose for years? Vesh might answer that. Then he would ask where I had seen one. I tried removing the person. Could a door become too narrow to use? That question would point even more directly toward the one opening on the bank everybody avoided looking at. Each path reached Rell.

The problem was not that I lacked clever wording. Vesh's work required him to turn private trouble into public fact. He gathered witnesses, said judgments where everyone could hear, and gave the bank one account to carry. Asking him to work secretly would be asking him to stop being a keeper at the exact moment I needed him to be one.

Marr had said no innocent secret could remain innocent. I disagreed with her, but I could see the machine around the belief. Oral memory protected welcomes because no single person could erase them. The same public system made an unspoken exception impossible to prove without exposing it.

Near midday, Vesh noticed me and called out to ask whether I had lost something. Several nearby arms paused. A loose Kelt slowed above us.

I said no. Vesh asked whether I needed help. I said no again. Each answer made him watch me more carefully, and each denial became public because that was where he lived.

Marr called from farther down and told me to stop circling because everyone would think Vesh had something interesting. Six voices asked what was interesting, so I left.

At the outer edge of the crowded homes, Nit caught up and offered me a small piece of food. I could not eat it, but Nit often forgot that. This time I accepted and carried it to an old shell beyond Coll who could reach no neighbor. The old Kelt thanked Nit, though Nit was already gone.

That route had carried something useful. The food changed hands because I did not need to explain it, and because everyone who saw me could understand what it was.

Rell's fact could not travel that way. The moment I carried it, it would change shape inside the first person who received it.

I returned to the start of the open stretch, no farther. Rell remained closed. I stayed only long enough to see that the scraper and both arms were absent. Then I went back inland.

Doing nothing was now a choice I understood. I did not yet understand how long I could keep making it.

## Chapter 14: The Water Pulls Outward

The storm answered before I did. Light vanished from the shallows in the middle of the day. The surface did not dim in the slow way I knew. It went dark in one sweep. A pressure crossed the bank hard enough to bend tall weed flat, and every basket not tied down began to slide toward the drop.

The Kelt called it bad water, but that name covered too much. The earlier water had lifted grit and made shelter sensible. This water took hold of the floor.

Set arms caught tools and baskets. Loose Kelt stopped carrying and chose doors. Coll opened wide enough for four. Marr took three, then reached across her own plate to pull a smaller one through. The bank disappeared one moving body at a time.

Nit collided with me near the gray shell it had used before and told me to shelter in Coll. I said Rell was last on the slope. Nit turned toward the edge, where brown grit already hid the outer homes, and said she knew what storms did. I asked whether her shell could hold all of it.

Nit did not answer. A strand of weed snapped loose and struck the stone between us. The current rolled it outward fast enough that we lost sight of it at once.

I asked Nit to come with me. Nit tried. It moved less than one body-length before the outward pull caught its broad side and sent it tumbling. I crossed under it, changed the water enough to slow the turn, and pushed it back toward the gray door.

“I cannot,” Nit said. The words came without its usual extra movement. “I am too small.”

That was not cowardice. The storm had measured Nit and given an answer. I waited until the gray shell opened and an arm caught Nit. Then I turned toward the edge.

I could move through the pull, but not quickly. The bank gave me no clear line. Grit struck from inland, and I had to stay close enough to the floor to find each shell before passing it. I used their high sides as breaks. Between homes the water took me outward faster than I intended; behind them I worked back toward my route.

After the last outer shell, there was no break. Rell lay beyond a brown distance that kept changing. I found her first by sound: scraper against stone, arms dragging through packed grit, the strained scrape of her door rim beneath both.

Silt had built along her inward side until it reached halfway up the shell. More came every moment. Rell used one arm to cut the bank and the other to sweep loosened material around her toward the drop. The water brought back nearly as much as she moved.

She saw me. Neither arm stopped. I went to the side she could not reach.

There was no useful speech in the storm. I pressed into the packed layer, loosened what I could, and carried it around her outer side. The first load vanished down the slope. The second spun back and struck the shell below the rim. I changed the angle, going farther outward before releasing it.

The changed angle worked, so I did it again. The work became six movements: enter the silt, loosen, gather, pull free, cross the current, release past the returning line. I counted the sequence because numbers kept the direction clear when sight failed. Rell worked her reachable half. I worked mine.

The level rose anyway. Once it reached the lower edge of the door. Rell scraped across the rim while I cleared beneath her arm. A second surge buried the place we had opened. We started again. I lost count after the eighty-third load and did not spend any attention finding it.

During the darkest part, the water shifted and drove a hard roll of grit straight over the shell. Rell pulled one arm across the door. The other remained exposed below a packed ridge. I went under the ridge, broke it upward, and the current took it. Her arm came free.

She set it beside mine against the next load.

We did not speak. The contact lasted only while both of us pushed, but after weeks of keeping a fixed distance from her rim, I knew exactly what she had allowed.

Near the next light, the pull weakened. Silt still moved, but it fell more than it traveled. We cleared the door, then the upper side, then a path around both arms. I continued until my next movement found bare stone instead of packed grit.

Rell stopped too. The storm had left a wall downstream and a hollow on the side we cleared. Her old tool was gone. The sharpened scraper remained trapped under one arm. The door was visible. I could not tell whether the plate had shifted under the weight. I asked whether it could open. Rell moved the arm away from the rim. “I don't know.”

We looked at the closed door. Someone waited beneath it, and I was the only person outside who knew.

I expected Rell to order me away again. Instead she said, “Stay until the water settles.”

I stayed where she could see me.

## Chapter 15: Twenty Years of Silence

The water settled by midday. I stayed.

Rell did not tell me to leave, which was not permission for everything else. I kept to the cleared side and waited while she tested the door. One arm fitted under the plate. She pulled. The rim rose no more than a finger-width before dropping with a scrape.

I asked whether she would try again. She said no.

She checked the rim for grit, found a packed line below the inner edge, and directed me to loosen it with the dull side of the scraper. I worked only where she pointed. The second test raised the plate slightly farther. Warmth reached the gap, then disappeared when she closed.

Whoever waited below had learned the storm was over from that line of water.

Rell turned her attention to the wall of silt we had built. She informed me that my release place during the storm had been wrong. I had carried loads outward, but not far enough around the shell. When the current changed again, part of the wall would roll back toward her door.

I reminded Rell that she had watched me use the wrong release place at least eighty-three times. She corrected the count upward. When I complained that she had many chances to correct my angle, she said she had been keeping her home above the floor.

That was a fair priority. Rell showed me the proper release line by drawing a curve through the grit. We moved the worst part of the wall together. The work was slow without the storm pushing against us. It was also harder to pretend the contact meant nothing when danger no longer required it.

Seven strips of driftweed passed while we worked. Rell counted the first. I gave the second. We alternated without deciding to.

After the seventh, both arms came down. “There is someone in my home,” she said.

I did not answer. The words had taken twenty years and did not need me to hurry them.

Rell told me only what she chose. The person had gone inside during bad water. Rell had not welcomed them, invited them to remain, or spoken any promise before the bank. When the storm ended, they did not come back out. They had been there ever since.

I asked how long. “Twenty years.”

I knew Kelt remained loose until settling. I looked at the narrow rim and thought of the pale limbs that had touched it from below. Twenty years was longer than some of the set shells near the warm center had been fixed.

Rell told me not to ask who the person was or why they had stayed. I agreed. Then she added the harder condition: I was not to offer help before I knew what help would do.

That was the hardest condition because the word was already forming. I let it go.

Rell lifted the sharpened scraper and began clearing the curve she had drawn. Her work had become unsteady. I took the other end only when she pushed it toward me.

After a while I asked why she had not gone to Vesh. The scraper stopped between us. Rell could have said the question crossed another line. Instead she answered.

Vesh might believe her. She did not know, but his decency was not the problem. A keeper verified what he declared. He would ask who had heard a welcome, who remembered the storm, who had seen the person afterward. Every question would give the bank the shape of the accusation before Vesh could reject it.

I argued that Vesh could declare her innocent and order others not to repeat the suspicion. Rell pointed out that he would first have to say what she might have done. One arm pressed hard against the scraper. “You have met Marr.”

I almost laughed. Rell did not. Marr would repeat the order, the denial, and the thing denied in one breath, convinced she was helping.

Rell said Vesh had only his voice and the place where he sat. Both were public. There was no second method hidden behind the keeper's work. Once the question entered the bank, no later answer could make people unhear it.

I asked what the bank would think, though Marr had already told me. Rell answered plainly: they would think she had kept someone.

Rell's door lay between us, a narrow plate over warm dark. I understood why she had shut for six days after I watched. She had not only feared that I would tell. She had feared I would decide my reason for telling was good enough.

“I will not go to Vesh,” I said. Rell lowered the scraper and warned me against promising away a thing I already wanted. “I wanted to. I am promising what I will do now.”

She did not accept or reject that. We moved the last of the dangerous silt beyond the curve, and when the next piece of weed came past, Rell said, “Eight.”

I answered, “Eight,” and did not ask for anything more.

## Chapter 16: The Clever Way

I kept my promise for three days. That sentence is accurate and useless. A promise does not become less broken because it survived several mornings. I include the number because I counted it, not because it helps me.

On the first day, I stayed with Rell and moved the last storm silt beyond her curve. On the second, we found her lost tool down the slope, caught in a fold of weed before the dark. On the third, the door opened enough for a narrow line of warm water. The person below did not come to the gap.

That should have been enough movement for me. It was not.

I had seen a rule fail on another world. Everyone there had treated the rule as part of the place itself, but it had been made from agreement and repeated habit. When one person stopped agreeing, the world did not break. Only the habit did.

Rell's problem looked similar. The bank believed an unwelcomed person could not remain innocently behind a door. That belief had been carried for so long that nobody had tested whether it needed to control the next question. I decided a keeper could change the method before hearing the fact.

This was the clever way: ask Vesh to agree to privacy first. Do not name Rell. Do not mention the edge, the storm, or the person. Establish a new way to ask, then place the question inside it.

I did not tell Rell about the plan. I called that protection. You can hear the trouble before I did.

Vesh was clearing storm growth from the top of his shell. Four loose Kelt worked above him, passing broken strands outward. Marr directed two of them even though nobody had asked her. I waited until the last bundle left and moved close to Vesh's rim.

I quietly asked Vesh to answer a question without asking who it concerned. His nearest arm stopped. He said that was usually how he learned who. I asked him to promise first. “I do not promise before I know what I am promising.”

Two neighboring arms had already angled toward us. My quiet voice had done the opposite of what I intended. It made people listen harder.

I should have left. Instead I explained that a person on the bank needed help with a door and would be harmed if the question became public. Vesh asked whether the person faced danger now. I thought of the storm weight on Rell's rim and the pale limbs below it.

I admitted the person faced danger. Vesh said he needed the home. I said I needed a way to tell him without giving it.

Vesh brought both arms down. His door closed behind them, not to shut me out but to stop the work inside from interrupting. “You have told me there is danger behind a door. You have told me the owner is being protected from the bank. You arrive here every day from one direction. How many choices do you believe I have?”

The neighboring arms became still.

I tried to take the words back by making them general. I said someone had entered a home during bad water, had not been welcomed, and had remained. The owner had done nothing wrong. Vesh must begin there.

His voice lowered when he asked how long. That was the question Rell had answered because she chose to. It was not mine. I did not say twenty years, but it no longer mattered.

Vesh looked toward the outer bank. He was old enough to remember which homes had stopped receiving visitors and which doors had not opened after storms. He did not need my number.

“Rell,” he said. I denied that I had named her. Vesh agreed. That was worse than anger. He laid out what he had heard: a hidden danger, a home needing protection from public suspicion, and my path from the edge. Any keeper who could not find Rell from those facts was no keeper.

I told him to forget I had come. Vesh asked whether I could. I could not.

Vesh moved one arm toward the homes around him. “My work happens here because people hear me. I have no private room. I have no second voice. You knew that before you came.”

I had known. I had believed the right arrangement of words could remove the cost from a fact.

I asked him to do nothing. Vesh looked toward the edge again. “You have already changed what nothing means.”

The four loose Kelt with the storm growth had returned. Marr was asking why Vesh had stopped working. I left before either of them could ask me, as though leaving now could protect the secret I had carried into the center.

## Chapter 17: What the Bank Heard

Vesh waited until the next light. I know because the bank remained ordinary through the night, and because I stayed near enough to hear it being ordinary.

I did not go to Rell. I did not have a warning that could help her. Telling her what I had done would move the public harm a few hours earlier and force her to receive it from me alone. Staying away was not kindness. It was the last choice that did not add anything.

At midday, Vesh called the bank to listen. The loose Kelt carried his request outward. Set voices repeated it across gaps. Work slowed in widening circles until even the shells near the shallows had turned an arm toward the middle.

Vesh said a question had reached him about Rell at the edge. He said no accusation had been made against her. She had welcomed no one in secret, and no one had grounds to claim she kept a hidden member of her home. He ordered the bank not to turn a question into a charge.

He did it carefully. He began with her innocence. He denied the worst interpretation before anyone else could state it. He placed his authority beside her name. Every word carried the harm.

The nearest homes repeated his statement accurately. Farther out, one voice dropped the part about no accusation. A loose Kelt passed above Marr saying Vesh had asked about somebody hidden behind Rell's door. Another said the keeper had discovered it. A third corrected both and repeated enough of each version to keep them alive.

Marr worked harder than anyone to defend Rell. She told every passer that Rell would never keep somebody, that she had known Rell for years, that the whole idea was foolish. Each defense began by handing over the image of a person behind Rell's door.

I told Marr to stop saying the accusation. She insisted she was stopping it. I said she was carrying it. Marr swept a basket aside so hard that pale grit spilled over the rim. “And who brought it to Vesh?”

I had no answer that belonged in the open water. Marr saw enough in my silence. Both arms settled. “You,” she said.

Nit arrived before I could reply. It had heard three versions and wanted the true one. I said there was no version I could give. Nit asked whether Vesh had lied when he said no one was hidden. I said Vesh had spoken about accusations, not about facts behind a door.

Nit said those were the same thing. I said they were not. “They are if nobody can look.”

That was the bank's problem in one sentence. Public memory could preserve what witnesses saw. It had no place for a private fact that required protection and proof at the same time.

I left Nit with Marr and crossed toward the edge. Versions followed me. The water itself seemed to carry them because voices reached farther than bodies. By the last outer shell, Rell had already heard her name repeated enough times to know.

Her arms were gone. The recovered old tool and the sharpened scraper had both been pulled inside. The silt curve we had made remained visible. Nothing else showed that Rell lived beneath the shell.

I stopped at the distance she had once chosen for me.

“I went to Vesh,” I said. “I promised I would not, and I went. He knew from what I told him. Now the bank is saying the thing you protected from them.”

I did not say that Vesh had tried to help. I did not say that nobody meant harm. Those facts might matter to Vesh and Marr later. They did not reduce what I had done.

I told her she did not have to answer me. Rell did not.

Light left the edge. I remained until I understood that staying was another demand. Then I went inward while her shell stayed dark behind me.

The bank quieted by night, but only because set Kelt closed and loose Kelt slept. The question had not gone anywhere. It was waiting inside every home that had heard it.

## Chapter 18: Nine Days

Rell stayed closed for nine days.

I did not wait beside her. Each morning I crossed only far enough to see whether her arms had returned. I stopped at the last outer shell, watched the empty stretch, and went back. This kept my presence from becoming another thing she had to shut against. It also let me count.

On the first day, nearly every voice on the bank carried her name. On the second, there were four main accounts. In one, Vesh had found a secret welcome. In another, Rell had refused to open during the storm. In a third, I had seen a child. The fourth claimed nothing had happened and repeated every detail to prove it.

On the fourth day, Marr began correcting anyone who mentioned Rell. Her corrections were shorter now. She had learned part of what I had tried to tell her. She still could not defend Rell without naming the thing, but she could refuse to give the whole story another trip around the bank.

By the sixth day, a fight about storm damage near the shallows took most voices. Two neighbors disagreed over which shell had caused a silt ridge and whether the ridge existed before the storm. They had physical grit to point at and arms close enough to point with. Rell's hidden fact could not compete.

The talk did not disappear. It became smaller and harder to challenge. I heard two young Kelt turning a phrase into a rhythm while they carried weed: someone in the edge-home, someone who cannot leave. They did not know what the words meant. The rhythm would last longer because of that.

I stopped them without explaining. One laughed. The other saw my reaction and carried it away as one more clue.

Nit came to me on the seventh day. It stayed near the boundary between the warm homes and the open outer bank, circling one spot instead of traveling anywhere.

Nit asked for the true account. I said I could not give it. “Because Vesh said there is no account, or because Rell said not to?”

Nit had learned to ask the pressure beneath a question. I wished it had learned from someone else.

I said the account belonged to Rell. Nit folded its limbs close. “Then something belongs to her. Something happened.”

I said yes. That much had already crossed the whole bank and denying it would only make Nit work harder.

Nit asked whether someone could shelter and remain. I said the facts I had did not fit the rule. Nit became still in the open water.

Nit asked how anyone could know who belonged in a home if the rule could be wrong. I said the welcome remained true. “But the door does not prove it.” I agreed.

Nit looked toward the yellow-weed place. Its six stones still waited in a ring. “I chose that because everyone near it knows everyone. They know who comes in. They know who comes out. They know who is part of them.”

I had thought Nit chose warmth and reachable neighbors. It had also chosen witnesses.

I said the neighbors knew what they had seen. Nit answered that this was less. I agreed.

Nit moved away from the yellow weed without going toward the edge. It did not make a loop. “I do not like less.”

Neither did I. I had crossed worlds by reducing strange things to facts, and facts had made me useful here. They had also led me to think that if I found enough of them, I could act for Rell without her.

On the ninth day, Rell's arms still did not emerge. The storm silt shifted along our curved release line exactly as she had said it would. I watched the line hold the returning grit away from her rim. One thing we had done together still worked.

That did not give me another right to approach. I counted the ninth closed day and went back before the light changed.

# Part IV: The Rule Under the Rule

## Chapter 19: Every Door on the Bank

On the tenth morning I stopped watching Rell and watched everyone else.

I began with Vesh because age should have made his door the narrowest. His shell carried layers of growth and old breaks. One arm moved slowly at the first bend. If the answer was age, the plate above him should have resisted.

It opened before I finished the thought. A loose Kelt took a bundle inside. The plate closed, opened again for someone leaving, then lifted a third time when Marr sent a question through Nit. Vesh did not open it fully for each use. He opened exactly enough, and the rim moved without scraping.

I counted thirty-four openings before midday.

Marr opened more. Her door received baskets, visitors, food, and information that could have remained outside. One young Kelt went in to retrieve a strip it had dropped through the gap. Marr opened again to tell it that the strip had been badly tied. She could have said that while the door remained open, but Marr preferred a fresh beginning for each opinion.

Coll's door was broad and worked well. Olla's was older than his, and it worked well too. Both had neighbors near enough to clean the rim and loose Kelt passing above all day.

Farther toward the shallows, I found an old female whose nearest neighbor sat beyond two arm-lengths. Her door opened once during the morning for a small basket. The plate caught halfway. She shifted her grip, opened farther, and apologized to the young carrier as if the delay belonged to her manners.

I asked how old the door was. She said it was as old as she was and older today than yesterday. When I asked whether it always caught, she said, “Only when somebody is watching.”

She asked me to repeat the first question because she had been thinking about the basket instead of listening. I stayed with her until the carrier returned for the empty basket. The second opening caught in the same place.

Beyond Coll lived a male whose brood had settled elsewhere or died. I learned that from Marr, who supplied both possibilities and then admitted she did not know which applied to whom. His door was a narrow plate under a mat of soft growth. No one approached while I watched.

Near midday he noticed me and asked whether I had brought something. I said no. One arm returned to sorting grit. “Then you have taken a long route,” he said.

I moved closer, still outside his rim, and asked about the grit. He had been sorting the same two shades for years because the darker kind held heat longer after the light moved away. He offered me a little to carry, then remembered I had no use for it. We spoke until a loose Kelt passed low enough for him to hand over a basket.

His door did not open. Two set Kelt near the center had chosen positions so close that their arms crossed. They spent an afternoon passing a shell of grit back and forth. The shell contained nothing unusual. They could both reach it where it lay. One would pull it inside, close the door, reopen, and pass it across. The other did the same.

On the ninth exchange, I asked what the grit was for. One said keeping. The other opened and accepted it again. “She is poor at keeping.”

They completed fourteen exchanges before the light changed. I stopped thinking the grit needed a purpose beyond the door.

Then I looked at the pattern instead of the ages. Vesh was older than nearly everyone and opened constantly. Marr had a broad rim because a day without visitors would have required the whole bank to change its habits. The two neighbors used their doors to cross one body-length they could already bridge with their arms. Farther out, the old female opened twice in a day and caught both times. The lone male had not opened at all.

The broad doors belonged to Kelt who used them.

The narrow ones belonged to Kelt nobody visited.

Age still mattered. Old shell accumulated wear and growth. But age and isolation had been traveling together so often that the bank had given one the whole explanation.

Rell had been alone for twenty-six years. Her door was not merely a sign of that time. It had been changed by it.

## Chapter 20: Nobody Checks the Number

I needed to know whether doors could widen again.

The two close neighbors gave me a way to test without touching anyone's home. I returned the next morning and asked them to stop passing the grit shell through their doors for one day. Both arms tightened around it. One said that was a long time to hold something the other would only ask to have back.

The other insisted she would not ask. They began the usual argument, and I realized the test would change the habit that kept the doors broad while proving nothing within a day. A door that had narrowed across years would not explain itself on my schedule.

I apologized and let them continue. By the fifth exchange, the first plate moved farther than it had the day before. The owner said a bit of storm grit had washed out of the hinge. Use and cleaning worked together. I could not separate them, but I no longer needed to. Rell needed both, and she had been deprived of both by the same distance.

That gave me the door. It did not give me the person.

I went back to the minor bad water in my memory. Three loose Kelt entered Marr. Two entered Coll. Nit entered the gray shell. I had watched all six emerge because I happened to be outside and interested in the rule.

Nobody else watched all six. Marr returned a dark grain to one of hers without checking the others. Coll complained about weed. The gray shell released Nit into a message already waiting. Each owner knew only a piece. The bank knew the event in the broad way it knew weather: young Kelt sheltered, then came back out.

I found Nit carrying cut weed and asked who counted the loose Kelt entering homes during storms.

Nit shifted the bundle to different limbs. Nobody counted, it said, because there was no need. I asked who checked that everyone emerged. Nit repeated the belief: everyone came out. I asked who checked the bodies.

Nit's limbs slowed. It named the nearest adult, then corrected itself because one adult could not see every door. It named the loose Kelt, then remembered they scattered during bad water and did not know who sheltered where. It named Vesh last and looked toward the crowded middle.

Vesh could not move. “Nobody,” Nit said. The word changed twenty years.

I kept my next question smaller. Had a loose Kelt ever vanished during a storm? Nit did not know. Young Kelt left the bank, settled, crossed to other shallows, and sometimes died. The bank knew those events through talk. A missing person with no final message might become one story in one home and another story elsewhere.

Nit said people would notice a missing young Kelt. I answered that they had noticed Rell had no visitors. Nit folded the weed bundle tighter and called that different. It was not different enough.

Six years after settling, Rell's door would already have opened far less than the doors near the center. A storm drove young Kelt into the nearest shelter without welcome or record. If one entered her narrowed gap and could not return through it, Rell would know only after the water cleared. The bank would not know who had gone in. Everyone would assume everyone had come out.

I could not prove that sequence. I had not seen the storm, and Rell had not told me why the person stayed. I marked each part correctly in my mind: observed rule, reported history, inference.

The inference fit every fact without requiring Rell to keep someone.

It also explained her fear. If she called for help after the storm, the bank would ask why a young Kelt had entered her home and remained. The true answer would depend on a physical rule no one knew. The familiar answer would be a secret welcome.

I looked toward the open stretch. On the ninth day I had stayed away because the working silt curve gave me no claim on Rell. On the tenth, I had found a fact that might release twenty years of blame. Carrying it to her could still be another demand.

I waited one more day.

## Chapter 21: An Answer Is Not Standing

Waiting did not weaken the explanation. It removed the excitement from finding it.

The next morning I repeated the evidence in the order it had arrived. Rell had settled twenty-six years ago. By her account, someone entered during bad water six years later and remained for twenty. Her outer position brought few visitors and little help. Doors that opened often stayed broad even on old shells. Doors that opened rarely caught and narrowed. Sheltering Kelt went through without welcome, and nobody counted them across the whole bank.

The sequence did not require secret welcome, force, or choice after entry. It required only a narrow door, a storm, and a young Kelt who crossed the gap one way. I wanted that to be proof. It was not.

Proof on Sull meant witnesses carrying the same event. I had comparisons, one storm observed years too late, and a mechanism no Kelt had named. I could be right in every part and still fail to show what had happened behind Rell's door.

I went to the old female near the shallows and found a loose Kelt cleaning her rim. The door opened for a food basket while the cleaning continued. It caught, shifted, and rose. The young cleaner scraped a line of hard grit from beneath the plate. On the next opening, the plate passed the old catching place before slowing.

The old female called the movement better. The cleaner said it would need many more visits. The old female replied that visits were what young Kelt did while avoiding work. The cleaner stayed and finished the rim.

That was the beginning of widening, not proof of twenty years. It did show that use and care could change the opening in the direction Rell needed.

I considered Vesh. He had already named Rell. Going back would not create the first harm again, but I could create another one by turning my inference into the official account before Rell heard it. A keeper's belief would make the story travel faster, not make it truer.

So I asked Vesh one narrow question in open water, where no one could mistake it for a secret: could one person speak for another home?

Vesh looked at me for a long time. Storm growth still covered one side of his shell because the cleaning crew had been drawn away by the rumor I caused.

Vesh said a keeper could speak about events witnessed in public, while the owner spoke for what happened behind a door. I asked what happened when the owner could not prove it. A keeper could believe without declaring. When I asked about everyone else, Vesh moved one arm toward the surrounding shells. “The bank chooses what it carries.”

That was not fair. It was also true. I had wanted Vesh to produce a method that would force good belief without public cost. He had never possessed one.

I crossed to the last outer shell and stopped. The old male sorted his two colors of grit. He asked whether I had finally brought something. I said I had a fact. “Keep it. They take up less room that way.”

I stayed with him until he passed a basket to Nit. Then I went to the start of the final open stretch.

Rell remained closed. The water above her moved clear. Nine pieces of driftweed had passed by the time I was near enough to speak, though I had not counted the first two aloud.

“I think I know how it happened,” I said. “I have an explanation for the door and the storm. I will not say it unless you ask. I will come once each day and stop here. If you want me gone, you do not have to open. Leave the sharpened scraper outside the rim. I will see it and stop coming.”

No movement came from the shell. I waited only long enough to make sure my words had traveled through the water. Then I left. For once, knowing the answer changed the limit I placed on myself before it changed anything I asked another person to do.

## Chapter 22: What Caused the Gap

The sharpened scraper was not outside the next day. I stopped where I had promised, counted five pieces of weed, and left. On the second day I counted eight. On the third, the current carried only two, and I nearly stayed because two did not feel like a complete visit. That was exactly why I left.

On the fourth day, one arm lay across Rell's rim. I stopped in the same place. The arm did not move toward me or point closer. “Say the explanation,” Rell said.

I began with Vesh because he was the oldest door I had watched. His shell had age, breaks, growth, and a slow arm, but his plate opened dozens of times without catching. Marr opened more often. Coll and Olla had older homes than some isolated Kelt and broader doors.

Then I described the old female near the shallows. Her plate caught twice before a loose Kelt cleaned hard grit from the rim. It passed the old stopping point on the next use. I described the two close neighbors moving the same shell of grit through their homes fourteen times because the movement itself mattered.

Rell listened without interrupting. I said age wore a door, but lack of opening changed it too. Hers had gone six years with almost no visitors before the storm.

The arm drew closer to the rim.

I told the shelter sequence next. Loose Kelt entered the nearest available home during bad water. Nobody welcomed them. Nobody recorded which door held whom. Every owner saw only a part, and no person on the bank could move far enough during a storm to check the whole count.

“A young Kelt came through your door because the water was dangerous,” I said. “When the water cleared, the gap was no longer enough to go back through. Nobody knew to count that door. You did not make the person stay.”

Rell's arm became still. The water passed over us. Three pieces of weed went by close together, and neither of us counted them. Rell said I did not know the sequence was true. I agreed. I knew the door pattern and the missing count; the rest fit what she had told me, but only she knew whether it fit the event. “It fits.”

That was the first confirmation. Until then, I had held an explanation. Rell's two words joined it to her life.

Her second arm emerged and rested beside the first. “I thought it was me.”

I did not ask what she meant. Rell said she had opened in bad water and found someone inside afterward. The opening would not widen enough. She had believed something in her choice to settle, her body, or her home had caused it. Later, the longer she waited, the narrower the door became. That seemed to prove the fault was hers. I said it had narrowed because nobody came. She agreed.

I expected relief. That expectation was another way of making her answer serve mine.

Rell pressed both arms against the stone. “I could believe I had done one terrible thing. I have spent twenty years trying to find it. You are telling me the whole bank did a small thing every day.”

Nobody had chosen to trap the person. Each neighbor had stayed where habit already kept them. Each loose Kelt had taken another route. Vesh had noticed something wrong and feared the harm of asking. Rell had survived, which made leaving her alone look harmless.

“That is worse,” she said. I understood why. One act could be named. A thousand absences belonged to everyone and no one. There was no single person to stop doing it.

I moved no closer and told her the door might widen with use and cleaning. I had seen one improve, but it would take time. Rell added the other cost: people. I agreed.

Rell's arms remained outside. She did not invite me nearer. She did not close.

The next piece of weed came over the shallows. Rell said, “Six.”

I had counted five since arriving. The three we had let pass in silence made eight, but I did not correct her. We were not counting weed. We were finding the place where an old count could begin again.

## Chapter 23: The Risk Belongs to Both of Us

The next day I told Rell I needed to see the person. Her arms had been outside for less than one morning. The request could have sent them back in. I knew that and made it anyway because the explanation had reached its limit. We knew why the door had narrowed. We did not know what twenty years inside had done or what the person needed now.

“No,” Rell said.

“Then I will not.”

That answer surprised her. One arm lifted from the stone. Rell observed that I had asked only once. I said she had answered once.

I remained at the agreed distance. The current brought no driftweed for a while, which made our silence difficult to measure.

Rell asked what I had expected to learn. I said I wanted a name only if the person offered it, a view of the opening from below, and enough knowledge to stop building plans around someone I had never spoken to. I would repeat nothing outside unless both of them chose it.

Rell pointed to the final step: I would have to come back out. When I agreed, her arm folded tight. “That is the part you are treating as ordinary.”

The gap had narrowed for another twenty years after the first trap. Rell could lift it barely wide enough for me at night, and the storm had put more strain on the rim. If I entered and the plate would not rise again, she could not go for help. She could not leave at all.

I suggested she could call for help. Rell asked what she could possibly say: that Dot had chosen to enter her hidden home and now two people were inside?

The answer ended that plan.

Rell named every loose Kelt smaller than me. Any of them would fit with less risk. Nit was quick, Sennet was careful, and two others were narrow enough to turn inside the gap.

I asked whether she would open for any of them. Both arms settled.

She said no. They would carry the story because carrying was how they belonged to the bank. Asking them to keep it would put Rell's safety against their whole way of living. Nit had already demanded the true account from me; a view from inside would become heavier than Nit could hold.

I said I could hold the private account. Rell reminded me that I had carried it to Vesh. There was no useful defense. I agreed.

I told Rell why the choice differed now. Before, I had believed I could improve her options without accepting her limit. Going inside would place my safety under her control. I would not be the person with more movement and more ways to leave. She would decide when to open and how far. If she refused, the answer remained no.

Rell asked whether putting my movement in her control made the risk equal. I said no. If I stayed trapped, I lost movement. She lived with having opened. The person inside lost more space. The costs were separate.

Rell was quiet.

I admitted one more thing because leaving it out would make the rest sound cleaner than it was. I wanted her to choose me. Not merely anyone capable of looking. Me. Part of my request was still about becoming important to Rell, and knowing that did not remove it.

Rell called that a poor reason to enter a narrow door. I agreed. “It may be the first honest one you have given.”

She did not open that day.

The next morning she made me inspect the rim without touching it. We traced the places where storm grit had caught and found a broken growth shelf on the far side. With her direction, I cleaned it. She tested the plate once and stopped when the scrape sharpened.

On the following day, she told me to come to the rim. I moved close enough that one arm could have touched me. It did not.

“If the opening stops while you are in it, go outward,” she said. “Do not push deeper. If I tell you to stop, you stop. If the person does not speak, you do not make them.”

I agreed to each condition. Rell fitted both arms beneath the plate and lifted. The opening rose in rough stages. At the third, she made the sound I had heard from the dark. At the fourth, the gap was barely larger than I was.

“Now,” she said. I crossed before she had to hold it any longer.

## Chapter 24: Behind the Plate

Warm water closed around me. Rell lowered the door as soon as I cleared it. The narrowing line of outside light showed the rim from below: layered shell, hard growth, and a polished strip where something had crossed many years ago. The last line vanished. The plate settled with a weight I could hear through the whole room.

I had entered a home. The space was smaller than Rell's outer shell suggested and larger than the doorway allowed me to expect. Its curved walls held a dim light with no visible source. Water moved when I moved, then became still. The temperature stayed even. After the storm, the cold edge had disappeared within one shell-thickness.

I waited. Rell's condition came before curiosity: if the person did not speak, I would not make them.

The floor carried paths. Two narrow tracks ran from the door toward opposite walls. A third circled the room and crossed itself near the center. The shell surface there had been worn smooth by the same small body traveling it again and again.

Marks covered the inner walls. Some were shallow curves pressed into the shell. Others were patches where the surface changed color or texture. They did not look like writing. I knew they had meaning because no two sat at random, and because older marks had newer ones placed beside them instead of over them.

I had misunderstood homes. A welcome did not create a rule only in public memory. It allowed a person to enter and change this space. The changes remained after the person left. A place inside Olla was Sennet's because Sennet had crossed it, touched it, and become part of the room's history. Rell had twenty years of new marks no witness outside could read.

Near the far curve, small objects formed lines along the floor. A pale curl of shell. Three dark grains. The dried frame of a weed leaf. A broken tie. Nothing was valuable by the measures used on the bank. Everything had been positioned with care.

The arrangement stopped me more than the worn paths. A trapped person had not merely waited beside the door. Someone had sorted the pieces available, chosen where they belonged, and kept them through years when nothing new arrived.

One line held twenty hard grains, then began again with one. I did not decide what that counted.

A sound came from the corner beyond the objects. Not speech. Several soft limbs moved against the floor and became still.

“I am Dot,” I said. “Rell allowed me inside. You do not have to answer.” The room stayed quiet. I moved no closer. Without the outside current, even a small movement reached every wall. The person would feel me before seeing me clearly. I remembered how Rell had lived with my visits: a body with more movement arriving in the only place she could not leave. I would not repeat that shape inside her.

Something pale came forward. The loose Kelt was a little larger than Nit and thinner across the middle. Many short limbs held close to the body, then released one at a time as the person crossed the worn track. No shell had begun along the back. No plate, no rim, no sign of settling appeared anywhere. Twenty years had passed, and the person was still loose.

The Kelt stopped beyond the last arranged line. I could not find a face, but attention settled on me with the same certainty I had felt from Nit on my first day. This person did not circle me. They waited for me to become understandable.

“I came from outside,” I said. The limbs moved once. I wanted to ask a name. I wanted to ask what the marks meant, how food reached the room, whether Rell opened every night, and what a person counted when light never changed. Every question treated the life before me as material for the answer I meant to carry away.

I stayed quiet. The loose Kelt crossed half the distance between us. One limb extended, stopped, and rested against the smooth path.

Then the person spoke.

“Did the water stop?”

The question made the room twenty years long.

# Part V: The Opening

## Chapter 25: After the Storm

“The water stopped,” I said. “The bank is clear now.”

The loose Kelt waited. One limb rose from the path and touched the wall beside a shallow mark. “Now?”

The answer I had given belonged to the present. The question did not. I looked at the arranged grains and at the marks placed beside older marks. If each one carried a day, I could not know which storm the person meant or how time had divided inside the room.

“The storm that brought you here stopped a long time ago,” I said. “There was another storm recently. That one stopped too.”

Several limbs opened at once and closed again. The movement might have been alarm. It might have been relief. I did not have enough practice with this person to name it.

Rell spoke from near the door. Her voice traveled through the shell instead of through open water. “You felt the new one.”

The loose Kelt touched two newer marks. “The wall pressed. You moved the dark water.”

Rell said yes. I remembered her arms clearing the blocked channel while the current struck her outer shell. Inside, those movements had reached another body as pressure and sound. They had lived through the same storm without sharing the same water.

I asked whether I could sit on the path. The Kelt shifted away from the nearest curve. I took that for permission only after Rell said, “There.” I settled without crossing the arranged line.

The person asked what the bank looked like. I described the crowded middle first because that was where my mind always began: doors lifting, loose Kelt crossing above them, Marr calling after people who had already heard her, Nit moving too fast with a basket. The Kelt interrupted at Nit’s name. “Small?”

“A little smaller than you.”

“Then young.”

I almost answered with a number. On Sull, age had more than one measure. Nit was young because no shell had begun, because a place had not yet been chosen, and because the bank treated Nit’s mistakes as movement instead of history. The person before me had no shell either. The bank would see the same body and have no rule for the years inside it.

“Young,” I said. “Nit wants to settle by a patch of yellow weed.”

The Kelt moved so quickly that water crossed the room and struck me. All the close-held limbs opened. The body came to the arranged line, then stopped before breaking it. “Yellow weed grows there?”

I told the truth in the smallest safe form. A broad patch grew above the warm stones, though part had torn away in the storm. Nit had chosen a place beside it. Sennet carried weed there after joining Olla’s home. Several shallow gardens had begun again.

The Kelt drew back. One limb traced the dried weed frame on the floor. Its veins were too faded for me to tell what color it had once been.

“I was going there,” the person said.

Rell made no sound. The person told us that before the bad water, they had carried ties for a Kelt near the shallows. After the delivery, the current shifted. The nearest opening belonged to Rell. They entered because entry during danger required no welcome and because every young Kelt knew they must leave when the water cleared.

The plate had risen enough from outside. From below, it had lifted less. The person tried alone, then with Rell. Their combined force moved it until the narrowed rim caught. They waited for the bank to notice. No one came.

Rell said, “I called once.”

The loose Kelt answered, “You called until the water took your sound.”

I had been ready to ask why Rell never called later, when the bank was calm. The room answered before I could turn the thought into speech. After the storm, the words would have had to carry the whole impossible shape: a young Kelt inside an unwelcome home, unable to leave, unseen by anyone. Rell would have needed the bank to believe the cause before anyone had observed a door narrowed by disuse. Each quiet day made the account harder to speak. Each year made it easier for others to ask why she had waited.

The person crossed to a low curve in the wall and drew out a folded strip of shell. Rell had passed food through the gap on it for years. New objects entered when the plate could rise a little: grains, weed, broken ties, pieces from the outer rim. Nothing larger than the opening. The room’s lines had grown from what could fit.

“Do you want to come out?” I asked.

Rell’s arms struck the floor once. I knew I had moved too fast before she told me. The loose Kelt did not retreat. They placed the folded strip beside the twenty-grain line.

“There was a place I meant to go,” the person said. “I do not know if that is wanting the outside.”

I held the two things apart. Leaving this room was a physical problem. Living outside it was another. The bank would bring questions, names for what had happened, and years the person had not lived among them. A wider door would not settle any of that.

“You can decide each part separately,” I said. “The opening can change before you choose to cross it.”

The Kelt turned toward Rell. “Can it?”

Rell said she did not know. Her door had changed because nobody used it. Use and cleaning might change it again, but trying would bring people near. The loose Kelt asked how near. Rell answered with the distance of an arm, then with the truth beneath it: near enough to see.

The person returned to the path. “You were seen alone.”

“Yes.”

“They would see you were not.”

“They would.”

They spoke about that cost without me. Rell named Vesh, Marr, Nit, and several Kelt I did not know. The person remembered some names and asked after others who had gone. Rell answered what she knew. Death, settlement, departure, uncertainty. Each reply changed the room more than my description of the bank had.

At last, the loose Kelt asked me one question. “If the door opens, will they welcome me?”

I thought of Sennet beneath Olla’s lifted plate, with the whole bank ready to remember one spoken exchange. “A welcome is for entering,” I said. “You have already entered. I do not know what they will call coming out.”

The person touched the oldest wall mark and then the newest. “Then they will have to learn.”

## Chapter 26: The Way Back Through

Rell decided when the visit ended. She said my name once from beside the door, and the loose Kelt moved away from the path as if the sound were a rule practiced many times. I rose. The person returned the folded strip to its curve in the wall.

I wanted to promise I would come back. The promise would have been easy for me because I was the one able to leave. Instead, I asked whether another visit was wanted. The Kelt touched the newest mark and said, “Not tomorrow.”

Rell told me to examine the opening from below. The rim curved inward. Hard growth made three ridges along one side, and the polished strip I had seen at entry ended beneath the lowest. When Rell lifted, the plate tilted before rising. Its outer edge cleared sooner than its inner edge. That was why a body could enter during bad water and then find the return smaller.

We planned the crossing aloud. I would wait until the plate passed the second ridge, turn one side toward the narrow edge, and go out without stopping. If it caught, I would reverse while Rell still had strength to hold it. The resident said I was too broad at the middle. Rell said I was broad everywhere.

It was the first time I heard them disagree about something with no fear inside it. The difference lasted only a moment, but the room changed around it.

I positioned myself beneath the plate. Rell fitted her arms under the inner edge while the resident braced at the hinge side. I had imagined Rell doing all the work for twenty years. The marks on the floor showed otherwise: two bodies had worn the paths, passed objects, lifted, failed, rested, and tried again.

The plate rose. At the first ridge, fine grit came loose and crossed my back. At the second, the opening became a white band of outside water. I turned and moved. My lower side cleared. The upper edge pressed against me and stopped.

Rell told me to go back. I pulled inward at once. The plate dropped a little before I cleared it, struck one side, and sent a hard sound through the shell. The resident released the hinge and came beside me. Rell lowered the plate fully.

No one spoke until her breathing slowed.

From outside, I had treated the door as an opening waiting to be made larger. From below, it was weight held by a person whose body had grown fixed beneath it. Every attempt spent Rell’s strength. Failure did not leave the door unchanged; the impact shook growth from one place and drove it deeper into another.

The resident ran several limbs along the edge that had caught me. One hard point had shifted. They asked Rell for the thin tool. Rell passed the sharpened scraper through the lowest gap, handle first. The same tool I had carried between them now moved easily where none of us could.

The loose Kelt scraped while Rell directed from the far side. I waited on the worn path and did not offer to help with a rim I could not see. Small flakes collected beneath the plate. The resident pushed them into a separate line, apart from the kept objects.

When they finished, Rell said we would make one more attempt after she rested. I said I could wait longer. Rell answered that waiting inside was not the same choice for me as waiting outside. If she delayed because I was afraid, she wanted me to say so. I said I was afraid of the weight and afraid my exit would injure her. I was not afraid of remaining with them for another day.

The resident asked whether a day still meant the light moving over the bank. I said yes. Inside, Rell measured it by warmth in the upper shell. The resident measured it by food and sleep. Their counts had stayed close until long storms changed both.

Rell rested through one warming and cooling of the wall. When she said we were ready, the resident lay flat against the hinge and placed six limbs under its side. I took my position. This time the plate moved past the second ridge without tilting. A broken piece of growth still narrowed the upper edge, but the scraper had taken its point away.

“Outward,” Rell said.

I turned and went. The edge touched my back, slid, and released me into cold moving water. I did not look down until Rell lowered the plate. Both her outer arms shook against the shell.

I stayed near the rim because leaving while she was strained would turn my successful exit into her solitary cost. I also stayed outside it. When I offered help, Rell told me to clear the loose grit on the side I had struck. She opened twice while I worked. Each opening rose to the second ridge and closed before the shaking returned.

At the third test, a pale limb appeared in the gap. It did not cross. The tips rested against the outside current, opened, and folded in again.

Rell watched the place after the limb disappeared. “Sef,” she said.

I did not know whether she was telling me the name or calling through the shell. Then Rell added, “My brood-sister.”

The new fact reordered what I thought I knew. Rell had not sheltered an unknown carrier and then built a life around necessity. She had recognized Sef after the storm, or before it, or perhaps only after the door would not open. The relationship did not make the trap less accidental. It made twenty years of silence hold a different set of losses.

I asked no question. Rell had offered a name and a relation, nothing more.

“We chose apart,” she said. Brood-sisters did not have to settle near each other. Rell wanted quiet water and distance. Sef wanted the yellow weed and the busiest shallows. They had argued before Sef left with the delivery ties. When the storm drove Sef inside, their last free words to each other had been angry ones.

Rell said she had spent twenty years wondering whether calling for the bank would make Sef’s entry look like a secret welcome between kin. Sef had spent the same years refusing to let Rell carry the whole cause. They agreed on every fact and could not find a version the bank would hear.

“Now there is a door,” Rell said. “And someone who saw it from both sides.”

I told her I would witness only what I had seen. She and Sef would decide what the bank learned about the rest.

The pale limb appeared once more under the plate. This time it stayed in the current while Rell and I finished cleaning the rim.

## Chapter 27: What May Be Carried

I did not go to Rell the next day. Sef had asked for that space before my first visit ended, and getting out a day later had not canceled it. I remained near the center and watched the bank carry ordinary things: food, grit, broken growth, three arguments, and a report that the yellow weed was returning faster where the storm had torn it.

Nit found me before the light moved off Vesh’s shell. It wanted to know why my back was scraped. I said I had passed through a narrow place and had permission to say no more. Nit looked toward the cold edge, then forced its body back toward the basket route.

“Is that the true answer?”

“It is the whole answer I may carry.”

Nit left without asking again. The restraint cost enough that its limbs struck the water unevenly.

On the following morning, Rell had both arms outside when I reached the agreed stopping place. A pale limb rested under the door. The gap had not widened, but they had opened it far enough for Sef to feel the current before I arrived.

Rell said they had decided to tell the bank. Sef corrected her from below: they had decided to tell some facts where the bank could hear. The rest remained theirs.

I asked them to name the facts before naming the audience. Rell began with the storm twenty years earlier. Sef entered because temporary shelter required no welcome. When the water cleared, the inner opening was too narrow. They could not lift the plate far enough for Sef to leave. No one had recorded which door each loose Kelt used, and no one came to count.

Sef wanted the length of time stated. Rell resisted because twenty years would become the only part people heard. Sef said the length belonged to both of them and should not be reduced to make the bank comfortable. They settled on the exact number.

They would not explain why Rell stayed silent afterward. That account contained their last argument, fear of accusation, failed attempts, and choices made when no safe choice remained. Those facts might be spoken later or never. The bank did not need them in order to repair the door.

The repair request was simple: visitors willing to approach at times Rell chose, open and close the plate under her direction, remove growth only where she marked, and leave without demanding to see Sef. Sef might speak. A limb might appear. Neither event counted as permission to move closer.

I repeated the facts and conditions until both said I had them right. Then I asked who should speak.

“I will,” Rell said.

She could not move to the crowded center. If the statement happened at her shell, the audience would also become the first group of visitors, and she wanted to choose those separately. Vesh could not come to her. No public space served two fixed Kelt at opposite ends of the bank.

Sef solved it. Nit could carry Rell’s exact words to Vesh while Rell spoke them in front of witnesses at the edge. The relayed version would not replace the event. It would connect two public places. Sef had spent years receiving the bank through whatever fit under a plate; she understood how a message could remain exact across distance better than any of us.

Rell chose Marr as the edge witness because Marr had named me as the source of the earlier rumor instead of protecting a useful lie. She chose the old female from the shallows because that door had begun to improve after cleaning. Nit would carry. Vesh would receive. Others could listen in either place, but those four would hold defined roles.

I asked what role they wanted from me. Rell told me to describe the doors I had observed and the count nobody kept. Sef asked me to state which parts were inference before Rell confirmed them. Both wanted me to say I had broken Rell’s confidence and helped create the accusation already moving through the bank.

I agreed. Naming my error would not repay it. The statement mattered because listeners might otherwise turn me into the visitor who discovered a hidden wrong and made Rell speak. I had not rescued either sister. I had entered without permission once, broken a promise, and then been allowed to see more after changing how I asked.

Rell chose the second morning from then. That gave Nit time to clear the route and Marr time to complain about the distance. It also gave Rell and Sef another full day to withdraw any fact.

Before I left, Sef asked whether Nit knew who waited behind the door. I said Nit knew only that I had passed through a narrow place and could carry no more. The pale limb moved once against the current.

“Good,” Sef said. “Then the first true thing the bank learns will belong to us.”

On the chosen morning, I reached Rell before anyone else. The scraper lay outside the rim.

For one moment I thought it was the old refusal signal and turned to leave. Rell stopped me. She had put it there for Marr to use during the demonstration. The same object could mean stop on one day and help on another. Permission did not live in the tool. It lived in the person who placed it and the words that came with it.

Marr arrived complaining exactly as promised. The old female came with her young cleaner. Nit waited above us with no basket for the first time I had seen. Far across the bank, Vesh had gathered a second group around his shell.

Rell rested one arm on the closed plate. Beneath it, Sef’s pale tips appeared in the gap and remained visible.

“Carry only this,” Rell told Nit.

Then she began.

## Chapter 28: Two Public Places

Rell spoke in short sections so Nit could carry each one without changing it. Twenty years earlier, bad water crossed Sull. Sef entered the nearest home under the shelter rule. Rell did not welcome her. After the storm, the inner gap would not allow Sef to leave. They tried together. Nobody came to count that door.

Nit repeated every section before departing. Rell corrected “could not open” to “could not open far enough.” Sef corrected “no one looked” to the fact they had authorized: no one came. On the third route, Nit stopped adding any connecting word of its own.

The trips took time. We waited in the cold water while the central group received words already spoken at the edge. More Kelt gathered along Nit's path. Some followed the message toward Vesh. Others came outward until Marr blocked the final stretch and told them hearing did not make them visitors.

One male asked whether the pale limbs under the door belonged to Sef. Rell answered that they did. Another asked Sef to speak so the bank could know she was there by choice now. The limbs vanished beneath the plate.

Marr turned on the questioner. “You asked for proof and changed it into a command.”

The male said he had only asked. Marr told him a fixed Kelt recognized that defense because people used it whenever a request could be escaped by moving away. Sef had no other door. The male withdrew beyond the open stretch.

When Nit returned, it carried Vesh’s first response exactly. He accepted Rell as the witness to her own home. He accepted the four named edge witnesses as witnesses to the statement and the visible limb. He asked me to state the door evidence and the limit of the inference.

I described Vesh's frequently used plate, Marr’s broad opening, the close neighbors’ exchanges, the old female’s catching rim, and the isolated male whose door had not opened during my watch. I described shelter during recent bad water and the fact that no one on the whole bank knew which door held every loose Kelt.

Then I placed the limit where it belonged. Those facts let me infer a possible sequence. They did not tell me that the old storm followed it. Rell confirmed the sequence from inside the event. Sef confirmed it from the other side of the same plate.

I also stated the promise I had broken. Rell had trusted me with a limited account. I carried part of it to Vesh because I thought his authority could protect her. His defense let the bank infer the hidden accusation, and the rumor moved faster than the correction. Rell and Sef had chosen this disclosure after that harm. I had not earned it by causing the first one.

Nit repeated my statement at the center. The route stayed quiet until the messenger returned with a question from an old Kelt I did not know: if the door had trapped Sef, why had Rell hidden her for twenty years?

Rell said, “That is not part of what we are carrying today.”

Nit took the answer. No explanation followed it. The bank waited, perhaps expecting refusal to weaken if enough bodies remained still. Rell lifted the plate to its first ridge and lowered it again. Sef’s limbs reappeared only after the question had traveled away.

The next reply came from Vesh. He said a refusal to disclose one part did not erase the witnessed parts. The bank could decide whether to help the door without possessing the sisters’ whole life.

Two Kelt near Marr objected. If Sef had remained loose for so long, perhaps the shell had been welcome enough to become Sef's home. If so, leaving might be neither necessary nor permitted under old welcome rules. Marr answered before Rell had to. A shelter crossing was not a welcome, and years without another exit could not transform necessity into consent.

The old female beside us opened her catching door. Her young cleaner removed a strip of growth while everyone watched. She opened again, farther. “A door changes when it is left alone,” she said. “Mine did. That part requires no belief about Rell.”

Nit carried her words and returned with questions about repair. Rell stated the conditions exactly as planned. She would accept two visitors at first light and two near the light's turning. They would stop outside the rim, follow her marks, and never ask Sef to appear. Rell could end any visit without giving a reason. Anyone who repeated a private observation as a public one would not return.

Several Kelt volunteered before Nit finished the first route. Rell chose none of them. She selected Marr and the old female’s cleaner for the first morning. Their prior actions gave her a basis beyond eagerness. For the later visit, she chose Nit and Coll. Nit knew how to carry a limit; Coll knew tools and had not pretended knowledge when he sharpened the scraper.

The refusal offended three volunteers. One said the repair belonged to everyone now that the bank knew. Rell’s plate opened to the first ridge. Sef’s pale tips rested there.

“The harm belongs to the bank,” Rell said. “The door is mine.”

Nit carried that sentence without asking her to shorten it.

Vesh's last response came after the light had shifted and the cold water deepened around us. He said he had seen Rell's isolation, suspected danger in asking, and called his inaction caution. The bank had treated the absence of evidence as peace because peace required nothing from it. He accepted his share of that failure.

He did not welcome Sef, declare her free, or order Rell’s door opened. None of those choices belonged to him. He asked the gathered Kelt to witness a different public commitment: after future bad water, every shelter would report how many entered and how many left. Loose Kelt would make their own count as well. Any difference would be treated as a search, not a story about where someone probably went.

Nit brought the commitment to the edge. Rell looked at the pale limbs beneath her plate. Sef tapped the rim once.

“Carry yes,” Rell said.

For the first time that day, Nit added one word of its own after repeating the answer. “Exact.”

## Chapter 29: The Work of Returning

Marr and the young cleaner arrived at first light carrying nothing. Rell had told them not to bring gifts because she did not want gratitude confused with access. Marr complained that empty arms made her feel unprepared. The cleaner said that was probably part of the preparation.

Rell marked one band of growth with the scraper. The cleaner worked there while Marr opened and closed the plate on command. At first the opening barely reached the ridge that had caught me. Marr counted each lift aloud until Rell told her the number was not useful. Marr stopped counting and began reporting where the movement dragged.

On the seventh opening, a pale limb appeared beneath the plate. Marr's whole body leaned closer. Rell lowered the door at once.

Marr moved back before being told. She said she had wanted to see Sef and had moved as if wanting created permission. Rell did not reassure her. The cleaner continued at the marked band until Rell ended the visit.

Marr returned at the next scheduled time. She stopped farther away and waited for Rell to place the scraper. Sef did not appear. Marr did not ask.

That became the measure Rell used. People were not trusted because they arrived with the right explanation. They became useful when they returned after a limit embarrassed them and acted differently.

Nit and Coll came near the light’s turning. Coll inspected the outer rim from the distance Rell set, then asked before touching each new place. Nit opened the plate under Rell's direction. Its smaller body could not lift much weight, but its movements were exact. After four openings, Nit asked whether carrying messages had made it seem trustworthy or whether Rell had chosen it because it was weak enough to control.

Rell said both. Nit considered that and made the fifth lift better than the fourth.

I was not part of every visit. The first time I arrived without being named, Rell left the scraper inside. I remained at the agreed stopping place until Marr finished, then left without adding myself to the work. Two days later, Rell asked Nit to fetch me because a hard shelf had appeared beneath the piece Sef scraped from inside. I examined it, described what I saw, and touched nothing until Rell chose the edge.

The work did not move evenly. Some openings shook grit free and rose farther the next time. Others exposed old cracks that had to rest. On the sixth day, the plate caught below its earlier height, and Rell ended both visits. No one came the next morning. In the afternoon, she opened once by herself and left it there.

On the eighth day, two Kelt who had not been selected crossed the open stretch carrying food. They said gifts were not visits and set the bundles near the rim. Rell told them to take everything away. One argued that refusing food punished Sef. A pale limb struck the inside of the plate hard enough for the sound to reach us.

I expected Rell to answer. She said nothing. Marr came from behind the intruders and moved the bundles back into their arms. “You found a reason her no should not count,” Marr said. “Carry that reason home.”

The two left with their food. Neither was added to the schedule.

Sull changed in less visible places while Rell's door changed by ridges. The old female's cleaner began stopping at the isolated male with two colors of grit. At first the cleaner claimed the route was convenient. It was not. By the fourth visit, his door opened for a small basket and caught so sharply that three nearby Kelt turned at the sound.

He told them it had always done that. Then he allowed the cleaner to clear the rim.

The close neighbors carried their useless grit shell outward and asked him to keep it for one day. He said he had spent years sorting grit and did not need theirs. They left it anyway, then returned before the light changed and accused him of poor keeping. His plate opened twice for the same object.

Nobody called that repair. They argued about the grit and came back the following day. The door opened again.

The visits near Rell remained harder because everyone knew why they mattered. Curiosity arrived disguised as duty. Guilt tried to do too much in one morning. Some Kelt wanted Sef to appear so they could feel their work reaching a person. Rell dismissed one visitor for asking whether the inner marks had counted every year. She dismissed another for telling Sef that the yellow weed was waiting.

The second visitor protested that the statement was kind. Rell said kindness could still claim a future for someone who had not chosen it. The visitor returned five days later, repeated Rell's sentence without argument, and asked to work on the far rim. Rell accepted.

Sef took part from below. The scraper traveled back and forth through the lowest gap. She learned the times of the visits and sometimes added her force at the hinge. Other times she stayed away. No one outside could tell whether she was resting, listening, or refusing to be observed. Rell did not explain.

After two weeks, the plate passed the second ridge on every opening. After three, a narrow Kelt could have crossed if Sef and Rell had chosen the attempt. They did not. The repair continued.

Nit became impatient with success. It asked why they were waiting when the gap looked sufficient. Rell told Nit to turn sideways and pass beneath the lifted plate. Nit did, easily. Then Rell asked whether Nit wanted to decide with its body what space another body should trust. Nit backed away from the rim.

The next day, Nit brought no argument. It opened the plate six times, left, and carried a basket across the bank. Waiting became work after Nit stopped treating it as the absence of work.

On the thirty-first day, Rell opened far enough for light to reach the arranged lines inside. I saw only the nearest pale curl of shell before turning my attention to the outer rim. The room was visible. It was not public.

Sef placed four limbs into the current. She held them there through one full rise and fall of the plate. When the door closed, Rell said the next opening would be private.

Every visitor left the open stretch. No one asked when the private opening would happen.

## Chapter 30: The Open Door

Rell kept the next morning for herself and Sef. No messenger came to tell us when the plate lifted. No witness waited beyond the open stretch. The bank continued its routes while two sisters did something that had belonged to everyone’s attention for a month and to neither of them for twenty years.

Near midday, Nit crossed the center without a basket. It went to Rell, stopped at the usual distance, and returned more slowly. People watched but did not surround it. Nit delivered only what Rell had authorized: Sef was outside.

That was all.

Marr began toward the edge, stopped after one body-length, and turned to argue with a neighbor about a food tie. Coll put away the scraper he had been sharpening. Vesh opened his door for a routine message and did not ask Nit for more. The bank’s first act after learning Sef was free was to leave the fact incomplete.

I went only when Rell sent for me near the light’s turning. Her plate stood open to the third ridge. The room behind it was dark, and the pale curl of shell remained in its line on the floor. Rell rested both arms on the outer rim.

Sef was not beside her. I found her above the shell where the colder current met a band of warmth. All her limbs were open. The water moved through spaces that had been held close inside, turning her a little before she corrected. She held no basket, tool, or line of arranged objects. Her body looked smaller in the open and less fragile than it had in the room.

I stopped before her shadow crossed me. Sef noticed and came down by her own route.

“You were not there,” she said.

“Rell said private.”

Sef had expected the bank to find a way around that word. Someone would claim to be checking the current, delivering food, or keeping the first count. No one had come. She did not call the absence kind. She called it accurate.

The crossing had taken three attempts. On the first, light reached too far into the room and Sef asked Rell to close. On the second, her front limbs entered the current while the rest stayed below. On the third, Rell held the plate and Sef moved outward. She could have told me which limb crossed first or what Rell said, but she did not. The account ended where she chose.

Sef had remained outside for half a day, then gone back through the open door once and returned. The second exit proved the first was not a single escape that could never be repeated. Rell’s home could again be entered and left.

I asked whether Sef wanted the bank to see her. She said the bank already had. Shadows moved far across the shallows, and loose Kelt had slowed on their routes. Seeing was not the same as approaching. For that day, the distance was right.

We stayed above Rell without speaking. It was not a welcome. It created no permanent claim, no remembered place inside a home, and no obligation to remain. Sef had spent twenty years with every silence forced by a closed door. This silence had open water on all sides.

Over the next nine days, Sef chose a larger world by pieces. She visited the yellow weed before first light while Nit waited beyond the patch. She spoke to Marr in public and refused three questions. She let the old female’s cleaner examine the pale growth along her limbs, then ended the examination when too many observers gathered.

Sef did not choose a place to settle. When someone asked, she said an old intention was not a present decision. She slept inside Rell's room on some days and in the weed on others. Each return through the door was voluntary, which changed the meaning of the room without erasing what had happened there.

Rell reduced the repair visits but did not end them. Her door no longer caught at the second ridge. Marr came twice without work because returning only when needed would make usefulness another condition of company. The close neighbors continued sending their grit shell to the isolated male, who complained about the shade and opened for it every day.

The first minor bad water after Sef’s exit tested Vesh's new count. Seven loose Kelt entered four homes. Each owner reported the number sheltered and the number gone. The loose Kelt made their own report. Both totals were seven. Nit carried the matching counts across the bank and discovered that prevention produced less excitement than discovery. It carried them anyway.

I left Sull after the count. Staying longer would not make the ending safer. Sef could still find the open water unbearable. Rell could close her door for a season. The bank could turn its new practice into empty ceremony. Change that required daily use remained vulnerable to days when no one wanted the work.

Vesh asked what account I would carry from Sull. I said a door had narrowed because people stopped using it, a storm had exposed the cost years later, and the bank had learned to count both entry and return. He asked whether I would tell people about Sef. I said I would use her name only where she had permitted it and keep the rest inside the boundary she set.

Vesh observed that this would make the story less complete. I said completeness for the listener was not the measure. He opened his plate, closed it, and agreed to carry that answer even though he disliked it.

Nit met me at the yellow weed. Its chosen ring had survived the large storm, though one side was buried. Nit had not settled. It said the place still looked right and asked whether waiting meant doubt. I said waiting could mean leaving a choice alive. Nit gave me a short strip of yellow weed for the road, then took it back because it had no idea what I would do with it.

Rell did not say goodbye. She told me the far current was stronger near the upper crossing and that my route through the warm channel would take longer but use less strength. I asked whether I could return. Rell said I could come to the stopping place and learn the answer then.

Sef traveled with me as far as the edge of the yellow patch. She moved unevenly in the broad current but refused help before I offered it. At the last warm stone, she asked what worlds did with people who returned after being missing.

“They ask where we were,” I said.

“Do they wait for the answer?”

I thought of every question I had asked because an answer would improve my position. “Sometimes. They can learn.”

Sef touched the current and turned back toward Rell’s open door.

The crossing above Sull ran between two walls of dark stone. I followed the warmer side as Rell had told me. Near the highest bend, a movement repeated behind a broken shelf: extend, wait, withdraw. The shape was smaller than I was and kept itself outside the main flow.

Another traveler approached below me and asked what I could see. Once, I would have pointed. I would have made the hidden body public before knowing whether hiding was danger, choice, or both.

“A difficult current,” I said.

The traveler moved to the safer wall and passed. I remained where the figure behind the shelf could see me. I did not move closer. I did not call anyone back. After a while, one limb extended into the open water and stayed there.

You might want to know who waited behind the stone. So did I.

Wanting was not permission.
