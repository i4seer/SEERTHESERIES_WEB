The Weight of a Word

# Part I: The Visible Load

## Chapter 1: Where the Rope Was Going

The first thing anybody said to me on the ninth world was, “Move.”

This was useful advice. A wet rope was racing through the place where I had stopped, and there was a metal hook at the end of it. I went upward. The hook passed under me, struck the side of a broad flat boat, and was caught by a person standing on four legs at the end of a landing.

“Thank you,” I said.

The person had already forgotten me. They looped the rope round a post with two quick front graspers, leaned their whole low body against it, and brought the boat against the wood. Three more people began unloading sacks before the boat had stopped knocking against the landing.

That was how I arrived at the Landings.

The river was too wide for shouting across. Brown water shoved past the posts, carrying small branches and a great deal of foam. On my side, wooden landings reached from the bank at different heights. Boats crowded them. Some sat so low under cargo that I kept expecting the river to climb over their sides. Nobody else appeared troubled by this.

Behind the water stood a long town that had put all its useful parts at the front. Sheds, cranes, scales, ramps, and open yards faced the river. Houses rose farther back, but from where I floated they seemed like the part added after people remembered they needed to sleep.

I liked the noise immediately. That should have warned me to look harder.

The people were built for the work. Four strong legs held a body close to the ground. Two smaller limbs near the head could tie, point, lift, and argue at the same time. Along each back ran a shallow raised strip wide enough for bundles. Almost everyone had something balanced there: tools, nets, food, folded cloth, a child who had fallen asleep between two baskets.

I watched the unloading because I had never seen bodies and labor fit each other so neatly. A worker could shift a sack from the landing to the ridge without turning around. Another worker added a coil of line on top, tested the balance with one step, and continued up the bank. Loads that would have hidden a standing person rode easily on those flat backs.

Then a new object appeared.

Two women were bargaining beside a pile of narrow crates. One wanted them taken to the north end before evening. The other objected to the distance, the mud, the price, and possibly the woman’s whole family. They were both enjoying themselves.

At last the carrier named a number. The owner made a sound that meant the number was disgraceful. The carrier began to leave. The owner stopped her. They settled somewhere between the disgraceful number and the impossible one.

“I’ll put the lot under your north awning before the lamps are lit,” the carrier said.

Something flat and gray appeared between her graspers.

I moved closer. She had not taken it from the ground. The other woman had not passed it across. One moment her graspers were empty; the next they held a dull slab as long as her forelimb. She slid it onto her ridge beside a lunch bundle and started stacking crates over it.

Nobody stared. Nobody even paused.

You would have stared. I did enough for both of us.

I followed her north. This was not difficult because she carried six crates and complained the whole way about the price. At an awning near the last landing, she lowered each crate onto dry boards. The owner counted them, opened one, and said the top pieces were packed badly.

“They arrived dry,” the carrier said. “You wanted dry.”

“I wanted properly packed and dry.”

“Then next time pay the proper price.”

They looked at each other for a moment. The owner laughed first. While she did, the flat gray slab vanished from the carrier’s ridge. It did not fall or fade. The space beside the lunch bundle was simply empty.

The carrier walked away without checking.

I spent the next hour testing what I thought I had seen. I could not ask the carrier because she had already taken another load. So I watched everyone. A man agreed to mend a hinge, and a small dark shape appeared in his graspers. A girl promised to bring somebody’s cup back and received a piece no bigger than a seed. Two workers divided an afternoon job between them. Each gained a separate object, though one was plainly larger.

Then the hinge was repaired. The little object went away. The cup returned. The seed disappeared.

By midday I had the rule.

Words that committed a person to an action became a thing that person had to carry. Completion removed it. The size had some connection to what had been undertaken, though I could not yet tell whether difficulty, time, or importance controlled it.

I had come to this world carrying a question about all the worlds. An old rhyme on the last one had suggested that ordinary hands had helped close the crossings. I wanted to know why. Instead, within my first morning, I had found a place where an intention could be weighed with your eyes.

I thought this would make people easy to understand.

That was my first mistake at the Landings, and I made it before lunch.

## Chapter 2: Forty-One

The weigh-house opened while the river was still black. I arrived before the doors moved because I wanted to see whether promises began before breakfast.

They did.

A sleepy worker unbarred the doors and promised to oil one hinge before the end of the day. A gray chip appeared between his graspers before the other door had finished swinging. He looked at it, looked at the hinge, and put the chip on his ridge with the expression of a person who had been betrayed by his own mouth.

Inside, the building was larger than it had any right to be. Its oldest beams were dark and thick, but newer roofs and storage rooms had grown from them in several directions. The floor changed from stone to plank and back again. At the river doors stood a balance big enough for a loaded cart. Everybody called it the scales, although it was one machine, and everybody disagreed with it.

The first boat brought grain. Sacks crossed the scale platform one group at a time while a clerk called weights to another clerk with a board. The boat owner objected to each number. A buyer objected in the other direction. The workers carrying the sacks added their own opinions without being asked.

I assumed a fight had begun. Then the boat owner laughed, the buyer improved his offer, and both complained happily about the clerk’s arithmetic. They were not angry. They were doing a familiar job well.

I began counting promises.

By midmorning I had seen forty-one appear. Most were small. Carry this to the second shed. Bring back the scale weights. Tell the south landing that the next hull sat low. Check a split board before somebody put a leg through it. The objects went from grasper to ridge with the same easy motion used for tools.

Twenty-nine vanished before I stopped the count.

That number interested me more than forty-one. A promise here was not a decoration or a threat. It did not force a body to move. The man with the hinge chip passed the squeaking door six times before he fetched oil. He could have passed it all day. The object only kept the fact of his words where anyone could see it.

Long work made larger pieces. A builder promised a repaired hull by the next turn of the season and received a slab that covered most of the front of his ridge. He shifted a tool around it, nodded once, and went on talking about timber. Nobody treated him as burdened. The slab meant work ahead, but the work was work he had chosen.

Other pieces looked old. One woman carried a tiny square near her shoulder. Twice, people asked if she had finally done whatever it recorded. She said tomorrow both times. The second person called tomorrow a very patient day. Everyone laughed, including her.

Then Pip entered.

Pip was six, low even for a child, and moving with the care of an official procession conducted at a run. A single promise sat in the middle of his empty ridge. It was round and brown-gray, about the size of a nut.

He crossed the whole floor to the board clerk and announced that a late boat had to be marked beside the evening arrivals. The clerk thanked him and said she would do it.

Pip remained exactly where he was.

The clerk reached for another paper. Pip looked at her. He did not complain. That made the waiting much more effective.

She glanced at the small object on his ridge, understood the problem, and found the chalk. Only when the boat’s name and time stood on the board did Pip’s promise vanish. He inspected the clear place on his back, thanked the clerk, and left faster than he had arrived.

“His aunt gives him one job at a time,” said a strap maker working near me. “Two makes him furious.”

“Does he always wait for the exact result?”

“Always. If she says tell, he tells. If she says get it written, he stands there until the chalk comes out. She chooses her wording now.”

Pip returned twice before afternoon. He carried a different small piece each time and left without one.

I revised my rule while the next cargo rolled over the scales. A promise-object did not prove kindness, skill, or even future action. It proved that particular words had been said by a particular person and had not yet been completed. You still had to know what the words were. You still had to watch what the person did.

That was less perfect than I had thought.

It was also much more interesting.

## Chapter 3: No Shelf on Me

My usefulness became a public question that afternoon. It began with a net mender on the middle landing. He was tying new cord into a torn edge and had watched me circle the work twice. Most people had stopped staring at my floating by then. He had moved on to the practical problem.

He asked where I set my load. I said I did not have one. He clarified that he meant the load I would have later, after someone handed me a parcel, and I explained that I could not take the parcel. He pulled a knot tight and considered this answer as if poor teaching might still be the cause.

The mender checked above me, below me, and along both sides. I turned slowly so he could finish. There was no hidden hook, no pouch, and no ridge. I am a dot. I can float where I choose, move faster than a walking person, and see quite well. I cannot grip a loaf of bread.

This seemed to trouble him on behalf of the whole town.

Within an hour, three other people had examined the problem. A woman from the north landing asked whether a sling would pass through me. I explained that it would fall. A boy suggested a basket underneath, then admitted there was nothing to hang it from. Someone proposed that I push a light object by flying against it. We tested this with an empty cup. The cup tipped over.

The net mender watched that failure from his stool. “So you can’t take anything anywhere.” I told him I could take myself.

“People mostly arrive where they already are.”

He was not trying to be rude. That made it worse.

I spent the next part of the afternoon seeking work I could do. I could report a number from one end of a landing to the other, but the workers could shout. I could look under a boat without getting wet, which interested one owner until we discovered I did not know what a sound hull looked like. I counted sacks for a clerk who had already counted them correctly.

At last Pip used me. He had been sent to find his aunt, and I had seen her go behind the weigh-house. I told him. He nodded as if I had met a proper standard and ran away. His small promise did not vanish, because finding her was his job, not mine, but I had shortened his route by perhaps two minutes. I was still pleased.

Near evening, a freight boat came in hard against the current. Its operator brought it alongside alone, threw a line to shore, and stepped off with enough objects on their ridge to make counting difficult. There were sacks and tools, a folded coat, two coils of line, and at least eight promise pieces fitted between them.

The operator was called Corrin. I learned that because five people said the name within the first minute. Corrin had a reply for each of them and a promise for two. A narrow piece appeared after Corrin agreed to inspect a pump the next morning. A smaller one came when a message had to reach the scales clerk. Both went onto the ridge without disturbing the load.

Corrin finished tying the boat, handed over a paper, and looked at me.

“You are the one who dropped a cup by touching it.”

“It was already on the ground.”

“That improves it.”

The net mender had spread the news efficiently. Corrin asked the same questions, but faster. Could I hold a line? No. Could anything rest on me? No. Could I carry one of the gray objects if I made a promise? I did not know.

We tested the last one. Corrin asked me to promise that I would remain beside the mooring post until the boat was unloaded.

I said I would. Nothing appeared. We both waited while two workers passed between us carrying a crate and the river slapped the lower steps.

“Say it again,” Corrin said. I did. My words changed nothing in the air around me, but Corrin looked interested rather than disappointed. That was better.

“Then stay anyway,” Corrin said. “I want to see whether you do.”

So I remained beside the post while the sacks came off. It took forty-seven minutes. Corrin noticed when the last one crossed the landing.

“You kept it.”

I said there had been nothing to keep.

Corrin glanced at the empty space all around me. “There usually is.”

That was the first person in the Landings who did not treat my lack of a back as the end of the subject.

## Chapter 4: The One That Stayed

I followed Corrin the next day because Corrin invited me and because I wanted to understand what a normal load looked like on one person. The day began at nine promises.

Two were left from the day before: the pump inspection and the message to the scales clerk. The rest had older surfaces and sat more firmly against the ridge. One was much larger than the others, a thick flat piece tucked behind the working load near Corrin’s rear shoulders. Rope crossed in front of it. A tool bag rested against one edge. The object looked less like cargo than part of Corrin’s shape.

Corrin delivered the message before the first run. That piece vanished. At the pump, Corrin removed a cover, found a strip of cloth wound round a cracked fitting, and used several words that made the pump owner defend his ancestors. Corrin promised to bring a replacement part after midday. The pump promise disappeared, and a new smaller piece took its place.

I asked whether that counted as progress.

“It counts as a different problem.”

We took barrels south, brought empty baskets north, and waited while a buyer argued that wet grain weighed more only because the scales were unfriendly to him. Along the way, people kept asking Corrin for things. Corrin refused some. A request to look at a leaking shed roof received a no before the owner had finished describing the rain. Corrin agreed to carry a sealed note, move a barrel on the return trip, and check whether a boat two landings down had room for a passenger.

By the middle of the morning the count reached twelve. Nothing about the number slowed Corrin. The objects fitted among the useful load because Corrin had spent a life arranging both. Small ones went forward where they could leave soon. Longer work sat farther back. The largest remained behind all of them, never moved and never checked.

After midday, the count fell. The note reached its owner. The barrel crossed the landing. The passenger learned there was room if he came before dark. Each empty space was used at once by rope, cargo, or another promise. By late afternoon Corrin had seven.

“Do you plan that?” I asked.

“Plan what?”

“The order. Which promise sits where.”

Corrin looked over one shoulder at the ridge. “You put the next work where you can reach it.”

“And the large one?”

“That isn’t next work.”

Corrin turned back to the river before I could ask what kind of work it was.

At the south landing, a crane line slipped while a crate was still hanging. The crate swung toward the rail. Corrin shouted once. Two workers dropped low and pulled the guide rope together. The crate struck a post rather than a person, split one board, and spilled metal fittings across the landing.

Everyone moved. I rose above the tangle where I would not block them. Corrin checked the workers first, then the crate, then the crane. One person had scraped a foreleg and was angry about the blood getting on the fittings. Nobody was badly hurt.

Corrin had promised the crate owner that the load would reach the weigh-house dry and complete. I knew which object held that undertaking because Corrin touched it once before looking at the pieces rolling toward the edge.

For the next hour, the whole landing searched. I could see between boards better than they could, so I called out three fittings lodged below the rail. Other people reached them. Corrin counted the recovered pieces twice, wrapped the split crate in canvas, and carried it to the weigh-house.

The owner inspected the contents. One fitting was bent. Corrin replaced it from a tool box without arguing. Only then did the promise vanish.

That left six ordinary pieces and the large one. I had watched Corrin refuse work, take work, change a promise when a problem changed, and spend an hour repairing the result of an accident. Nothing on that ridge suggested carelessness. The old piece at the back was not there because Corrin had forgotten it.

As we returned to the boat, Corrin asked how many I had counted.

“Twelve at the highest. Seven now.”

“Six now.”

I counted again. A tiny one near the coat had gone while I was thinking.

Corrin did not tell me which. The river was darkening, and another boat wanted the berth, and there was line to coil before supper. The large promise stayed under the rope while everything around it moved.

## Chapter 5: Against the Lay

Nilla’s workroom announced itself from half a street away.

Something heavy struck wood three times. Rope scraped across a bench. Then a voice said, “No,” to nobody I could see.

The door stood open. Inside, coils climbed the walls and lengths of line hung from beams in ordered loops. The middle of the room belonged to a rope as thick as one of the weigh-house posts. Its end had been opened into separate strands. Nilla stood over it with all four feet braced and the front graspers deep in the work.

I asked whether I could watch. Nilla said I could if I did not explain the work back. I agreed at once. Later I understood that Corrin had already described me.

Nilla was young, perhaps seventeen, and moved with a patience that seemed to apply only to rope. One strand crossed another. A narrow tool opened a path. The end went through, came back, and disappeared into the body of the line. Nilla pulled the whole splice tight, ran a grasper over it, and said no again.

I asked what had failed. Nilla said nothing had failed yet, which made taking the splice apart seem wasteful to me.

“I’d like the failure to stay in the future,” Nilla said.

Nilla undid several minutes of work to correct a change in thickness I could not see. I moved closer. The strand still looked exactly like rope. Nilla showed me where the lay turned against itself. I said I could not see that either. “Good,” Nilla said. “You’re improving.”

By midday I knew six names for things rope could do and one rule: a splice was not a knot. If I called it a knot, Nilla stopped answering questions for several minutes.

Corrin came in for a hawser that was not ready. A hawser is a line large enough to control a boat and small enough for everybody to pretend it can still be moved conveniently. Corrin said the boat needed it before the afternoon run. Nilla said the rope would be finished when the splice was fair. They disagreed about the time, the boat, and a repair somebody had made at the north landing years earlier.

Neither of them sounded newly annoyed. They had used these positions before.

While Corrin waited, I asked about the large promise. Nilla’s graspers stopped for less than a second, but they stopped. Corrin looked toward the open door, then shifted a coil off the ridge so I could see the gray slab behind it. The words were too shallow to read from where I floated.

Corrin told me the short version. When Nilla was eleven, Nilla’s family life had narrowed quickly. Illness had taken some people out of the work. A boat had been sold. For a while, nobody could say where Nilla would live or what work would remain when the trouble finished changing shape.

Corrin had given one answer. Someday, Corrin’s boat would go to Nilla.

The promise became the large object I had counted. It had stayed on Corrin’s ridge ever since.

“It helped,” Nilla said, still watching the rope. “I knew one thing that came after.”

Corrin touched the hawser. Nilla slapped the grasper away.

The trouble now was plain once I stopped looking only at the promise. Nilla’s room was full. Boats sent damaged lines from several landings. The long rope shed upriver had asked Nilla to work there twice, but that place made new rope in repeating lengths. Nilla wanted old rope with a problem hidden inside it.

Taking Corrin’s boat would mean leaving before daylight, running freight, maintaining a hull, keeping a route, and being responsible for everyone who expected cargo on time. It would mean closing this room or giving it to somebody else. Nilla did not dislike boats. Nilla simply knew which side of the rope was interesting.

“So the answer changed,” I said.

“My answer changed,” Nilla corrected. “The promise didn’t.”

I asked Corrin if they knew Nilla did not want the boat. Corrin said they had been told often and recently.

Nilla tightened the splice. “You were in this room for the last one.”

Corrin considered that. “Then very recently.”

I looked at the old slab. On this world, spoken commitment had weight and shape. I had assumed that made the meaning firm as well. Instead, a sentence that had once given a frightened child a future was now pressing against the life that child had built.

Nilla finished the hawser and made Corrin inspect the splice. Corrin found nothing wrong, which did not prevent a complaint about the delay. They left with the line across the ridge and the promise underneath.

After the doorway cleared, Nilla began a smaller repair. “It was a good thing to say then,” Nilla told the rope.

That was what made the room difficult to leave.

## Chapter 6: Three Doors

I returned the next morning with the answer that had seemed obvious during the night. Nilla was binding the end of a cut line. Corrin had come to collect a repaired block and was standing in the doorway, which saved me from having to explain the idea twice.

“Nilla can release you,” I told Corrin. Both of them stopped. Nilla set the cord down with care.

“Go on,” Corrin said.

I explained that a promise involved two people: the one who spoke and the one meant to receive the act. If Nilla no longer wanted the boat, Nilla could refuse it. The purpose would be gone. The object should disappear.

Nilla looked at Corrin. “Why did we never think of that?”

“We lacked a dot.” I knew they were making fun of me. It was gentle, but accuracy requires the admission.

Corrin shifted the repaired block onto the ridge and turned so I could see the large promise. “Nilla has refused. More than once. Say it again, if you like.”

Nilla did. The words were plain and left no useful opening. Nilla did not want the boat now or later, did not want Corrin to hold it aside, and did not accept any duty created by the earlier promise. The slab did not change.

When I asked why, Corrin said, “Because those are Nilla’s words. The thing on my back records mine.”

I moved close to the slab, though not close enough to read what belonged to Corrin. The distinction was simple and unpleasant. Nilla could control Nilla’s answer. Nilla could not undo the fact that Corrin had made an undertaking.

Corrin laid out the choices while Nilla returned to binding the rope. The first was fulfillment. Corrin could transfer the boat to Nilla. The promise would leave the ridge. Nilla would gain a valuable hull and all the decisions, work, and expectations tied to it. Even if Nilla sold it later, the receiving would be real. The boat would enter Nilla’s life because Corrin needed an object to disappear.

The second was delay. Corrin could keep the boat and leave the promise unfinished. No date had been spoken, so no deadline would arrive. Corrin might carry the slab for years while Nilla continued the rope shop. That sounded harmless until I looked at the two of them. Nilla would see the object every time Corrin entered the room.

“I already do,” Nilla said.

The third choice was breaking it. Corrin could sell the boat to someone else. Once the hull belonged to another person, Corrin could never perform the promised transfer. The slab would stay on the ridge, but its condition would be plain. Anyone looking would know Corrin had made fulfillment impossible.

“Forever?” I asked.

“As much forever as I get,” Corrin said.

Death would remove it from the body but not destroy it. Nilla explained that promise objects left on a person at death could be lifted and stored. That fact opened several questions, but Corrin had to leave for a run, and I still had not solved the one in front of me. I insisted there must be another choice. Nilla picked up the binding cord and said there was not.

When I suggested there might be one they had not found, Nilla said, “That would be another way of saying there isn’t one yet.”

I wanted to object. Instead I asked whether they had tried giving the boat to a third person who would hold it for Nilla, or transferring only part of it, or making a new promise that replaced the old one.

Corrin waited until I ran out. “You can spend some time on those,” Corrin said. “We did.”

Nilla pushed the repaired block toward Corrin. “Take that before you miss the run.”

Corrin loaded it, complained about the repair price, paid it, and left. The large slab passed through the doorway last.

I remained in the workroom and began listing possible arrangements aloud. Nilla corrected the ones that broke ordinary ownership rules and ignored the rest. By noon I had four. By the time the light reached the back wall, I had seven. Every one depended on treating words as if they had been spoken differently.

That should have stopped me. It did not. My strength, if you want to call it that, is that I keep looking after a sensible person would rest. The weakness has the same name.

I promised Nilla I would find the fourth choice. Nothing appeared for me to carry.

Nilla looked at the empty air around me. “Convenient.”

It was. I did not yet understand how much.

# Part II: Every Way Around

## Chapter 7: Held for Someone

My eighth plan required a cooperative boat owner.

I found one at the north landing. Her name was Sella, and she already kept a small ferry for her sister, who had gone upriver for a year. Sella used the ferry, paid for repairs, and returned the earnings to a box with her sister’s name on it. This seemed close enough to my idea that I brought Corrin there before breakfast.

Corrin listened while workers rolled two barrels down a ramp. I proposed that Corrin transfer the freight boat to Sella under the same kind of arrangement. Sella would keep it for Nilla. Corrin would have given up ownership. Nilla would remain free to decide later.

Sella made me name the owner of her sister’s ferry. Her sister owned it. Then Sella asked who would own Corrin’s boat under my plan. I answered Nilla.

Nilla had come because Corrin had warned that I was organizing people. Nilla stood beside a coil waiting for repair and said, “No.”

I adjusted. Sella could own it while holding the intention to pass it on. Corrin turned enough for the large object to show. “Then I gave my boat to Sella.”

I said it would be for Nilla. Corrin said, “Still Sella.”

The problem was not paperwork. A boat could be managed by one person while belonging to another. The Landings did that often when illness, travel, or river conditions required it. But Corrin’s promise concerned who would receive the boat from Corrin. Sella could protect Nilla’s future interest. She could not turn herself into Nilla at the moment of transfer.

Sella asked whether the discussion required her to miss the morning ferry. I said no. She left immediately.

I followed Corrin to a loading job and tried the other half of the plan. Perhaps the promise could remain open until some distant day. The words had contained no date. Corrin could keep the boat through a whole working life, then pass it to Nilla when the rope shop no longer needed Nilla every morning.

Nilla walked with us long enough to answer that one. “You mean when I’m finished with my life, I can have the one you picked.”

That was not what I meant. It was exactly what my plan meant.

At the landing, Corrin loaded sacks while I searched for a cleaner version. Could Nilla receive only the title while Corrin kept the route? Could the boat be transferred for one hour and returned? Could Corrin give Nilla one board from the hull as the beginning of a gradual gift?

Corrin answered by taking me to the bow and pointing at a replaced plank. “If I give Nilla that, have I given the boat?”

“No.”

“Then adding thirty more boards slowly won’t change the first no until the boat goes with them.”

The work continued around us. A sack tore. Grain spilled into a seam between planks, and everybody stopped discussing my idea to save what they could. Nilla fetched canvas. Corrin tied the split closed. I called the weigh-house for brooms because carrying one was beyond me.

When the deck was clear again, Nilla returned to the rope shop with the damaged sling that had caused the tear. The failure had given Nilla work and Corrin another ordinary promise: deliver the salvaged grain at a reduced weight before noon. The new object sat in front of the old slab.

That was the picture my plan could not survive. Every real transfer created work for an actual person. “Held for Nilla” sounded empty only while I left Sella’s route, Corrin’s ownership, and Nilla’s future outside the words.

The boat could be given to Nilla or not given to Nilla. I had spent a morning drawing shapes around that fact.

Corrin delivered the lighter sacks before noon. The small new promise vanished while the large one remained.

## Chapter 8: One Hour of Ownership

Plan nine lasted until Nilla asked who would clean the boat. The idea was simple. Nilla could accept the hull, causing Corrin’s promise to vanish, and sell it the same day. Nilla would not run freight. Corrin would not carry the slab. Somebody who wanted a boat would obtain one. I presented this as an unusually complete answer.

Nilla was replacing the failed sling from the grain spill. One end had worn through where the rope passed a metal eye. The damage was small enough to hide under tension and large enough to drop a sack when the load shifted.

Nilla asked what would happen during the hour of ownership. I said Nilla would arrange the sale. When Nilla asked who would find the buyer, I suggested Corrin could do that first.

Nilla pulled the old sling apart and laid the bad section open. “Who agrees the price? Who checks what comes with it? Who answers if the pump fails tomorrow? Who clears Corrin’s tools out? Who cleans the hold?”

“The hold is clean,” Corrin said from the doorway.

Nilla looked at me. “It isn’t.”

Corrin had arrived with a crane hawser across the ridge. The line had been damaged in the same accident, though it had not failed. Nilla inspected it before allowing the discussion to continue.

My answer was that Corrin could prepare everything. Nilla would only need to receive ownership and pass it onward.

“Only,” Nilla said.

The word made the plan sound different because it was different. Accepting the largest thing Corrin owned so that Corrin could stop carrying a visible consequence would not be a gift to Nilla. It would be a final task placed on Nilla’s ridge, even if no promise-object formed there.

I tried money. Nilla could keep the sale price. That was valuable.

“I know what a boat costs,” Nilla said. “I also know why Corrin bought that one, what had to be repaired, and which route paid for it. I don’t want to turn all of that into coins because a gray thing bothers you.”

I said the slab bothered Corrin. Corrin shifted the hawser. “Didn’t say that.”

I had assumed it. The slab was large. It was old. It made the problem visible, so I had treated its removal as the result everyone wanted most.

Nilla wanted to keep the workroom. Corrin wanted not to force a boat into that life. Those wants met in the object remaining exactly where it was.

The crane hawser had three crushed strands near the middle. Nilla marked the damage and said the whole section had to come out. Corrin objected to losing that much length. Nilla refused to mend a line that would fail beside the repair. Their argument moved from the promise to rope without changing its basic shape: Corrin wanted the useful thing preserved; Nilla would not accept it in the wrong condition.

This time Nilla won. Corrin promised to tell the crane crew the hawser would be shorter. A new object appeared, narrow and dark, and went onto the ridge ahead of the boat promise.

Nilla began cutting out the damaged section. “One hour is still an hour,” Nilla said. “I’d spend it owning a boat I never asked for and getting rid of it for him.”

Plan nine ended there. Corrin left to deliver the bad news about the hawser, carrying one fresh promise and one old one. I stayed while Nilla measured the replacement section twice. The second measurement matched the first. Nilla checked again anyway.

## Chapter 9: Nobody Else’s Words

Plan ten was accounting. I developed it in the weigh-house while three cargo owners settled a chain of work. The first owed storage to the second. The second needed transport from the third. The third owed the first for grain. They placed coins, tickets, and weights on the table until most of the money returned near where it had begun. Their promises did not behave like the money.

One owner agreed to move the grain and received a narrow slab. Another undertook to clear the stored crates and gained a square piece. Even after the clerk reduced the coins to one payment, both objects remained on separate backs.

I asked the clerk whether one person could assume another person’s promise. She first thought I meant doing the work, so I clarified that I meant carrying the undertaking itself.

She tapped the ticket. “I can move a debt from this account to that one if everyone agrees. I can’t move what somebody said.” When I asked whether anyone had tried, she said everyone tried while young and eventually exhausted their family’s patience.

Corrin arrived with a load before I could ask about the youngest recorded attempt. I presented a better version. Corrin could make a new promise to someone willing to take responsibility for the boat. That person could make an equal promise to Nilla. The duties would balance.

Corrin asked how many objects the plan created. I counted two new ones.

“And the old one?” Corrin asked. I wanted it to disappear because an equivalent route now existed. Corrin waited while I heard the error in my own explanation.

Three promises would exist. None would cancel another. They might create a path that ended with Nilla owning the same unwanted boat, but they could not alter who had spoken first.

I moved to division. Perhaps Corrin could transfer parts of the duty to many people until no single person carried enough to feel it. The clerk had been pretending not to listen. She stopped pretending.

The clerk asked who had promised the boat if ten other people each carried a plank. I answered Corrin. “There you are,” she said.

The grain mover passed behind us with the new slab still on the ridge. He was already doing the work. Another person lifted one sack to help. The helper carried weight without carrying the mover’s promise. The distinction was visible in front of me. I had confused labor, ownership, debt, and speech because this town put physical objects around all four. They could overlap. They were not the same thing.

Corrin finished unloading. Two ordinary pieces disappeared during the work. The large one did not.

I called it plan ten. Corrin asked whether it was only one plan. I said it had divisions. “Count those if it helps,” Corrin said.

It did help, briefly. I called the division plan eleven and the balancing plan twelve. The total rose, but the number of ways forward remained three.

The clerk watched me revise my list. “You can count a hole twice,” she said. “Still can’t stand on it.” I wrote nothing, because I cannot hold chalk. I remembered the sentence anyway.

## Chapter 10: Nineteen

The highest promise count in the Landings belonged to a man named Venn. The agreed number was nineteen. I counted eighteen when I found him, but one object lay under a folded rain cover and the town considered the matter settled. Venn ran a handcart between the warehouses and said yes before most people finished asking.

Half the waterfront described this as generosity. The other half said Venn feared the pause required for a refusal. Both halves asked him for help.

I watched him for a morning. He completed five tasks and accepted four more. The ridge never cleared enough to see its surface. Yet he knew every object. When a woman pointed at a thin piece near his shoulder, he told her the requested lamp would be mended after supper. When a clerk asked about a broad square, Venn named the missing delivery and the boat it was waiting on.

“Does nineteen feel different from nine?” I asked.

“Yes,” Venn said. “At nine I know I’ve made a mistake. At nineteen everyone else knows too.”

He laughed and hauled the cart away before I could decide whether the answer was a joke.

At the north landing, the manager carried one promise. People admired her discipline and complained that she was cold. She listened to requests, checked her work board, and refused anything that could not be done. Her single object concerned a roof patch scheduled for the next dry morning.

“Which of you is better?” I asked her.

She looked toward Venn’s distant cart. “At what?” That answer should be kept for many questions.

The smallest old promise in town belonged to a woman called Merra. I never learned its words. It was a narrow chip near the base of her ridge and had been there for more than two years. Merra walked a longer route four times a day to avoid the shed of the person connected to it.

The other woman knew. Everyone knew. She had begun taking breaks behind the weigh-house at the exact time Merra passed there.

I saw them meet. Merra stopped. The other woman wished her a good morning. Merra wished the same and continued toward the river. Neither looked at the chip. Three workers nearby watched with the concentration usually reserved for a damaged crane.

After Merra left, one worker offered me the history in exchange for nothing. I refused. The promise was visible; its wording was not mine. Besides, the town had turned the avoidance itself into a game, and learning the original task would not tell me why both women now protected the game.

That surprised me. A few days earlier I would have read the object if I could. Visible did not mean public, and countable did not mean understood.

Corrin found me near the back of the weigh-house and asked what I had learned. I said nineteen could mean generous or unable to refuse, one could mean disciplined or cold, and a small old promise could matter less than the route around it.

“That’s a lot from counting,” Corrin said. I explained that I had also watched. “That usually ruins a good count,” Corrin said.

Corrin carried eight that afternoon. By dark there were four, plus the old slab. The number told me how much remained. It still did not tell me what Corrin should do with any of it.

## Chapter 11: The Current Takes a Vote

Rain upriver reached the Landings before the rain itself.

The water climbed over two lower steps and ran faster between the posts. Boat operators shortened their lines. Cargo moved from the lowest platforms. Everyone knew what to do, which meant everybody shouted different instructions while doing roughly the same things.

Corrin came in late with a deck of covered baskets. The current caught the stern before the bow line was secure and turned the boat away from the landing. One worker held the line on shore. Corrin held another aboard. The distance between them increased one wet handspan at a time.

“More bodies,” Corrin shouted.

I could provide those.

I went through the weigh-house doors high enough to avoid the incoming carts and called for help at the scales. Three workers followed me back. One brought a coil, which was more useful than anything I could have carried. They caught the second line, passed it round a post, and used their combined weight until the boat came alongside.

During that work, one basket shifted against the rail. Corrin left the line long enough to brace it, and a worker on shore swore because the stern moved out again. Nothing happened in the order anybody wanted. It happened in the order the river allowed.

At last both lines held. The baskets came off under canvas while rain began tapping the roof of the landing. The cargo owner counted every basket and found water under one cover. Corrin opened it. The contents were damp along one edge but not ruined.

Corrin had promised delivery under the cargo terms. The object for that promise sat near the center of the ridge. It remained after the owner accepted the baskets at a lower price.

“Accepted isn’t finished,” Corrin said before I asked.

The owner waved the matter away. Corrin did not. They separated the damp goods, paid the difference, and promised to replace any piece that spoiled by morning. The delivery object remained, and a second smaller promise appeared.

I stayed while Corrin carried the baskets inside. Two other commitments were completed along the way. A message reached the north clerk. A borrowed cover went back to its owner. Those objects vanished while the rain-delivery promise stayed.

The large old slab remained behind all of them.

It would have been easy to describe Corrin as a person who delayed. I had spent days thinking about the one delayed act because it was the problem that interested me. The river gave me a better sample. Under pressure, Corrin did not search for a wording that made wet cargo dry. Corrin named the failure, paid the immediate loss, and accepted a new duty for whatever came next.

By evening the damp pieces had been spread in a warm loft. Corrin checked them with the owner and completed everything the cargo terms required. The delivery promise vanished. The new one remained until morning because spoilage still might appear.

“Does the object decide when enough has been done?” I asked.

“No,” Corrin said. “The promise does.”

That answer left exactly as much room for judgment as it sounded like.

The rain strengthened after dark. Corrin had five new errands because weather makes work faster than people can complete it. Yet the wet cargo had been dealt with before supper, and the old slab was still not neglect.

It was a choice being renewed every day Corrin kept the boat.

## Chapter 12: Knowing the Cost

The morning inspection found no spoiled goods. Corrin’s last weather promise vanished before the first scale argument began. I had twelve failed plans and no thirteenth.

For two days I tried to make one. I watched transfers at the weigh-house, asked about inheritance without reading anyone’s stored objects, and followed a boat partnership through its ordinary paperwork. Every route reached the same wall. Corrin had promised a boat to Nilla. Any arrangement that did not make Nilla the owner failed the words. Any arrangement that did make Nilla the owner failed Nilla.

Late the next afternoon, a tapping sound led me aboard Corrin’s boat. Corrin was driving out the pin from a worn fitting. Clean metal showed on two faces where the part had already been turned to extend its use. Three different people had told me how long the replacement had been waiting, so I did not ask.

I admitted I had nothing else. Corrin kept working the pin loose and called twelve respectable. When I confessed that some were one plan divided, Corrin said, “Then less respectable.”

I said the failure should trouble Corrin more. That was unfair. I meant it should look as if it troubled Corrin more, because I wanted evidence that matched the size of the slab.

Corrin set the tool down and said each choice was already understood. Giving Nilla the boat would protect Corrin’s record and place an unwanted future on Nilla. Keeping the boat would preserve both working lives for now, but Nilla would watch the promise every day and feel increasingly responsible for Corrin’s load. Selling it would free Nilla from both boat and waiting, cost Corrin the hull, and leave a broken promise visible for the rest of Corrin’s life. “That’s not nothing,” Corrin said. “That’s the whole problem.”

I wanted the answer to be hidden because hidden answers can still be found. Corrin and Nilla had already done the finding. What remained was choosing a cost.

“Why not choose the easiest one to live with?” I asked.

“Easiest for which person?”

The question made all twelve plans fail again at once.

Corrin returned to the fitting. The old pin came free. Under it, the metal was worn wider on one side, which meant the new part would not sit true without more work. Corrin stared at the wear and used a quiet word I had heard much louder at the pump.

I said keeping the boat forever would be easiest for Corrin’s work. Corrin answered that Nilla would spend every sight of the slab wondering whether Corrin still waited for agreement.

I suggested Corrin deny that. “I have,” Corrin said.

Words could settle facts here, but they could also leave an object that argued with every later sentence.

I stayed while Corrin cleaned the fitting seat. A passing worker asked whether Corrin would move a crate after finishing. Corrin checked the river, the light, and the remaining repair before saying no. No object appeared. The worker asked someone else.

That refusal mattered because Corrin could make one. The old promise had come from a time when saying yes gave an eleven-year-old something firm. Its cost had grown because the people had changed, not because either of them had been careless.

“Have you decided?” I asked.

Corrin fitted the new part and tested its movement. “I’ve stopped pretending there’s a door you’ll discover if I wait long enough.”

That was not an answer about which cost Corrin would choose. It was an answer about what would happen next.

# Part III: What the Shelf Kept

## Chapter 13: After the Speaker

The weigh-house had one quiet hour, and quiet was a relative word. Morning cargo had moved onward. Evening boats had not arrived. Only two clerks worked at the scales, one cart crossed the floor, and somebody at the back was dropping boards at regular intervals. In the Landings, this counted as peace.

I went there because Corrin’s mention of stored promises had opened a question I could examine without improving anybody’s life for them.

The back of the building held objects that had outlasted their use. Broken scale arms stood against the wall. Old ledgers filled a cupboard with a door that did not close. Hooks, measuring rods, and tools for fittings nobody used sat on a low shelf. At the far end were two flat gray pieces beside a bucket.

I had seen them before and mistaken them for parts of the scales.

Up close, they had the same dull surface as every promise I had watched appear. Their edges were rounded from handling. Dust lay beneath them but not on top, which meant someone moved or wiped them sometimes.

Pip came in carrying a promise the size of a bean. He had to get a tally corrected. He went straight to the clerk, waited for the number to be changed, watched his object vanish, and ran out without looking toward the shelf. Pip had a gift for ignoring any fact that was not his current errand.

I asked the nearer clerk why the two promises were not on people. She kept adding a column and said the people had stopped being available.

When I asked where they had gone, she looked up. “They died.”

I had expected an answer involving transfer or court storage. Death was simpler. The promise object remained when the person’s body no longer carried it. Family, friends, or whoever handled the dead person’s things could lift it from the ridge and put it somewhere else.

I asked whether the promise still counted as unfinished. The clerk said it was unfinished.

When I asked whether someone else could complete it, she said, “They can do the same act. They can’t complete what the dead person said they would do.”

The clerk returned to the column. I looked at the two pieces again.

Every other promise in the town moved through a living day. It rode under tools, got wet, blocked a load, or vanished during work. These had lost the possibility of change. They were not broken in the same way Corrin’s might become broken. They were the last physical record of something a person had meant to do and never did.

I wanted to read them.

That wish arrived before the question of permission. Merra’s small old promise had taught me something, but not enough to remove curiosity. I moved near the shelf, found shallow marks on the surfaces, and stopped before I could make out words.

The clerk noticed. “The one on the left belongs to the building. The family that kept it gave permission for the text to be read. The other is stored for someone who lives upriver.”

I moved left.

The rule was not that old promises became public. The rule was that dead people could not protect privacy, so living people had to decide who would. The distinction mattered even when the objects sat together under the same dust.

The approved piece was too low for comfortable reading. I lowered myself to a hand’s width above it. Lines covered the gray surface. They were shallow, the same color as everything around them, and nearly invisible until the light crossed at an angle.

I read only the first few words before I stopped again.

They concerned the crossings between worlds.

Pip returned through the doors with a new errand and nearly struck the bucket. The clerk told him to slow down. He did not.

The ordinary room continued around the shelf. My question had been waiting there for longer than the oldest beam above me.

## Chapter 14: Family Property

I did not read farther that afternoon. Permission to read a thing was not the same as being ready for its answer. I left the shelf when the evening boats arrived and spent two hours helping by going where messages needed to go. None of the messages formed objects on me. All of them mattered to somebody for at least a minute.

After dark, I found Corrin on the boat tightening a line around a spike. The river had dropped one step from the rain height, but the current still pulled hard at every hull.

I told Corrin about the shelf. Corrin checked that I meant the left object, then said it had come through Corrin’s family.

Corrin said it without stopping the work. A distant relative, many generations back, had carried the object home after the crossings were closed. Later the family gave it to the weigh-house because the building could keep dry records longer than any one house could. The right to read it went with that gift.

I asked whether the relative had helped close the crossings. Corrin said the person must have taken on some part.

When I asked which part, Corrin pulled the strand tight and tested it. “The part written there.”

That was all Corrin knew. No hidden family account had been passed down. No name survived with the object. The relation might have guarded a crossing, carried a notice, built a barrier, kept a record, or done something neither of us could name. Corrin would not turn missing facts into a better story for me.

The family discussed the object when someone asked. Children learned about it mainly through the rule against spilling anything on the shelf.

I waited for more. Corrin finished the line, coiled the loose end, and complained that the new fitting had made the old bracket look worse. The historical object did not pull attention from the work.

I said the object explained why the crossings had shut. Corrin agreed as if this were not a surprising sentence.

When I pointed out that Corrin already knew, Corrin said, “Most people know the shape of it. Unsafe passage. Worlds affecting each other. Deaths. Then closure.”

Corrin had spoken four short phrases and returned to the bracket. I had crossed eight worlds collecting pieces of that answer. Here it belonged to childhood history and a dusty shelf.

At first I thought this meant Corrin did not understand its size. Then I looked at the boat, the lines, and the ordinary promises arranged on the ridge. Corrin understood many large things without stopping work for them. The river did not pause because history had entered the conversation.

I asked whether anyone had tried to reopen the crossings. Corrin said there had been several attempts long ago.

When I asked what became of them, Corrin said, “You crossed them closed.” The answer was exact rather than cruel.

Corrin knew no details of the attempts. Corrin’s grandmother had known more, but she was dead, and no object on the shelf contained everything a person had understood. The promise recorded words. It did not record a life around them.

I asked if Corrin minded my reading it. Corrin reminded me that the family had donated it for that purpose.

When I separated Corrin from the earlier family, Corrin checked the stern line. “I am in the family. If the dead object, they have not managed to say so.”

I went back toward the weigh-house. Behind me, Corrin shouted at a passing boat for throwing wake against the landing.

The largest answer I had ever approached waited beside a bucket. Nobody guarded it. Nobody needed me to admire it. I only had to lower myself close enough and read what the dead person had chosen to carry.

## Chapter 15: Four Undertakings

I returned during the next quiet hour and read the whole object.

The writing was not a story. It was a record of a decision, cut into four parts by spacing. I read each part, moved away, and returned to make sure my wish for an answer had not changed the words.

The first part said that a group of people accepted the work of closing the routes between worlds. They gave a reason. Passage was taking the laws of one world into another, where those laws did not belong. The resulting failures had killed people.

I stopped after that.

For eight worlds I had seen rules that could not travel cleanly. Distance changed. Memory behaved differently. Roads, shadows, names, and weather held powers that belonged to one place and made no sense in the next. I had treated those differences as separate mysteries. The object named their connection in the plain language of a safety report.

People had died when world-rules crossed with travelers.

The second part recorded the damage closure would cause. Travelers would be trapped away from home. Families and whole communities would be divided. Routes used for work, care, and ordinary life would end. The decision would affect people who had never agreed to it.

Nobody had hidden that cost inside softer words.

The third part committed the speakers to keep the crossings shut for as long as travel remained dangerous. That line mattered because closure was not described as a punishment or a permanent preference. It was a safety action tied to a condition.

Then came the fourth undertaking.

When travel could be made safe, the speakers would restore passage.

I read the four parts again. Close the routes because incompatible rules were killing people. State in advance that closure would strand and divide people who had no vote. Maintain the barrier while the danger continued. Open the ways again after someone solved the danger.

The promise object still existed because the last condition had never been reached.

The clerk at the scales called a weight. Someone disputed it. A cart wheel knocked against a floor seam three times before climbing over. I stayed above the shelf while ordinary sound filled the room around the words.

My first feeling was relief. I had wanted an agent behind the closing, and here were people who had acted. Not a natural collapse. Not an unnamed force. People had examined a danger and chosen to seal the crossings.

Then the rest of the wording returned.

They had acted because people were dying. They had written down the suffering their remedy would cause. They had promised restoration rather than claiming the loss did not matter. The answer gave me people, but it did not give me the shape of guilt I had expected.

I went close enough to study the cuts. More than one voice stood behind the repeated word for the group, but this object belonged to one speaker. Corrin had said there would once have been many such pieces, one for every person who took on a share. I was looking at a single person’s undertaking within a larger decision.

The object was not large. It covered less space than Corrin’s boat promise.

That did not mean closing worlds was smaller than giving away a boat. Promise objects measured what the speaker personally took on, not the final size of everything that followed. One person in the closing had carried this piece. Thousands of other pieces might have gone to thousands of other ridges.

Most would be gone now. Fulfilled parts may have vanished during the work. Unfinished pieces may have been buried, stored elsewhere, lost, or destroyed. I could not know. This one survived because a family carried it home and later put it where damp ledgers and changing houses were less likely to erase it.

I had arrived asking why. The shelf answered: to stop deaths caused by unsafe passage.

It also answered a question I had not known to ask. The people who closed the worlds understood that saving lives would ruin other lives, and they acted with both facts in front of them.

## Chapter 16: What Crossed With Us

I spent the rest of that day testing the first undertaking. The promise said passage carried more than travelers. I had eight worlds of evidence and had never arranged it that way. So I walked the Landings from south to north and rebuilt the list while work moved around me.

On one world, distance could fold under particular conditions. A short road became a long one if approached wrongly. On another, memory did not belong only inside the person remembering. Elsewhere, names, shadows, weather, or paths obeyed rules that would be impossible here. I had crossed between those places.

If a traveler brought even a small part of one rule into a world built around another, the result would not have to be dramatic to kill. A distance changed at the wrong step. A remembered danger entered a room. A road failed to lead where food or shelter waited. People could die because one world’s ordinary fact became another world’s failure.

At the south crane, workers were replacing the shortened hawser. Nilla had made an eye at the new end. The crane operator tested it first with an empty hook, then with a small load, then with the full weight. Nobody called the first two tests unnecessary simply because they had succeeded.

“What happens if this splice follows the wrong rule?” I asked Nilla.

Nilla looked at me as if I had finally invented a useful question. “It parts under load.”

“Even if that rule works somewhere else?”

“If somewhere else wants the rope, somewhere else can have it.”

Here, fibers had to cross in the right order and lie with the twist. A perfect fastening built for different material might be useless. The worlds were larger than ropes, but the failure was not harder to understand.

I told Nilla the first part of the historical object. Nilla already knew the broad reason, as Corrin had. The exact wording interested Nilla because it treated passage itself as the carrier of danger rather than blaming travelers.

“That matters,” Nilla said.

I asked why.

“A person can follow every rule they know and still bring the wrong rule with them.”

The crane lifted the full test load. The new eye tightened, held, and lowered the weight cleanly. Nilla inspected the splice again after success because success under one load did not promise the next.

I moved to the middle landing and watched boats exchange goods. A seed from upriver grew here because both banks belonged to the same world. A tool worked at every landing because metal, force, and distance remained consistent along the river. Trade depended on rules staying boring.

Cross-world travel had not offered that safety.

The wording on the shelf had not said danger might occur. It recorded deaths already happening. Closure came after failures, not before risk. Somewhere, people had watched enough harm to accept dividing every route rather than allowing another crossing.

I wanted numbers. How many deaths? On which worlds? Which rule moved first? The object held none of that. It was a promise, not an investigation report. Corrin did not know. The clerk did not know. The dead speaker’s missing life could not be recovered from the surviving words.

That absence did not make the reason weak. It only limited what I could claim.

By evening, I believed the first undertaking. Passage had become unsafe, and the failures had killed people. The belief did not make closure kind. It made the decision real enough to judge without replacing its reason with my anger.

Back at the weigh-house, the object waited in the same place. I read the line about deaths once more, then the line about the people who would be stranded.

## Chapter 17: Someone to Point At

The next morning I went looking for fault.

I did not call it that. I called it checking the decision. This is a useful name when you want to keep doing something after you know why you are doing it.

First I examined the reason. Had the closers protected themselves while shutting everyone else out? The object did not say. It named deaths from unsafe passage, but not which worlds suffered most or who held power. I could imagine selfish arrangements. I could not turn an imagined arrangement into evidence.

Then I examined the warning about harm. Perhaps it had been added later to make the decision look kinder. That failed because the words existed on the same promise object as the action. The speaker took on closure, its human damage, continued safety, and possible restoration as one undertaking. I had no basis for separating the uncomfortable part into later decoration.

I tried the people who came afterward.

Generations had lived while crossings stayed sealed. Surely one of them should have restored the routes. Corrin had said attempts were made, but Corrin did not know details. I asked the weigh-house clerk where histories were kept. She directed me to a locked cabinet and the person with its key.

The key holder was away upriver.

For one embarrassing minute, I considered the lock as if looking hard might open it. Then I remembered that even an open cabinet would contain records I had no permission to use. The source boundary was the promise, Corrin’s limited account, and what this world showed me directly. A locked door did not become an invitation because I disliked uncertainty.

I returned to Corrin at the north landing. Corrin was arguing over the measured depth of a boat that both people could see.

When the argument ended, I said the closers had tried to make passage safe. Corrin answered that this was what people were taught.

I asked whether they had tried enough. Corrin considered the river. “Could be. I don’t know what enough looked like.”

I wanted Corrin to defend the ancestors so I could find the weak point. Instead Corrin left the uncertainty where it belonged.

I asked what it would mean if safety had been possible and the earlier attempts simply missed it. Corrin said they would have missed it.

When I tried to turn that into complete fault, Corrin said, “Some part, perhaps. You’d need to know what they had, what they tried, and what failed.”

I had none of those facts.

The condition mattered. The dead speaker had not promised restoration after a number of years or after people became tired of loss. The undertaking tied reopening to safe passage. Time could deepen the harm without changing whether the condition had been met.

That felt wrong because time does change people’s duties. A temporary barrier can become neglect. A careful delay can become cowardice. Corrin’s promise had shown me that. Yet declaring a lethal route safe because the closure had lasted too long would not repair neglect. It would create new deaths.

I could hold both failures in view. Later generations might have stopped trying. They might have tried badly. They might have done everything possible and failed. The object and Corrin’s knowledge did not choose among those histories.

The honest answer was smaller than the answer I wanted. The crossings had not been shown safe. I could not name a person who knowingly refused a safe restoration.

Corrin began unloading. I watched the large slab move with the body beneath it. Here was a problem whose people, facts, and choices I knew, and I still could not make one person guilty of the whole thing.

The ancient closing covered more worlds, more dead, more stranded lives, and more missing years. I had wanted its blame to be easier because its harm was larger.

It was not.

## Chapter 18: The Afternoon Continues

I told Corrin my answer while the new fitting failed to fit.

We were aboard the boat. Corrin had removed the bracket, cleaned it, and discovered that one hole sat half a finger-width from where the replacement expected it. This was apparently the fault of the boat builder, the fitting maker, and a former owner who had believed measurement was optional.

“People closed the crossings,” I said. “They did it because travel was carrying rules across worlds and the failures were killing people. They knew closure would strand travelers and split families. They promised to keep the routes shut while passage stayed unsafe and restore them if safety became possible.”

Corrin held the bracket against the new part. “Yes.”

I waited while Corrin tried the bracket upside down, though both of us knew this would not help. Corrin declared it badly made.

I asked whether Corrin had heard me. Corrin set the bracket down. “I did. You found the old promise.”

I had expected the answer to change the air around us. Instead the river struck the hull, somebody called for a line at the next landing, and the bracket remained wrong.

I said the closers had been right to stop the deaths and had still harmed people with no choice. Corrin agreed.

When I said I was angry, Corrin looked at me. “That seems fair.”

It was not enough. I wanted anger to point. Anger without a person at the end of it felt like a message with no landing. I told Corrin that too. Corrin did not offer a name. “Some old things don’t improve because you understand them.”

We were quiet for perhaps four seconds. Then someone shouted from the south that the crane was crooked. Corrin called back that it had always been crooked.

The person said it was more crooked, so we went to see.

The crane had settled toward the river after the high water softened ground under one support. A repair crew had packed boards beneath it. One board extended beyond the footing and carried no weight, which three people treated as proof that the whole repair was decorative. Corrin agreed with all three and then disagreed with their solution.

For the next hour, the largest fact I had found in eight worlds existed beside levers, mud, and a crane that needed to be kept from falling into the river. I fetched Nilla because the shortened hawser had to be moved. Nilla refused to let anyone drag it across a sharp edge. Corrin found a roller. The crane was raised, the bad packing removed, and a wider support set under the sinking side.

The work mattered. If the crane failed, somebody could be hurt and freight would stop. Understanding the crossings did not make the support less urgent.

When the crane stood straight enough for everyone to argue about a smaller difference, Corrin returned to the boat. I followed. The bracket still did not fit.

Corrin marked a new hole and began drilling by hand.

I asked what I should do with the answer. Corrin told me to know it.

When I asked whether that was all, Corrin turned the drill. “You can keep looking. Just don’t turn what you wish was there into what you found.”

I stayed until the hole was finished. The bracket went on straight. Corrin moved the fitting through its full travel twice and then once more because two tests had not been enough at the crane.

The answer about the worlds did not close anything. It did, however, change what I was allowed to say was true.

# Part IV: The Cost Chosen

## Chapter 19: A Man From Downriver

Deth arrived on the morning boat with a folded paper and the manner of someone who had traveled for a particular answer.

He was square even for the people of the Landings. A small bag sat on his ridge beside two promise objects. Before stepping fully onto the landing, he looked along the tied boats and found Corrin’s.

Corrin was waiting. I understood that fact only later. At the time, Corrin’s presence seemed ordinary. Corrin was often at the water before the first boat. But no freight waited aboard, no tools were set out, and the hatch covers had been cleared. The deck looked ready for inspection.

Deth greeted Corrin, glanced at me, and asked whether the hull still took water near the stern. Corrin said every hull took water.

Deth repeated that he meant the stern. “Less than yours,” Corrin said. Deth accepted this as useful and went aboard.

I followed because nobody told me not to and because the folded paper bothered me. Deth checked the pump, the ribs below deck, the repaired bow plank, and the fastening Corrin had finally replaced. He knew where to look. At each place, Corrin named a fault before Deth found it and objected when Deth called the fault serious. This was not a visitor admiring a boat.

Deth had been asking about a hull for more than a year. His own cargo work ran two days downriver, where shallow side channels punished a deep boat. Corrin’s hull sat flatter and could take the route. These facts emerged through argument rather than explanation.

When Deth pointed out that Corrin had called the stern sound, Corrin insisted it was. Deth reminded Corrin about the water.

“Water isn’t unsound,” Corrin said. “Water is the river trying to own the boat.”

They inspected the stern again.

I counted Corrin’s promises while they talked. Six ordinary pieces sat around the large slab. None concerned Deth, as far as I could tell. No new object appeared because no commitment had yet been spoken.

Then they began discussing the price, and I moved closer. Deth named a number. Corrin said it would buy the pump and perhaps one side of the hull. Deth pointed out three repairs. Corrin listed seven improvements. The numbers moved toward each other by very small amounts while both people behaved as if every movement caused pain.

The folded paper was a draft transfer record. That was when I knew Corrin had chosen.

Nobody had announced it to me. Corrin had not returned after our fitting conversation to explain which door would be used. A decision that would cost the boat and mark Corrin for life had moved forward through a message sent downriver, a cleared deck, and a meeting before breakfast.

I had wanted a declaration because declarations are easier to recognize. Corrin had made the choice usable instead. Deth had traveled. A paper had been prepared. The boat was being inspected by the person who might own it.

“When?” I asked. Both of them looked at me, so I clarified that I meant the transfer.

“If we agree,” Deth said.

“We haven’t,” Corrin said.

They had nearly agreed. I could hear it in the small distance between the prices.

Nilla appeared at the top of the landing carrying a repaired line. Nilla saw Deth aboard, saw the paper, and stopped. Corrin did not call out. Nilla did not come down. After a moment, Nilla set the line by the landing post, turned, and went back toward the workroom.

Deth asked whether Nilla was the person connected to the promise. Corrin said yes. Deth did not ask for the words.

The price argument resumed. I stayed over the stern while the river moved beneath us and the gap between two numbers became small enough to cross.

## Chapter 20: What Goes With the Hull

The number was agreed before midday, but a number did not sell a boat. Deth’s paper listed the hull, pump, mast, steering gear, tiedown rings, deck covers, and a collection of smaller fittings Corrin believed were included only when praising the boat. Deth believed every useful object aboard should come with the price, so they worked from bow to stern deciding what would remain.

The spare line belonged to Corrin. The anchor belonged to the boat. The tool box was Corrin’s, except for two tools bought from route earnings and one wrench Deth claimed had been borrowed years earlier. Corrin called that claim an attack on memory. I counted forty-three separate decisions.

The process showed me what “give the boat” would have meant for Nilla. A hull was not one object in practice. It carried maintenance, records, equipment, habits, old repairs, missing tools, expected routes, and arguments that had begun before the current owner. Ownership gathered all of them even when the transfer sentence stayed short.

Nilla returned with the repaired line after lunch. This time Nilla came down the landing, placed it on deck, and watched Corrin check the splice. “You always do that,” Nilla said.

“You always expect me not to.”

Deth watched without interrupting. Nilla asked what was happening to the route work already booked. Corrin had arranged to finish two loads before the transfer and send one later job to another operator. Deth would take no Landings contracts until returning downriver.

“And the rope account?” Nilla asked.

Corrin would pay the open repair charges from the first part of the sale. Nilla said the charges were not all due yet. Corrin said they would be due by the time anyone found the correct paper, which was an argument strong enough to end that question.

Nilla examined the cleared deck. “When?”

“Tomorrow, if Heth can witness.”

The answer changed Nilla’s stance. I cannot tell you what Nilla felt. I can tell you that one rear foot moved back, then returned to its earlier place, and the front graspers pressed against the repaired line until the fibers bent.

Nilla asked whether Corrin had chosen freely. Corrin said yes.

Nilla said the choice existed because of the refusal. Corrin answered, “Because the choices are mine.”

Nilla looked toward the large slab. “That one says otherwise.”

Corrin did not turn. “That one says I spoke. It doesn’t say you owe me the ending.”

Deth moved to the bow and became interested in a ring that had already been inspected. I moved as well. The conversation belonged to Nilla and Corrin, but both had chosen to have it on an open deck where leaving entirely would have made a show of privacy. We gave them distance instead. They spoke more quietly. I did not hear the words and did not go closer.

Afterward Nilla lifted the repaired line onto the ridge and carried it off again. Corrin called out that the boat still needed it for the final loads. Nilla said the line could return in the morning and continued up the landing.

Taking it was not sabotage. It was Nilla’s work, and the bill was not settled, and nobody had yet transferred the hull. The line would come back because Nilla said it would.

Deth watched Nilla leave and asked whether the splice was good. Corrin called it the best on the river.

That praise did not fix the deck. It did tell Deth what kind of person had refused the boat.

By late afternoon, the inventory was complete. The transfer could happen the next day. All that remained was for everyone involved to keep choosing what they had already said.

## Chapter 21: Before Witnesses

Nilla returned the line before the doors of the weigh-house opened.

It lay coiled at Corrin’s berth when I arrived. Nilla was already gone. Corrin lifted the coil, checked the splice once, and fitted it for the final load without comment.

The boat transfer was set for midday. Heth, whose name appeared on the town’s witness list, spent the morning checking the paper. A witness here did more than watch. Heth confirmed the people, the property, the price, the payment terms, and the fact that both parties spoke without being forced.

Heth inspected Deth’s money and found one coin from a place whose value was disputed. Deth said every town downriver accepted it. Heth said this town was not downriver from itself. The coin was replaced.

The price would be paid in two parts. Deth had the first on the scale table. The second would be due by the turn of the season. That later payment required Deth to make a promise after ownership passed.

I asked Heth what would happen if Deth did not pay. Heth said the promise would break while the debt continued to exist.

When I asked whether Corrin would recover the boat, Heth said, “Only if the paper said so. It doesn’t.”

The answer showed another difference I had once ignored. A broken promise made a fact visible. It did not reverse the world to the condition before the words.

Corrin finished the two booked loads before noon. One involved sacks of meal. The other was a crate of lamp fittings that the owner counted three times because the count changed whenever he became distracted. Corrin discharged both cargo promises and returned with the ridge briefly reduced to the old slab and three minor commitments.

Nilla came into the weigh-house after the second load. This time Nilla stood where Corrin could see. No repaired line provided a reason for being there.

People gathered without being invited. Some had business at the scales. Others developed business when they heard what would happen. Nobody called the transfer a ceremony, but the clear space around the scale table grew wider.

Deth brought the draft with the inventory attached. Corrin brought the boat record. Heth read both against each other and found a difference in the number of deck covers.

There were four.

Deth’s list said three. Corrin’s said five. Everyone went outside and counted. Four covers lay aboard. Corrin claimed one was being repaired. Nilla said no cover was in the workroom. Corrin remembered that the fifth had been cut up during the wet delivery.

The paper was corrected to four.

That small delay mattered to me. Nothing in the process allowed the moral size of the decision to excuse a wrong count. Deth was buying the actual boat, not the idea of Corrin’s sacrifice. If four covers existed, four went on the paper.

Back inside, Heth marked the correction and read the full inventory aloud. Deth agreed. Corrin agreed. Heth asked each whether the price and timing were understood. They were.

The first payment remained on the scale table. It belonged to Deth until the transfer words were spoken.

I watched Corrin’s large promise. It had survived years, Nilla’s refusals, twelve of my plans, and every arrangement made for the sale. The next sentence would not remove it. The next sentence would make removal impossible.

Heth looked once around the room, then began reading the final part of the paper.

## Chapter 22: The Thing That Did Not Leave

Heth read slowly enough that nobody could later claim to have heard a different transfer. The words named Corrin as seller, Deth as buyer, the boat by its record mark, the four deck covers, the attached inventory, the full price, and the payment dates. Heth paused after each part. Both people agreed.

Deth placed one grasper on the table beside the money. Corrin stood opposite. Nilla remained near the back wall, clear of the people who kept shifting crates through the room.

Heth asked Deth to state what he was buying. Deth named the hull and everything attached to the corrected list. Heth asked Corrin to state what was being sold. Corrin named the same property.

Then Corrin spoke the final transfer words. The money changed sides. The boat belonged to Deth.

A new promise object appeared between Deth’s graspers for the unpaid part of the price. It was narrow, longer than a hand, and entirely ordinary. Deth placed it on the ridge beside the two pieces already there. Nothing appeared for Corrin because selling was an act completed in that moment, not a future undertaking. The old slab on Corrin’s back remained.

I had known it would. Corrin and Nilla had told me. The rules had been tested from every direction. Still, I watched for a change. No edge cracked. No color shifted. The object did not grow heavier in any way I could see.

Its meaning changed because the boat was gone. Before the transfer, Corrin could still give the hull to Nilla, however harmful that would be. After the transfer, Corrin no longer owned the promised thing. The remaining object recorded words that had become impossible to fulfill by Corrin’s choice.

People in the room noticed. A few looked at the ridge and then at Corrin. Nobody moved closer to read the private text.

Heth counted the money once more and passed it across. Corrin put the coins into a locked box prepared for that purpose. Deth asked about the stern pump before the box lid had closed.

“You own the pump now,” Corrin said.

“I own the problem. You still know the answer.”

Corrin explained which sound meant the valve had caught grit. The transfer did not erase useful knowledge or require Corrin to become unkind.

A crate blocked the scale table and had to be moved. Two workers argued about where. Heth filed the paper in a box of other boat transfers. The clear space around the table disappeared as ordinary business reclaimed it.

Nilla had not moved from the back wall. Corrin went over. Deth stayed with the witness paper. I remained near the scales, close enough to hear if they chose ordinary voices and far enough that a lower voice would stay theirs.

“It’s done,” Corrin said.

Nilla looked at the large slab. “No. That part isn’t.”

“No.” They stood together while a clerk called another weight.

I had once thought a correct choice should remove the problem. Corrin’s choice had protected Nilla from unwanted ownership and from years of waiting under visible obligation. It had also cost Corrin the boat and made a promise permanently false. Nothing in the room converted those losses into a clean result.

Deth called Corrin over to dispute the location of the spare pole. Corrin said it was on the inventory. Deth said the inventory did not say where.

Corrin went to show him, and the broken promise went too.

## Chapter 23: What It Was For

Deth required ten minutes to locate the spare pole in the place Corrin had already named. During those ten minutes, Nilla waited beside the scale table.

When Corrin returned, Nilla asked why the sale had needed to happen now. Corrin could have kept working the boat. No deadline existed in the promise. The hull had years left. Carrying the slab did not seem to slow Corrin, and the town had never treated it as shameful while it remained unfinished.

Nilla said Corrin could have continued. Corrin agreed. Nilla asked why fulfillment had to be made impossible.

Corrin looked toward the river doors. Deth stood aboard the boat that was now his, moving the spare pole to a location he considered more sensible. “Because I’d still be waiting in your head,” Corrin said.

Nilla’s graspers tightened on the edge of the scale table. Corrin explained without making the explanation a defense. Every year the boat remained available, Nilla would see the promise and wonder whether refusing again was required. Each new repair in the workroom would sit beside the possibility that Corrin still expected another life. Corrin could say no expectation remained, but the slab would continue to show an unfinished act.

Corrin said Nilla would begin paying each time the carried object came into view. Nilla said Corrin was paying instead. Corrin agreed.

Nilla looked directly at the broken object and said everyone could see. Corrin answered that they could see one promise had been broken. When Nilla pointed out that they would not know why, Corrin said they did not need the rest.

I thought of Merra’s tiny chip and the story I had refused. Visibility could establish a fact without giving the town ownership of its details.

Nilla asked the question I had been avoiding because I knew it belonged to Nilla. “Was the first promise a mistake?”

Corrin answered at once. “No.”

When Nilla was eleven, the boat had not been mainly a hull or route. It had been a statement that something would still exist after illness and loss finished taking things away. Corrin could not promise which adults would recover or what home would remain. Corrin could promise one future object, one place in the working river, one next thing.

“You needed someone to say there was an after,” Corrin said. “I said one I could make solid.”

Nilla looked down at the floor and, after a while, said the promise had worked. Corrin said, “I know.”

Nilla said that did not make the present part easier. Corrin agreed.

The promise had succeeded before it failed. That was difficult for me because I preferred acts to keep one moral condition. Kept or broken. Helpful or harmful. Right or wrong. Corrin’s slab carried all of those states across time.

A worker asked Heth to clear the scale table. Nilla and Corrin moved aside together.

Nilla said the boat had been good. Corrin agreed, then added that Deth would ruin the stern within three years. Deth shouted from outside that he could hear.

“Then listen about the pump,” Corrin shouted back.

Nilla laughed once. It did not settle anything. It did let the next breath happen.

They left the weigh-house together. At the street corner, Nilla turned toward the rope shop and Corrin turned toward the landing where the boat had been. Neither asked the other to turn too.

## Chapter 24: The Empty Berth

Corrin stood at the empty berth for seven minutes. I know because I counted. The boat had moved to the lower landing under Deth’s ownership while he prepared to leave the next morning. Corrin’s old mooring lines were gone. Two wet marks on the post showed where they had rested.

I stayed above the next post and did not ask what Corrin was thinking.

After seven minutes, a warehouse owner came down the steps and asked whether Corrin wanted work. A grain boat needed a second operator for the upriver run. The owner had heard about the sale before the paper reached its box.

Corrin asked about pay, route, sleeping space, and who held command. The answers produced complaints. The boat was larger, the pay was acceptable, the sleeping space leaked, and the owner gave too many instructions from shore.

“When does it leave?” Corrin asked.

“Day after tomorrow, before light.”

Corrin agreed to one run. A new promise appeared, small enough to sit in front of the broken slab.

That was how Corrin left the empty berth: carrying permanent evidence of one impossible choice and a fresh undertaking for ordinary work.

We walked to the weigh-house. At the back, the old crossing promise sat on its shelf. I looked from it to Corrin’s ridge. One object remained because a condition had not been met before the speaker died. The other remained because Corrin had deliberately made fulfillment impossible. They were not the same failure.

The old speaker had helped close dangerous routes, admitted the damage, and promised restoration after safety. I did not know whether later people had tried enough. Corrin had protected Nilla by breaking a promise that had once protected Nilla another way. I knew the local facts and still could not make the break clean.

The objects did share one truth. A necessary or defensible act did not erase what it cost.

I had wanted the ancient undertaking to clear the closers because people were dying, or condemn them because families were divided. I had wanted Corrin either to keep the word or admit that the earlier promise was wrong. Both demands required time to hold still. It did not.

People changed. Conditions failed to arrive. A sentence that saved someone at eleven could burden the same person at seventeen. A barrier that stopped deaths could become the reason generations never reached home. The earlier good did not cancel the later harm. The later harm did not prove the earlier choice had been careless.

Pip entered with an errand during this thought. He went to the clerk, waited for a figure on a paper to be corrected, and watched his promise disappear. His life offered a clean example because his aunt gave him tasks that could be completed before lunch. I envied Pip for nearly a second.

Then Pip noticed Corrin’s ridge. He stared at the large object, which now carried the visible condition of a break.

“That one went bad,” Pip said.

“It did,” Corrin answered.

Pip considered asking more. I could see the question arrive. Then he looked at Corrin’s face, decided against it, and ran out through the doors.

Nobody had taught him privacy in that moment. Perhaps his aunt had taught him before. Perhaps Corrin’s answer had been enough. I did not know, and I let the fact stay small.

Corrin asked whether I wanted to see the larger boat. We went upriver to inspect the vessel Corrin had already promised to join. It was broad, high-sided, and badly arranged. Corrin disliked it with growing energy.

By the time we reached the bow, Corrin had found nine faults and accepted the coming run.

The broken promise remained on the ridge. The new one would vanish when Corrin boarded before light. Neither object needed to pretend the other was not there.

# Part V: Not Yet, Then Go

## Chapter 25: Everybody Sees

The first person to mention Corrin’s broken promise was the clerk who had argued about wet grain. Corrin came into the weigh-house the morning after the sale to settle a route payment. The clerk looked up, saw the changed slab, and stopped halfway through writing a number.

The clerk said the promise had broken. Corrin agreed. Then the clerk looked at the payment paper and said the unloading fee belonged in another column. That was the whole exchange.

For the hours that followed, I stayed near Corrin to learn what public failure did in a town where nobody could conceal it.

Four people noticed. One was a boat owner who had known Corrin for years. He asked whether the break had happened with the sale. Corrin said it had. The owner nodded, then wanted advice about a rudder pin.

A woman carrying rolled canvas saw it while passing on the middle landing. She made a sympathetic sound. Corrin told her not to make that sound. She changed it to a rude one, which Corrin preferred.

Venn saw the slab while hauling his cart. He had seventeen promises that day, according to him, though I counted sixteen. Venn asked whether the break was bad. Corrin called it bad enough.

Venn did not ask for more. He needed help turning the cart, and Corrin helped without making a promise because the work began immediately.

The fourth person was the north-landing manager, who carried no promise at all that morning. She looked at the slab, looked at Corrin, and said nothing. Later she offered Corrin two days of work after the grain run. Corrin refused because the schedule overlapped another job not yet promised.

Nobody came close enough to read the broken words.

That was not because people failed to care. The woman with the canvas changed her manner. Venn chose a gentler question than usual. The manager offered work. Each response proved attention. They simply did not treat visibility as permission.

I asked Corrin whether seeing and not asking was normal. Corrin checked the payment paper. “If someone wants you to know, they tell you.”

I pointed out that the object told part of the story. “Then you know that part,” Corrin said.

This world had placed promises where everyone could see them and built a custom around not taking more than the sight offered. I had expected the opposite. A visible moral record should have produced trials in every street. Instead it produced short acknowledgment, practical response, and gossip carefully aimed at other matters.

There was gossip, of course. The price of the boat had already grown in both directions. By noon, Corrin had sold it for too little, too much, or exactly the wrong amount. One person said Deth had cheated. Another said Corrin had cheated Deth. A third believed the stern was involved, which was true in the sense that the stern was always involved.

None of them claimed to know the promise’s words.

Nilla crossed the weigh-house floor during one of these discussions. The speakers stopped talking about the price. Nilla looked at them.

“You can keep going,” Nilla said. They did, but the numbers became less dramatic.

I followed Nilla toward the workroom and asked whether the attention was difficult. After one street, Nilla said, “Less difficult than waiting for Corrin to decide.”

That was not relief. It was a comparison between two costs. Nilla did not smile, and I did not tell the reader beside me that the choice had fixed everything, because you have seen enough to know it had not.

At the workroom door, a customer waited with a damaged line. Nilla inspected it, named a price, and refused a request for same-day repair because two earlier jobs came first.

No promise appeared until the customer accepted tomorrow and Nilla accepted tomorrow. Then a small gray object formed in Nilla’s graspers and went onto the ridge.

The town could see that one too. It did not belong to them either.

## Chapter 26: The Worse Boat

Corrin boarded the grain boat before light two mornings after the sale.

The small work promise vanished when both rear feet reached the deck. The broken slab stayed. Nobody aboard remarked on either event because the owner was explaining the loading order too loudly.

The boat was called the Wide Wake. Its name was accurate and not complimentary. It had a broad deck, a square cabin, and enough free space to make every useful object difficult to reach. Corrin had spent the previous day finding nine faults. By departure, the count had risen to fourteen.

I came for the first part of the run. The owner allowed this after establishing that I weighed nothing and therefore would not affect cargo calculations.

Corrin’s job was to manage lines and landings while another operator handled steering. On Corrin’s own boat, those tasks had belonged to one practiced body. Here every action required speech. The steering operator called for a line. Corrin called back that the line was already set. The owner called both of them wrong from shore until we moved beyond shouting distance.

Once the Landings began to shrink behind us, I asked whether the new job was better. Corrin called it quieter.

The first stop was a riverside storehouse half a morning upriver. The landing there sat high after the water dropped, so cargo had to climb a steep ramp. Corrin reorganized the unloading order without asking the owner. Heavy sacks came off first to lower the deck, then crates, then the lighter bags needed at the top.

The other operator objected that this was not the listed order. Corrin said the list had been made at level water.

When the operator said the owner wanted crates first, Corrin said, “The owner is not carrying them up that ramp.”

The sacks went first.

Corrin did not become less capable by working on somebody else’s hull. The route knowledge, load judgment, and habit of finishing things came aboard without a transfer paper. Ownership had given Corrin authority over the old boat, but the work itself lived elsewhere.

At the storehouse, a worker noticed the broken promise and stared long enough for Corrin to ask whether something was needed. The worker said no and took the next sack.

The moment passed. Being known for a break did not prevent Corrin from being trusted with the line, the load, or the changing river depth. It added a fact. It did not become every fact.

We returned toward the Landings in the afternoon with an empty deck and a cross-current. The Wide Wake handled badly when light. Its stern moved sideways each time the river turned. Corrin complained that the rudder belonged on a barn door.

The steering operator said Corrin was free to swim home. Corrin answered that swimming was not an option.

“Then admire the rudder.” Corrin admired it by naming three repairs it needed.

As the town came back into view, I asked whether Corrin missed the old boat. Corrin looked at the water rather than at Deth’s berth and said yes. No joke followed. Corrin had chosen the sale and missed the thing sold. One fact did not weaken the other.

At the middle landing, the owner stood waiting with a list of complaints. Corrin had a list too. They began at the same time and stopped only when a cart needed to pass between them.

The day’s work was finished. Corrin had earned pay, learned the Wide Wake’s worst habit, and promised another run the following week. A fresh object appeared for that future work. It went in front of the broken promise. Corrin loaded a borrowed coat between them and walked toward supper.

## Chapter 27: A Clean Join

Nilla let me watch a splice she did not like.

This was different from letting me watch a good splice. A good one moved steadily from first measurement to final trimming, and the shape of the rope seemed to know where it was going before I did. The bad one looked finished for nearly an hour. Nilla kept it beside the bench and completed three smaller jobs while finding reasons to pass it.

The rope belonged to a mill upriver. Its end had worn thin where it ran through a pulley, and Nilla had joined new line below the damaged section. The work looked sound to me. I said so after the fourth pass.

Nilla turned the rope over with both front graspers. “It looks sound to the side that wants to be seen.”

The underside showed a slight ridge where one tucked strand sat above the others. I had missed it because the flaw was under the rope and because I was beginning to understand that rope makers had trained the rest of the town to distrust confidence.

Nilla cut the binding and opened the splice. Nothing required that choice. The mill worker had already approved the quoted price. The repaired section would have held for ordinary use, according to Nilla, though it might have rubbed unevenly against the pulley. Sending it out would have ended the job before midday. Starting again cost new cord, part of the joined section, and most of the afternoon.

I asked whether that made it a broken promise. “No. I promised it tomorrow.” Nilla separated the strands and laid them across the bench. “If I send that tomorrow, the promise will go away.”

“Even with the ridge?” The mill had asked for a working rope, Nilla said, rather than a rope Nilla respected. I waited for the difference to finish arriving. Nilla picked up the knife. “I asked for one I respect.”

That was not a promise and would never become visible. It still took the afternoon.

I held no strands and carried no tools. Nilla no longer offered me either kind of help. Instead I read measurements from the wall marks and watched the loose ends when they crossed behind the bench. The work used what I could do without pretending it was what another body could do.

Near the second hour, Deth’s boat passed the shop window on its first downriver run. The paint had not changed, but two of the cargo covers were stretched differently and the forward pole sat on the other side of the cabin. Nilla saw it because everybody in the room saw it. The apprentice nearest the window lowered their eyes to the cord basket.

Nilla said the stern was moving sideways. It was. Deth corrected late, and the boat entered the lower channel at an angle before straightening.

I mentioned Corrin's claim that the boat always did that when light. Nilla tightened the first tuck and said Corrin had spoken often about the stern, usually while making it worse.

The apprentice laughed, then stopped as if laughter might be a question. Nilla did not rescue anyone from the silence. Nilla also did not leave the window.

Deth’s boat passed beyond sight. The shop returned to the sounds of cord drawing tight, knives touching wood, and the old floor answering weight. Nilla worked the new strands into the old with enough force to move the bench. Each tuck changed which end had to cross next. None could be hurried because the next one depended on the last lying flat.

I asked whether seeing the boat had made the day harder. Nilla said yes. I had learned not to improve an honest answer by adding a more hopeful question.

At closing time, the splice lay straight on every side. Nilla rolled the rope across the bench, pressed along the join, and pulled it through a wooden gauge twice. The raised place did not return.

The mill’s promise remained on Nilla’s ridge. It would stay until delivery tomorrow. The old promise was no longer there. Its absence did not leave the ridge empty, and it did not make the hours before the sale imaginary. Nilla had been helped by it, burdened by it, and released from receiving the boat through an act Nilla had not chosen. All three things had to fit in the same history.

Nilla hung the finished rope on the delivery peg and said, “That one I’ll send.” Outside, the river carried Deth and the boat toward whatever landing came next. Inside, Nilla swept the cut strands from the bench and kept the clean join.

## Chapter 28: Worth Seeing

I meant to leave the following morning.

Corrin learned this at supper and said the market boat would arrive before noon. I asked what that had to do with my departure. Corrin said it was worth seeing. It was not necessary, and it would almost certainly teach me nothing about the crossings.

This was a more effective invitation than a claim of importance would have been. I moved my departure to the afternoon.

The market boat arrived late, which Corrin said was one of the traditions that made it worth seeing. It came around the upriver bend with painted boards rising along both sides and strips of colored cloth tied above the cabin. People were already waiting at the middle landing with baskets, lists, and the particular impatience produced by an event that happens on a schedule nobody trusts.

The boat carried tools, dried fruit, lamp oil, paper, three cages of small animals, and a seller who announced each object as if the river had invented it during the trip. The deck opened into narrow aisles. Buyers climbed aboard from one side, were carried by the crowd past everything they did not want, and came off the other side holding several things they had not planned to buy.

Corrin took me aboard. Floating above the crowd would have been easier, but Corrin said the aisles were the point. I lowered myself until my feet almost touched the deck and moved at the speed of the bodies around me.

At the tool rack, Corrin examined four nearly identical gauges for measuring rope thickness. I asked whether one was needed. Corrin said no, then examined them again in case need had developed during the conversation.

We passed jars of preserved river greens, folded weather cloth, and metal hooks shaped for bodies with front graspers. Many objects had no use for me. That did not make them uninteresting. A life is partly the collection of problems its tools assume.

The animal cages held round, six-legged creatures that slept through the seller's full explanation of their liveliness. One woke when Corrin leaned close and made a noise like a wet hinge. Corrin made the same noise back. The animal went to the rear of its cage and refused further discussion.

I told Corrin that had been worth seeing.

Corrin bought a packet of dried fruit from a stall at the stern. Before taking payment, the seller promised to prove the fruit had been packed that morning by opening it. A small object appeared and vanished when the seller pulled back the wrapping to show inner paper still damp from washing.

The seller claimed the wet paper proved recent packing. Corrin said it proved recent water, but paid.

We ate beside the lower landing after the boat pulled away. I could not eat the fruit, though Corrin held up each kind for inspection and supplied opinions that became less reliable as the packet emptied. A pale strip was called excellent. A darker piece was called evidence. The smallest red one required silence and then another red one.

No question about Corrin's broken promise arose. People had looked at it in the market aisles. A few had looked away too quickly. Corrin had compared gauges, argued about damp paper, and frightened an animal while carrying the visible result of the sale. The morning had made no public lesson from that fact.

I asked why Corrin had wanted me to see the boat. Corrin folded the empty paper into a narrow square. “You were here when it came.”

That did not explain why I had needed to stay. It explained why the market boat could be offered without a lie. I understood the difference and did not make Corrin provide a better reason.

The afternoon current was still suitable for traveling to the crossing. I could have left then. Corrin remembered that quarterly weighing began the next morning and said I had never seen the whole town complain in numerical order.

I had seen enough of the weigh-house to know this was unlikely to improve my understanding of anything. I also knew exactly what Corrin was doing.

I asked whether the weighing was worth seeing. Corrin said, “No. It is very boring.” I stayed to see it.

## Chapter 29: No Better Reason

Quarterly weighing began with three scales disagreeing.

The disagreement was small enough to be useless and large enough to require a committee. At the weigh-house, two clerks moved the same test block from one platform to another while owners of carts and boats formed lines behind painted floor marks. Every person believed the other line was faster. They proved this by changing lines until both stopped.

Corrin had to be weighed for work records. The fresh promise for the Wide Wake was removed first and set on a numbered shelf. The borrowed coat followed. The broken promise could not be removed. The clerk entered its weight with Corrin's body because there was nowhere else for it to go.

I watched from above the doorway where I obstructed nobody and weighed nothing. The clerk still asked me to move twice. Procedure had become stronger than observation.

When Corrin reached the platform, the left scale gave one number and the center scale gave another. Corrin suggested using the lower number for wages and the higher one when lifting cargo. The clerk did not laugh because eleven people had made the same suggestion.

The test block traveled again. A shim went under one corner. Dust was blamed, then damp, then the people standing too close. Everyone moved back. The readings remained different.

I reminded Corrin that the weighing had been described as boring. Corrin said, “I did not say uncomplicated.”

By midday, the committee selected the center scale because it had been wrong by the same amount twice. Corrin was weighed, the work object and coat were returned, and the line advanced one body. The town's complaints did arrive in numerical order, though some repeated.

Outside, Corrin remembered a parcel that needed to go to the north landing. We carried it together, meaning Corrin carried it and I kept rain from reaching the top by spreading myself above it. There was no rain. Corrin said preparation explained that success.

At the north landing, the parcel's intended receiver had gone to the lower storehouse. We crossed town and found the receiver had returned by another street. Corrin called this worth seeing too. I said the standard had changed during my visit.

The parcel finally reached a cooper whose ridge held no room for one more object. The cooper opened the wrapping, counted six metal bands, and fulfilled Corrin's delivery promise. Its gray weight vanished before the empty cloth was folded.

Only the broken slab remained visible above the coat. We walked back toward the river by the long street. Corrin rejected the short street because a cart had blocked it that morning. The cart was gone when we passed the far end. Neither of us investigated whether this had been known.

At the lower landing, the afternoon current moved cleanly toward the old crossing. Light lay on the river in long white breaks. The route was open, the weather was settled, and I had finished every useful question I knew how to ask.

I said I would leave at first light. Corrin looked along the landing instead of at me. A crew was stacking empty baskets near the water. Someone called a count, someone else corrected it, and both began again.

“You may as well stay,” Corrin said. There was no market boat in that sentence, no public weighing, and no parcel that required two trips across town. Corrin had removed every practical reason and left the request standing by itself.

No promise appeared. I could leave at dawn without breaking anything visible. Corrin could ask without creating a claim over the answer.

I wanted to stay. I also wanted to continue. The two wants did not cancel each other, and naming one would not make the other false. This world had been teaching me that lesson with larger objects.

I offered to stay until first light. Corrin nodded once and called that a poor definition of staying. I said it was the amount I could give. “Then it will have to be badly named.”

We remained at the landing while the basket count failed for the third time. Corrin did not turn the request into a joke or ask me to alter the answer. I did not explain that companionship could matter to someone carrying a permanent public failure. That was my inference, and using it aloud would have made it Corrin's confession.

After dark, we walked back through the town. The weigh-house lamps showed the shelves through the front windows. Old promises and new ones waited under their labels. Above the roof, the route to the crossing was a darker line between the stars.

At the guest room, Corrin said the Wide Wake departed shortly after dawn. I said that sounded worth missing. Corrin agreed, and said good night.

## Chapter 30: First Light

First light lasted four minutes.

For most of the night, the sky above the river was black and the river was only a quieter kind of black beneath it. Then a narrow color appeared behind the eastern roofs, pale as the inside of a shell. It touched the water, the landing posts, and the upper windows of the weigh-house. Four minutes later, ordinary gray morning replaced it.

I was waiting at the north landing when Corrin arrived.

Corrin had packed the borrowed coat, food for the day, and two small tools the Wide Wake's owner had failed to provide. The ridge carried the next work promise, three unfinished errands, a promise to return the coat, and the broken slab. The arrangement was balanced. Corrin had apparently begun the morning by making failure orderly.

“You missed the good light,” I said.

“I saw enough to know it would end.”

The Wide Wake waited at the middle landing downstream. Its owner was already calling instructions that could not be followed from where Corrin stood. Corrin listened until the list reached five, then looked at me.

There were several possible farewell promises. I could have said I would return. Corrin could have promised to wait, remember, explain, or improve the boat before I saw it again. Any of those sentences might have become weight. Neither of us used one.

Corrin asked whether I knew the way to the crossing.

I said I did. The route followed the north bank until a split stone, then crossed a strip of reed water to the old marker. Beyond it, the air changed and the next world began. I had walked the route once with Corrin and repeated it twice after that because Corrin believed directions improved through repetition.

“They improve through survival,” Corrin said.

“That is a different measurement.”

“It is the useful one.”

We stood together while the Landings woke. A bakery cart rattled over the street boards. Two crews unfastened covers from boats at the lower berths. At the rope shop, someone opened the shutters and shook yesterday's dust cloth into the river before remembering that the river moved toward the neighbors.

The weigh-house door opened. Through it I could see the front shelves and, farther back, the locked room where the old promise remained. It held an undertaking made by people who had understood the danger of the crossings and closed them anyway. They had prevented more deaths, stranded others, and left later lives inside a condition they could not solve. The object still waited because safety had never become true.

I had wanted its existence to identify a wrong person or a correct answer. It had done neither. It had shown me that a necessary action could leave an unpaid cost, and that admitting the cost did not undo the action. Corrin's smaller broken promise had taught the same fact at the size of one family.

Neither lesson made breaking a promise good. It made the judgment longer.

The owner of the Wide Wake shouted Corrin's name from downstream.

Corrin turned toward the sound, then stopped. “You may still stay badly.”

“I already did.”

That answer seemed to satisfy something, though I did not ask what. Corrin touched one front grasper to the landing post, a gesture I had seen workers use when leaving a berth they knew. I lowered close enough for the edge of my body to meet it. There was no weight in the contact. It was contact anyway.

Corrin left for the middle landing without looking back. I watched because refusing to look back does not require the other person to do the same.

At the Wide Wake, the owner pointed to a stack of grain sacks and began another list. Corrin climbed aboard, shifted the forward line, and moved two sacks before answering. The deck settled lower at one side. The fresh work object stayed at the front of the ridge. The permanent break remained behind it, with the other promises and the borrowed coat arranged between.

Nilla opened the rope-shop door across the street. From that distance I could not tell whether Nilla watched Corrin board. I could tell that Nilla carried a coil to the outside rack and tested its tag before hanging it. I kept the difference between those facts.

I went north.

The path left the working streets and followed the river through low reeds. Behind me came the sounds of ramps dropping, ropes tightening, and people revising plans made the day before. No bell marked my departure. The town had cargo to move.

At the split stone, I found the narrow branch of water and followed it to the old marker. The crossing looked like heat above cold ground, though the morning air was still. On its far side, no river continued. That was the only proof it offered.

Downstream, the Wide Wake pushed away from the middle landing. It was a worse boat than the one Corrin had sold. Corrin stood on its deck under a full ridge, capable, burdened, and plainly occupied. The stern slid sideways as the current took it.

Corrin's voice reached me across the reeds, explaining to the owner exactly what was wrong with the rudder.

I crossed while the complaint was still carrying over the water.
