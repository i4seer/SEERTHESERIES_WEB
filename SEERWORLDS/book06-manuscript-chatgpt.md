# Part I: Ways in the Air

## Chapter 1: No Down

I arrived with no stone under me.

This should not have surprised me. I do not stand on things unless I want to, and I have crossed into worlds in stranger positions than that. Still, light opened on every side, my attention dropped toward the place where ground ought to be, and for several seconds I believed I was falling.

I checked.

The pale glare below did not come closer. The blue above did not pull away. Nothing rushed past me, and there was no wind from motion. I chose a point in the brightness and held myself against it. I had not moved at all.

That improved the situation without making it sensible.

You probably know where down is where you live. Even if you close your eyes, your body tells you. Mine does not. I have no weight, no feet, and no inner feeling that chooses one direction as the one objects should drop toward. Usually a world supplies the answer for me. A floor helps. A hill is even better. This place offered light in one direction and more light in the other.

Then I turned and found the world beside me.

A pale stone span crossed the open air. It was broad enough in places to carry fields, though there were no fields on the part nearest me. Its upper surface had been rounded by weather. Both ends ran beyond sight. Another span passed high above it at a slant, and a third climbed almost upright behind that one.

Once my attention learned the shape, the empty air filled with stone.

Spans crossed without touching. Some curved into loops. Two ran close together, separated by a gap narrow enough for me to occupy and wide enough to matter to anybody who needed support. A thin one plunged into the white haze below. Far above, several thicker runs gathered into a knot that darkened the sky with crossings.

There were dozens within easy sight. There were hundreds farther out. I started counting, lost three behind a broad curve, began again from a different point, and quit at one hundred and twelve because the number explained nothing.

I moved toward the nearest span and stopped a small distance from its side. The stone looked old. Fine cracks held pale dust. Heat lay strongest on the face turned toward the clear blue, while the shaded underside was cooler. Those facts suggested sun, weather, and time. They did not suggest what supported several mountains' worth of rock in open air.

I went below the span to look for columns. There were none. I followed its underside until the haze softened every distant edge, but the rock did not widen into a foundation. When I rose above it, the same span simply continued, level for a while and then bending away.

I tried the question another way. If the span fell, where would it go?

I traveled downward. I passed a narrow rib, then a pair of crossed ones, then a great shelf whose lower face carried dark streaks from old rain. The light thickened around me until distance became hard to judge. No ground appeared. I kept going long enough to become aware that I wanted to turn back.

That feeling was useful evidence. It was not evidence of a bottom.

I rose faster than I had descended. I am telling you that because I would like to claim I returned only to continue my survey from clearer air. The truth is that an endless bright depth bothered me. A dark depth would have seemed honest about what it was doing.

Back among the higher spans, I placed myself near the warmed side of the first one and looked across the lattice again. This was the sixth world I could put in order. I had stopped congratulating myself about that. Every time I believed I understood how worlds behaved, one of them produced a simple fact that made the word *understood* look ambitious.

Lately I had also begun thinking about crossings. I had used them for as long as I could remember without asking why one world ended where another began, or whether those beginnings had been arranged. That question was already with me when I arrived, which may be why the unsupported stone held my attention so completely.

Something had made a world out of ways with no ground between them.

I kept trying to ask what held those ways up. The question failed each time, because *up* belonged to the same arrangement I was trying to explain. There was no hidden land beneath the spans, no floor waiting below the haze, no single direction from which the whole construction rose.

The stone did not stand up from anything.

It only went on.

## Chapter 2: A Place to Put Yourself

The stone had shallow hollows worn across its warm side. They repeated at regular distances, though not regularly enough to be tool marks. Something had passed that way many times and pressed the same parts of the surface. I had just decided the marks belonged to weather when the weather came around the bend.

The creature was low, pale, and much longer than I was wide. Hard sections formed its body, each joined to the next by a dark flexible crease. Limbs ran along both sides. They did not all lift together. A wave traveled down them instead: grip, pull, release, then the next pair. The motion carried the whole body over the sloping stone without haste or waste.

I moved away from the surface to let it pass. It went by me, then slowed. Dark sense organs clustered at both ends. I looked for a mouth and found no useful difference between front and back. One end pointed along the route of travel, so I chose that as the front. This system lasted until the creature reversed without turning. The limb wave ran the other direction, and the end I had called the back led it toward me. I removed both labels from my notes.

“You missed,” it said. The sound came from near the middle of its body, though I could find no opening there.

I asked what I had missed.

“The rib.”

“I meant to move away from it.”

The body curved until both ends faced me. I had the uncomfortable sense of being inspected by a committee consisting of one member. “There isn't anything under you.”

I pointed out that it had been traveling on the side of the stone, with nothing under its body either.

Several limbs tightened their grip. “The side is something.”

That was true, but it did not settle the matter. I moved a short distance into the air and stopped. The resident watched with both sets of organs while the rest of its body stayed fixed. I explained that I could remain there and suggested it could cross a gap the same way.

“I could leave the stone,” it said. “Crossing would last a moment. Falling would use the rest of my life.”

I asked whether it knew the fall had an end. It had never tested the question. I told it about my descent into the haze and how I had found neither floor nor change. The creature bent farther around. If those dark clusters could express judgment, both were doing it.

When I returned to the rib, its broad pads settled into the worn hollows. Fine ridges beneath them matched the marks I had mistaken for weather. People like this one had made the shallow pattern by traveling the same route over years. I wanted to ask how the grip worked, whether the hard sections were added with age, and what decided which end led. Before I chose a question, the resident set off.

It traveled perhaps thirty body-lengths, reached the edge, and vanished. I went after it, expecting to see a pale body dropping through the brightness. Instead, it had wrapped around the lip and continued along the opposite face with the same easy ripple of limbs. Its body hung between rock and open depth, held by pads alone, and apparently that counted as sensible.

“You are underneath the rib,” I called.

The voice answered without slowing. “A rib has faces. It doesn't have an underneath.”

“We were above this face a moment ago.”

“Then we were on the other one.”

I followed through the air while the resident crossed the stone. From where I floated, it looked upside down. From where it gripped, I probably looked like an object that had neglected to finish falling. Each of us had a sound reason for finding the other alarming.

At a groove, it stopped and seated several pads more deeply before asking whether I was headed anywhere.

“I don't know where anything is yet.”

“That makes arrival difficult.”

“Only until I see a place.”

A low sound moved through the creature's middle. It might have been amusement, or concern, or a private decision that I would not survive the day. With no face to read, I could not tell. It continued around the stone and disappeared beyond the curve.

I stayed by the groove. The hollows now meant traffic. The grip marks meant repeated journeys. One passing resident meant homes, work, and destinations hidden among the spans. The lattice was not merely a collection of ways. Somebody needed those ways to lead somewhere.

## Chapter 3: The Near Place

Finding the settlement was easy once I stopped searching for a floor. High to my left, several ribs passed close enough to cast broken shadows across one another. Frames occupied the spaces between them. Some sat on the sunward surfaces; others hung beneath the stone on braces. Curved walkways linked one face to another. Lines of green followed the brightest slopes, and shallow dishes held dark water where the rock leveled.

People moved through all of it. Pale bodies traveled along the ribs, around their sides, and down the loops between them. Something heavy rose in a sling below the largest span while four workers called measures to one another. Even from where I hovered, the place had the particular noise made by many people who know their own tasks and assume everyone else is in the way.

I looked for the safest approach out of habit, then remembered that every approach was equally safe for me. The settlement sat less than two hundred body-lengths away in a clear line. I crossed the distance in about a minute.

I entered beside one of the water dishes and nearly placed myself in the path of a carrier. A warning came from somewhere in the carrier's middle. I rose, apologized, and stayed above the busiest surface while I learned the pattern. Traffic did not keep to the top. A broad body would vanish over an edge, travel upside down through shade, and return along a loop on another rib. Two residents heading toward the same visible frame often approached it from entirely different directions.

The resident from the outer span appeared below me with a long sling fastened across the center of its body. The sling bulged at each segment and made the creature look wider than before. I followed until it reached an open frame where other loads were being counted.

The resident noticed me while lowering the sling into a set of hooks. It asked where I had come from and gave the distance a long look when I pointed out the west rib, as though the span might have followed me. Then it asked which route I had taken.

“I came here.”

“That isn't a route.”

I pointed through the open side of the frame. The rib where we had met was still visible between two crossings. I described leaving it and moving straight through the air.

Both ends swung toward the view. The resident traced a much longer journey for me: down the first rib, across a lower run, through an opening near a bend, around the far side of another span, and back toward the settlement from below. It would take a large part of the morning even without the load.

I said the settlement was close because I had measured the distance.

“You measured the gap.” The resident adjusted one of the sling hooks. “The way is longer.”

I studied the ribs between our frame and the west. None formed a direct connection. Some crossed the line without joining it. One stopped short of the settlement's outer loop. Another did join, but only after curving far below and returning on the opposite side. If I had needed stone beneath me, the resident's morning-long path made sense.

I insisted that a direct line existed. The resident called it open air. To me, those descriptions meant the same thing.

The resident tightened the empty sling. “Not to me.”

I had arrived prepared to learn the arrangement of the spans. I thought that would tell me how the world connected. Instead, I had learned that connection belonged partly to the traveler. The visible space between two points measured one thing. The journey measured another.

The resident left to finish whatever work came after delivery. I spent the remaining light above the settlement, watching routes that seemed wasteful until I found the missing grip, narrow turn, or break that made them necessary. Residents stopped at crossings that looked simple. They backed out of slots that accepted shorter bodies. Loads that fit one curve failed at the next.

Near the center of the place, four ribs passed within reach of one another without touching. Traffic gathered there anyway. Workers had braced platforms across the smallest gaps and looped paths around the wider ones. The settlement existed because the stone nearly connected, and because generations of effort had made use of the word *nearly*.

As the light changed, I tried to draw the route from the west rib in my mind. I could see every important span. I knew the distance. I knew where I had started and where I had arrived. Yet I could not have told the resident which turns to take, because I had crossed none of them.

That bothered me more than I expected. I could reach a place without knowing the way there.

## Chapter 4: The Hide Chooses

Everyone in the settlement carried folded hide.

I noticed because they touched it constantly. A traveler reached a junction, drew a creased sheet from beneath a strap, checked one part, and put it away before choosing a turn. A worker lowering a water vessel stopped halfway around a hoop to consult another. Two young residents compared theirs on a sunny ledge, laughed at something I could not see, and left by opposite sides.

The behavior looked unnecessary. All the ribs were visible. If a bend disappeared behind stone, one could move far enough to see around it. I had already formed this opinion with the confidence available only to somebody who did not understand the problem.

The next morning, two residents blocked a busy crossing while disagreeing about a loaded sling. One said the sling would jam at an elbow. The other said it had passed there recently. Each had good reasons. Neither moved, and the traffic around them divided with the patience of water around a post.

One resident pulled out a sheet of worked hide and spread it against the rock. Dark lines covered the pale surface. Some crossed; some ended in small marks; others bent around blank spaces. I moved closer because at last the repeated checking made sense. These were maps.

Then the second resident took the hide.

The drawing changed.

There was no motion to follow. A line that had left the central mark toward one corner now departed toward the opposite edge. A narrow loop vanished. Two nearby junctions remained, but the connection between them took a different shape. I checked the hide itself for folds or layers. It was one sheet.

The argument continued. The second resident pointed to the new line as proof that the elbow was open. The first insisted it was not the same elbow. This produced a detailed account of an earlier attempt, several unkind facts about the second resident's judgment, and no concern at all about the map changing in front of them.

“The lines moved,” I said.

Four clusters of dark organs turned toward me.

The first resident asked whether I needed help.

“With what just happened.” I pointed at the hide. “That drawing was different when you held it.”

“Yes.”

The answer carried the careful patience of someone confirming that stone was hard. The second resident bent the sheet toward me, perhaps believing I needed a better view. I moved around it and asked both of them to exchange it again.

They did. The route altered back.

I asked the second resident to touch one edge while the first held the middle. The drawing remained the first resident's. They changed positions; still the same. When the first released it fully, three lines shifted and a symbol near the fold disappeared.

“It knows who owns it?” I asked.

“Nobody owns a map,” the second resident said. “It shows whoever has it.”

That answer created more questions than the crossing could hold. Traffic had begun pressing around us, and both residents still needed to settle the sling. They directed me toward a quieter frame and finished their argument by choosing a third route that pleased neither one.

At the frame, I watched other hides. A short resident unfolded a map, then allowed a much longer one to take it. The whole lower half redrew itself. A loaded carrier set down two containers and checked again; a tight curve appeared where there had been a detour. After the containers went back into the sling, the curve vanished.

The maps did not show the stone lattice as a fixed picture. They showed which parts of it a particular body could use at that moment.

A long body could stretch across a break that stopped a short one. A shorter body might fold through an opening where length became an obstacle. A wide sling could erase a narrow passage. Weakness in one side could remove a bridge even when the other side reached it. The stone stayed where it was. The drawing changed because the traveler changed.

I borrowed no map. Holding one would have taught me only what the hide thought I could do, and I suspected the answer might be every open gap in the world. Instead, I asked people to show me changes they considered ordinary. Most did, after asking how I had reached the settlement and deciding I was already stranger than the request.

By midday, I understood the hides well enough to stop calling them pictures of the world. A map here recorded an agreement between stone, body, load, and present light. Change any term and the route might change with it.

At the busy crossing, the two residents had finally gone. I unfolded the sequence in my memory: same hide, same ribs, different holder, different truth.

Neither map had been wrong.

## Chapter 5: Ilm in Changing Light

The settlement was called Ilm. People said the name as if I should already know it, which was fair. A place large enough to stop traffic in six directions has earned the right to expect some recognition.

Ilm was a knot. That word meant more than several ribs close together. It meant the frames built across their gaps, the curved links joining upper and lower faces, the water bowls, the trained green, and every old bracket left in the rock after its original use had ended. Stone made the opportunity. Work made the town.

I spent several days learning it badly.

On the bright upper faces, growers kept narrow lines of green wherever the light stayed longest. Water moved in covered vessels from the catching bowls because an open container became difficult the moment its carrier went under a span. In the shaded lower frames, old or unwell residents rested where the rising glow from the haze stayed gentle. Noise from the busy ribs reached them through the braces, softened but never gone.

The settlement wrapped around its stone so completely that *top* worked only as a local instruction. A market could occupy one face while homes hung from the other. A person leaving a frame might travel upward, around, and inward without ever changing the direction their pads faced. I continued to use above and below because I needed words. The residents continued to understand me through patience.

Loads taught me more than directions did.

A carrying sling fastened along the central body sections. Fill it, and the traveler became wider, heavier, and less able to fold. I watched one courier arrive with a deep sling that bulged on both sides. The courier released the cargo at a store frame, checked the hide, and left by a narrow inner curve that had not appeared on the incoming map.

The return trip was a different journey because the traveler was a different shape.

I followed another carrier toward a low opening. The map showed a route through it while the sling held only two flat packages. At the last moment, someone added a round container. One line vanished from the hide. The carrier complained, redistributed the other parcels, checked again, and accepted a longer route without treating the change as unfair. The map had not denied permission. It had reported fit.

Light changed the same way.

Near the end of the day, orange color moved across the stone and every long shadow grew. Residents all over Ilm paused. Hides came out along roads, loops, frames, and raw ribs. The stopping passed through the knot like a signal, though nobody had called one. Hundreds of travelers checked what the lower light had done to their ways.

Some routes remained safe in shadow. Others depended on seeing a narrow grip or judging a break. A path usable at midday could disappear before evening without any stone moving. Nobody argued with that either. They chose other turns, postponed a load, or settled into a resting frame until the light improved.

I positioned myself above the central knot and watched the maps open and close. No two sheets showed exactly the same agreement. A small resident beside a water bowl kept a tight loop that vanished from the map of the broad adult nearby. An old traveler with one side held stiff lost a crossing used by three younger ones. Two couriers carrying equal loads took separate paths because one could bend where the other could reach.

From the air, all of them seemed to be solving a needlessly complicated problem. I could see the destinations. I could cross every blank space. But I was beginning to understand that my view removed the very facts the maps preserved.

If you have never needed a route to hold you, a map can look like information. In Ilm it was closer to a promise. It said this body, carrying this load, in this light, could travel from here to there and remain in the world.

That was why people checked so often. Their lives changed by small amounts all day. A parcel was added. A joint became tired. Light moved. Rain left a face slick. The hides answered the body that existed now, not the one that had left home in the morning.

When the orange light faded, movement thinned. The upper surfaces cooled. Voices drifted from frames on both faces of the stone, and the last carriers made slower choices at the junctions.

I had liked Ilm before I understood any of this. It was busy without hiding its quiet places, and practical work showed everywhere. After I learned why the whole town stopped to consult itself at dusk, I liked it more.

I also stopped asking why people needed maps for ways they already knew.

## Chapter 6: Made Safe

The tapping began before the busiest light and continued until late afternoon.

Four workers occupied the north rib beyond Ilm. Each held a narrow tool against the stone while another tool struck it. Their bodies formed a spaced line, and the sound traveled along the rib in uneven beats. Dust gathered in the creases between their sections. Behind them, a fifth worker used a heavier cutter to remove the rounded crown from the stone.

I watched long enough to become an inconvenience.

The unfinished surface curved and sloped with every old change in the rib. The finished stretch carried a flat center. Small grip cuts repeated along both margins. Shallow grooves marked the outer edges, and braced resting frames appeared at measured distances. A traveler approaching at speed could see the worked strip long before reaching it.

One worker finally asked whether I intended to help.

“I cannot hold a tool.”

“Then you have selected watching correctly.”

I moved out of the dust and asked what they called the two surfaces. The worker indicated the untouched stone as a rib and the finished strip as a road. I had been using *rib* for every span since hearing it on my first day. I had assumed *road* meant the same thing when one wished to discuss travel.

“A road has been worked,” the worker said. “A rib is what we were given.”

The difference had required eleven years on that one stretch. The worker's mother had begun it. Some of the frames behind us had been rebuilt twice while the cutting continued forward. Nobody expected to finish quickly, and nobody seemed interested in my question about how the last worker knew the effort would be worth it. Enough people needed the route. That was the answer.

I traveled back toward Ilm along the finished surface. The flat center removed awkward tilts. The side cuts gave pads a sure place in poor weather. Resting frames allowed loads to be set down without blocking the route. A road was not merely a line between locations. It was years of people deciding that strangers should be able to trust the next stretch.

The largest collection of that trust sat beneath Ilm's central span.

The courier station hung in a broad lower frame where several useful routes met. Slings filled one rack. Blank worked hides filled another. A job board displayed destinations and weights. Couriers studied the board, consulted their maps, measured the size of available loads, and made calculations that involved their bodies as much as time.

Terro ran the station. Age had dulled the dark color at the joints, but nothing about Terro's movement was uncertain. While we spoke, Terro weighed a parcel, rejected the knot on its sling, showed a younger courier how to tie it again, and remembered my arrival from the west rib.

“You crossed the break without stone,” Terro said.

“There was no need for stone.”

“For you.” Terro checked the new knot and returned the parcel. “Useful condition.”

I could not tell whether that was praise. I asked what made someone a courier instead of any traveler already headed toward a destination. Terro pointed one end toward the board. A person might take a parcel because their route allowed the weight, because they could reach a junction others could not, or because the payment made a longer way worthwhile. None of that was the central distinction.

“I give somebody a thing another person needs,” Terro said. “Most days I cannot follow. I may never have stood at the receiving frame. What keeps the parcel from falling is the courier's body. What gets it there is a true map.”

“And if the courier already knows the route?”

“The load changes it. Light changes it. A tired joint changes it. Knowing yesterday is useful until today differs.”

Terro assigned the retied parcel and watched its carrier leave before adding, “If the map can be trusted, I can send work past the end of my own reach. If it cannot, this is a room full of straps.”

That sounded severe. Then I looked at the job board again. Food, messages, containers, and private packages waited beside destinations their owners might never reach. The station joined lives that the stone kept apart. Every assignment depended on a route Terro could not personally inspect.

Outside, the north-road tapping continued through the structure of Ilm. Somebody was cutting safety into bare stone one small hollow at a time. Inside, Terro sent a courier out on an unseen path because a hide said the body and load would fit.

The road required trust in work already done. The trade required trust in a line.

# Part II: The Unfinished Line

## Chapter 7: New Reach

Nib found me at the water bowls and announced a fact before offering a name. “I can take the high bend now.”

Nib had four hard sections, perhaps five if the small end piece counted, and every joint still carried the dark shine that age later dulled. The high bend rose from Ilm's upper knot at a steep angle. I had watched adults use it without difficulty. Nib pointed both ends toward it as if nobody else had noticed the structure.

Nib had done it twice without stopping in the middle. I offered congratulations. This was a mistake only because congratulations suggested the subject had ended.

Over the next hour I learned exactly where most young bodies paused, which shallow grip Nib no longer needed, how many residents had witnessed the second attempt, and why the first attempt also counted even though one end had touched the resting rail. Five adults later informed me, separately, that the bend was ordinary and Nib had discussed it for four days.

None of them said it where Nib could hear. Reachfolk did not grow continuously. Their bodies remained much the same for a time, then lengthened or added a section over several weeks. A familiar gap could become passable. A familiar slot could disappear. The maps adjusted before pride did.

When I asked about the old routes, Nib first claimed they were all unchanged, then admitted that a little curl beneath the water frame had disappeared. “I don't need it.”

The last sentence came too quickly. Nib had lost a route while gaining the high bend and was trying to decide which fact deserved more weight. I did not ask again. A body could improve by one measure and become less suited by another. The map kept both accounts.

I met Vess at the courier station that afternoon. Vess had six sections, quick pads, and joints still nearer Nib's shine than Terro's dust-gray. Terro rejected the weight Vess wanted to carry, Vess argued with good humor, and the parcel went to a broader courier. Vess took a smaller western job and appeared pleased to have lost the disagreement without losing time.

“You are the one who crosses gaps without noticing,” Vess said. I objected that I noticed them. Vess allowed that I might do so afterward.

Vess was one of the best couriers in the station. I learned that from work rather than praise. Vess checked every fastening, balanced loads across the body instead of merely fitting them into the sling, and studied the light before accepting a return time. Complaints continued through all of it: poor knots, customers who named homes instead of junctions, and one person in the lower quarter who apparently moved a receiving frame whenever Vess learned the route.

The complaints were cheerful. The precision was not performed for anyone. Vess had also finished a growth change about a month earlier. One joint sat at a different angle and the total length had increased. The map had already revised itself, but western work had been scarce, so Vess was still discovering what the change meant.

Vess had found two new routes. The useful one crossed the Fell gap and removed an hour from an eastern delivery. Before the growth, Vess had nearly reached the far lip. “Almost is not a route,” Vess said.

The second addition was a shelf beneath the market span. Vess could fold onto it now, though no destination occupied it and nothing useful had been stored there. Vess described the approach, the exact turn of the newer joint, and the amount of room left at the far edge.

I pointed out that the shelf improved no journey and held no work. Vess tightened the small western load. “Correct. But it is mine.”

Nib arrived in time to tell Vess about the high bend. Vess listened to the entire account, asked where Nib placed the third pair of pads, and said the middle was the difficult point. Nib left taller in every way available.

After Nib left, I asked whether the high bend was truly difficult. “Not for me,” Vess said. “It isn't my new way.”

That was my first useful lesson about growth on the Reach. A route did not need to shorten a working day or lead to anything valuable before it mattered. Sometimes a body changed, the world answered with one more place, and the place itself was enough.

## Chapter 8: Almost Is Not Across

Vess took an eastern parcel the next morning and let me follow to the Fell gap.

The route left Ilm along a worked surface, turned around the underside of a broad rib, and climbed through a frame where travelers could set down loads. Vess did not need the frame that day. The parcel was small, but Vess checked its straps there anyway. A loose load changed reach even when the weight stayed the same.

The Fell gap appeared after the next bend. Two ribs approached each other at different angles and failed to meet. The distance was not great. The useful grip on the far side, however, sat around the lower curve where a body on the near rib could barely see it.

Vess opened the map and placed it flat. The line crossed.

“Before the change?” I asked.

Vess showed me the old attempt. The leading end could reach the far stone, but the middle joint had lacked the new angle. Pressing forward would have peeled the rear pads from the near side before enough of the front held. The gap had always looked possible. That was what made it unhelpful.

Vess secured every pad except the forward pairs, lengthened across open air, and set the first grip around the hidden curve. The new joint turned. Weight moved from the near rib to the far one in a controlled sequence until the last pads released.

Nothing dramatic happened. Vess simply stood on the other side.

I had crossed beside Vess without touching either rib. For once, I did not mention the difference.

The shortcut removed an hour because the old route descended to a lower crossing and climbed back. We could see much of that detour from the Fell. Vess traced it in the air for me, naming bends and rest frames I would not have recognized. The direct distance was short in both cases. The saved time came from one new angle in one joint.

The parcel continued east. At a receiving frame, Vess handed it over, waited while the fastening was checked, and marked the delivery on the back of the job strip. The work mattered more than the new route. Vess had not crossed the Fell to prove anything. The gap made an ordinary paid journey better, which was the proof that counted.

We visited the shelf on the return.

It lay beneath the market span in a shallow pocket between stone and an old brace. Vess approached without a load, folded the newer joint tightly, and slipped into the space. Dust covered the shelf. One broken fastening lay near the inner edge. There was no hidden store, forgotten passage, or useful view.

“You were correct,” I said. “Nothing.”

“I gave you a precise report.”

Vess moved to the far end, turned within the narrow clearance, and came back. The turn was the part the old body could not have completed. Entering without room to reverse would have made the shelf a trap rather than a route.

I examined the map while Vess held it open. The shelf appeared as a small curl from the market line and ended in a plain stop. The mark did not distinguish valuable destinations from useless ones. It reported that Vess could go there and return.

“Does it know you can turn around?”

“It knows the whole route.”

“How?”

“By being a map.”

That was the answer everyone gave when I asked how hides knew bodies. I had tried map makers, couriers, growers, and one old resident who had owned more maps than I had visited worlds. The question always arrived at the same wall. Hides showed usable ways. If they did not, they were defective goods.

On the way back through the market, Vess stopped for a heavy container that needed moving only two frames. The shelf curl vanished from the map as soon as the sling took the weight. Vess delivered the container, set the sling empty, and checked again. The curl returned.

The world's stone had not moved. Vess's new joint had not moved. A temporary load had taken away a newly gained place and then given it back.

I was still trying to decide whether the map measured or judged. Vess did not care about the difference. The Fell line had shortened a working journey. The shelf line described a small, useless pocket that now belonged within Vess's life.

Both lines were true. That was enough for a courier.

## Chapter 9: Beyond the Hide

The third line appeared eight days later. Vess had accepted a western delivery, the first since the growth change. The destination sat beyond the break where I had entered the world, so Vess opened the hide farther than usual and flattened the old outer fold against the station floor.

Then every limb stopped. I had learned this much about Reachfolk: stillness meant more when it happened everywhere at once. Vess usually adjusted a strap, tested a pad, or angled one end toward the next task. Now the whole body held the map without a single small correction.

I asked what had changed. Vess indicated a thin line leaving a western junction and said it had not been there before. It passed two nearby connections without joining them, continued through an area with few other marks, and reached the outer edge of the hide. I saw no obvious problem. Several routes reached the margin. When I called it a third new way, Vess answered, “Perhaps,” without any of the pride that had accompanied the Fell.

Vess adjusted the hide so I could see without releasing it. I stayed above the surface and did not touch. One route near the new line ended at a place mark before the sheet ran out. Another stopped at the symbol for a drop to a lower rib. Farther down, a line carried the mark for a closed way. Their drawings could continue on another hide, but their present endings had names.

The new line had none. It simply arrived at the cut edge of the hide. There was no small stroke, shape, or change in weight before it left the available surface. The sheet ended because a sheet has a size. The route did not appear to know that.

I asked how Vess chose the next map if a route continued beyond one sheet. Vess showed me that a route crossing several hides still named its next known condition before every margin. A place, descent, closure, fall, or warning told the traveler what kind of continuation to unfold. When I asked what happened if the map knew nothing, Vess said, “Maps do not report guesses.”

Vess turned the hide over. Its back held only creases and a few small delivery notes. When Vess turned it face-up again, the line remained unchanged. We moved into stronger light. Nothing appeared at the end.

I suggested the outer fold might be damaged. Vess examined it with care. The edge was old but clean. Other marks near it stayed complete. The line reached the same point no matter how the hide lay.

“It is a usable route,” Vess said slowly. A closure, failed rib, downward transfer, or destination would each have its own ending. None appeared here. “The hide always runs out. The route is supposed to finish first.”

Noise continued around us. Couriers selected jobs. A sling buckle struck the frame. Terro corrected a weight at the board. Nobody had noticed the quiet problem spreading west across one old fold.

Vess folded the map along its established creases, stopped, opened it again, and followed the line from the junction to the edge. The route did not change under a second look.

I felt the pull of the unknown immediately. A way belonged to Vess's new body and went toward an empty part of the Reach. No map symbol explained its end. I wanted to know what physical fact could defeat a system that calmly accounted for darkness, damage, and missing stone.

Vess was thinking about something else. “I have to report this.” There was no debate in the statement. A strange route on a private map was already station business because Vess carried other people's property. The mystery had entered the trade before either of us moved from the floor.

## Chapter 10: First Check the Ordinary

Terro did not inspect the impossible line first. Vess waited until a courier had been assigned and the station keeper had finished recording a weight, then reported three routes from the recent growth change: the Fell crossing, the shelf under the market, and the western line without an ending. Terro asked to see the first two.

I thought this avoided the point. Vess did not. A claim about one extraordinary route rested on the map's ordinary knowledge of the same changed body. If those simpler lines were wrong, the mystery might belong to Vess's measurements or the hide.

They went to the Fell that afternoon. Vess crossed with an empty sling, came back, accepted a small test load Terro had brought, and crossed again. The second passage required a slower transfer but remained secure. Terro watched pad placement, joint angle, and the amount of body left on each rib before the weight moved.

At the market shelf, Terro stayed outside while Vess entered, turned in the narrow space, and returned. Dust came out in the joints. The broken fastening remained where it had been. Nothing about the shelf had become useful since the previous day.

“Two real additions,” Terro said. “Now we measure.” The station kept a frame for bodies. Vess fitted into it with poor humor while several couriers discovered urgent reasons to remain nearby. A knotted cord measured total length, reaches at several points, and the angle of the newer joint. Then Terro asked Vess to fold as tightly as possible. Vess claimed to be fully folded. Terro mentioned smaller supply frames, and Vess insisted those had been different. Nobody asked how. Advice came from all sides. Vess was accused of breathing too much, bending the wrong end, and protecting dignity at the expense of useful numbers. I laughed until Terro read the figures aloud and the laughter left me.

The measurements matched the two demonstrated routes. Vess's body was exactly as long and flexible as the map required. The change was ordinary, healthy, and sufficient. That removed one possible fault.

Terro took a fresh worked hide from the station stack. It had no lines while it rested on the rack. Vess received it, and dark routes spread across the surface. The Fell connection appeared. The market shelf curled from its familiar span.

The western line crossed the old fold and continued to the outer edge without a mark.

Terro selected a second new hide from deeper in the stack. Vess held it. The same three changes appeared.

“Release it,” Terro said.

Vess set the hide on the floor. The routes faded. Terro picked it up, and a different network formed, Terro's own agreement with Ilm and the surrounding ribs. The unfinished western line was absent.

Vess took the sheet again. The line returned.

We repeated the exchange because repeating a fact is cheaper than trusting surprise. Each time, the hide showed Terro's routes for Terro and Vess's routes for Vess. The result held across both new sheets and the old folded map.

The problem was not one damaged hide. It was not a false report about growth. It was not a route Vess imagined seeing alone, because Terro and I could both see it while Vess held the surface. Yet the moment Terro took possession, the very evidence needing inspection vanished into a different truthful map.

“Now the third one,” Terro said.

Vess spread the old hide and traced the line without touching its end. The station had quieted around us. Even the couriers who had laughed at the measuring frame now found work that allowed them to listen without looking.

Vess explained every missing symbol. Terro checked each nearby ending, asked which continuation hide would normally follow, and tested whether folding the margin concealed a mark. Nothing changed.

“You can see it,” Vess said.

“I can see a route that belongs to you,” Terro answered. “I cannot put my body on it. I cannot make it appear on my own map. I cannot tell whether the line is right by holding the thing that shows it.”

The checks had proved Vess accurate. They had proved the hides consistent. They had also proved that nobody in the station could independently finish the test.

Terro folded the fresh map and returned it to the stack. “We know what isn't wrong,” she said. “That is not the same as knowing what is happening.”

## Chapter 11: A Fact No One Can Hold

The station did not close for the anomaly. That seems obvious now. At the time, I expected every job to stop while Terro considered the unfinished line. Instead, a grain sling arrived heavy, two messages needed eastern carriers, and somebody had brought a package with no useful destination mark. Terro dealt with each problem while Vess's map remained folded beside the weight slate.

Work made the waiting worse. Every ordinary assignment demonstrated the rule under question. A courier checked a route, Terro trusted the hide, and somebody's property left the frame. The system worked again and again within arm's reach of one result nobody could explain.

When the board cleared, Terro asked Vess to open the western fold once more. The line remained. Terro asked for only what the map said, so Vess began at the last known junction. The route passed the west break, followed an outer set of ribs, and continued beyond the part of the Reach where deliveries usually went. It belonged to Vess at present length and bend with an empty or light sling. The map showed no warning along it. At the sheet edge, it failed to name the ending. Asked what that meant, Vess answered honestly: “I don't know.” Terro approved the separation.

The answer sounded harsh until I understood it. A courier's report had facts on one side and explanations on the other. If the explanation failed, the facts still needed to remain usable.

I suggested following the route. Vess turned one end toward me, but Terro answered first. Travel would test the line, but Vess already had assigned work and the unknown route led beyond any station need. “I do not send a courier into an unexplained route because the explanation would please somebody who cannot fall.”

That last part was for me.

Vess did not defend the idea. The route might be real and still end somewhere unsafe. Maps marked danger when the system had a mark for it; the absence of an ending mark proved only that the usual account had failed. It did not turn uncertainty into permission.

Terro took the fresh hide again. Vess's west vanished. Terro's own network included a route toward the shoulder but stopped well before the outer ribs. The station keeper could travel in the same broad direction and still never stand on the disputed line.

“If I hold this,” Terro said, “I have my facts. If you hold it, I can look at yours. Looking is not traveling.” Vess made Terro state each point plainly: the line was visible, both ordinary additions were real, and the measurements were sound. Terro agreed to all three.

Vess waited. The station sounds seemed larger around the pause: straps sliding through rings, stone tools tapping somewhere above, one courier reading a destination aloud at the board.

“Then what is left?” Vess asked.

“A map doing something no map has done in thirty-one years of my work.” Terro's voice stayed level. “I believe your report. I do not understand its cause. Those statements can live together.”

I wanted belief to solve the problem because disbelief would have given me something simple to oppose. Terro offered no such convenience. Vess's honesty was not on trial. The map's unexplained behavior was.

Terro separated the two fresh hides from the station stack and marked their edges so they would not be issued. Vess kept the original. Nothing else could be done that day without choosing the unknown route itself.

“Finish the western delivery you accepted,” Terro said. “Use the known route. Come back before the low light.”

Vess refolded the map so the unfinished line disappeared inside. The parcel was still waiting. Its recipient still needed it. Vess checked the sling, took the job strip, and left with the same care as on any other run.

Terro watched until Vess passed out of sight behind the lower curve. I could not tell whether the station keeper had decided anything. I knew only that belief had not made the unexplained fact safe enough to use.

## Chapter 12: The Work Terro Could Risk

Terro made the decision when Vess returned before low light. The western parcel had been delivered along its known route. The job strip carried the recipient's mark. Vess had done exactly what Terro asked and brought no new problem back to the station.

“I am taking you off long assignments,” Terro said. Vess placed the completed strip beside the slate and asked how long the restriction would last. Terro had two conditions for ending it: understanding what the map was doing, or proving where the unfinished route went.

I began arguing before Vess did. The map had shown no false route. Two fresh hides agreed. The body measurements agreed. Vess had reported the anomaly without delay and completed the day's work by a safe known path.

Terro agreed with every point, which made me louder. If no lie, error, or unsafe act had occurred, removing work seemed like punishment without an offense. Terro rejected the word *punishment* while admitting that the decision removed the best assignments. Vess supplied the exact term: “A restriction.”

The word did not improve anything, but it was exact. Terro was not claiming Vess had done wrong. She was limiting the value placed behind a map with one unexplained result. Long runs carried larger loads, better pay, and greater distances beyond help. They also established standing in the station. Taking them away had real cost even if no blame came with it.

Terro turned both ends toward the couriers who had found work nearby and made the public record plain. Vess had not altered a hide or invented a route. Vess had reported the appearance and passed every available check. Anyone repeating another version should answer to Terro. Then the station keeper faced Vess again. “I trust you. I do not yet trust the condition we cannot explain.”

Vess pressed the uncertainty. Terro did not know whether the condition could change another route, and no ordinary route had been proved wrong. The restriction rested on what might happen.

Terro stayed still for a moment. “I assign somebody's food, medicine, message, or private property to a body and a map. I cannot write *probably fine* on the weight slate. If an unexplained change exists, I have to decide who carries its risk. It cannot be the customer without their knowledge.”

There was no easy flaw in that answer. A station keeper's caution could be unfair to Vess and still protect people who had never agreed to join the test. Both facts fit in the same frame.

Vess asked what work remained. Terro listed local deliveries, short routes around Ilm, and messages within reach of quick help. The station would pay for the work done. Terro would assign as much as was honestly available. None of it equaled the long runs Vess had earned over eleven years.

Terro promised to say the map was unexplained rather than wrong. Vess folded the hide and observed that people would hear *wrong* anyway. Terro would correct the story wherever it reached her. She could not correct every market frame, and both of them knew it. That was the cost Terro could not remove.

The next morning, Vess arrived at the usual time. Terro gave the eastern run to another courier and handed Vess three local parcels. Nothing in the exchange was rude. Nothing required an apology. Vess checked each fastening, planned the shortest usable order, and left.

The western line remained inside the folded hide. It had not been proved false. It had not even been used. Still, it had already changed the route of Vess's life.

# Part III: The Cost of Being Interesting

## Chapter 13: Smaller Work

The restriction did not ruin Vess's life in one clear event. It reduced it by errands. On the first day, Vess delivered three parcels inside Ilm. On the second, there were five, but all traveled less than an hour from the station. Terro kept the local work coming as promised. Vess remained quick, careful, and paid. Anybody looking only at completed job strips could have called the arrangement successful.

The long board told another story.

Names appeared beside distant runs that had belonged to Vess for years. Semmit took the eastern route. Another courier carried a heavy western sling. Vess still checked their knots when asked and pointed out one return path that would close in low light. Competence had not left. Authority over distant work had.

People learned the news in pieces. A market seller asked whether the hide had failed. Vess said no: one route had no ending mark. The seller agreed too quickly and added that no offense was intended. By the next frame, the story had become a map showing roads that were not there.

Terro corrected that version publicly. It helped for almost an afternoon. One customer arrived with a small package for a frame four spans away. Vess was available. The route lay entirely inside Ilm and appeared on every tested map. The customer looked at Vess, looked at Terro, and asked whether somebody else might take it.

No accusation came with the request. That made it difficult to answer. Terro could have refused and lost the station a customer. Vess could have argued and confirmed the fear. Instead, another courier took the package while Vess accepted two messages going in the opposite direction.

Afterward, Terro marked the full fee on Vess's slate anyway. Vess pushed the mark back because readiness was not the same as completed work. Terro did not insist. Both decisions cost something: the money left unpaid, and Terro lost the chance to pretend the station could make the restriction harmless.

I followed Vess on the message route. The first recipient lived below the green rows and accepted the strip without comment. The second asked how long the map problem would continue. Vess said the map had no proved error and the station was still testing one unexplained route.

“Of course,” the recipient said. “I only wondered when it would be settled.” Vess waited as if another sentence might arrive. None did.

On the return, Vess checked the map at every junction. The behavior had always been part of the job. Now other travelers noticed it. One moved aside with unnecessary room. Another watched until Vess folded the hide. Nobody was openly cruel. People protected their bodies and property by small choices, each reasonable enough to defend alone.

Together, those choices followed Vess through Ilm. By the fifth day, the work had narrowed again. Local parcels went to couriers customers knew less about. Vess carried messages, because a lost message could be repeated more easily than food or medicine. Terro never said this rule aloud. The board revealed it anyway.

I tried to find a clean point where caution became unfairness. There was none. Terro assigned within the restriction. Customers chose carriers for their own property. Other couriers accepted paid work. Vess kept reporting accurately. Every individual acted within a right they plainly had.

The result was still Vess standing beside the board while Semmit left for the east.

Vess did not become less skilled during those days. That would have made the reduction easier to understand. The knots remained exact. The route estimates remained right. Twice Vess prevented another courier from accepting a load that would remove a needed bend. Each useful act proved the same thing the station already knew and could not use to answer the western line.

At the end of the week, Vess took one final message to the market and came back before the busiest light had passed. The whole working day fit into a small section of the slate.

Vess asked whether anything remained. Terro looked at the board longer than necessary before saying there was nothing she could give. Vess folded the map, secured an empty sling, and went home early.

## Chapter 14: The Wrong Kind of Company

Vess lived beneath a rib three spans from Ilm's center. The home was an open frame braced into shaded stone, with a hanging store, rails for slings, a work brace for mending straps, and a view down into the bright depth. At the outer edge sat a perch shaped to Vess.

The stone had been cut when Vess was smaller, then widened twice as the body changed. Older tool marks remained beneath the newer curve. Vess could have replaced it with a fresh support. Instead, the perch held a record of several bodies that had all been Vess.

Most evenings, Vess rested there and watched the light move under the ribs. During the first week of the restriction, the empty sling went onto its rail earlier each day.

I stayed nearby because I believed company was useful. This was true in a broad sense and did not make me good company.

The unfinished route occupied me. I compared it with every map rule I had learned. Closed routes had marks because somebody had found a closure. Fallen ribs had marks because somebody had survived long enough to report missing stone, or another map had learned the condition. Warnings distinguished a difficult way from an unusable one. The system held language for every repeated failure the Reach knew.

Vess's line had reached a condition without language. I said this aloud on the first evening. Vess listened while repairing a frayed strap. On the second, I proposed that the route might lead to a rib so new the maps had no destination name. Vess reminded me that a nameless place still had an ending mark. On the third, I wondered whether the route changed while traveled, and Vess pointed out that we had no traveled fact at all.

Each correction made the problem better for me.

One evening, Vess came home with only two message marks on the slate and set the work sling down without cleaning it. Dust remained in the inner seam. This was the first neglected piece of station equipment I had seen Vess leave overnight.

I noticed and said, “The map may be reporting something that has never happened often enough to need a symbol.” Vess looked at the sling, then at the hanging rail, but did not move. I continued. A symbol required agreement. First somebody encountered a condition. Then the condition had to matter enough, and occur often enough, for map users to name it. An ending with no mark might be the first example of a new category.

Vess offered the simpler possibility that the map was wrong. I answered with the truth of its other lines. Vess said that was no answer. “No,” I said. “That is what makes it interesting.”

I used that word often. I can hear it now because of what came later. At the time, *interesting* felt accurate and harmless. It meant the facts did not fit, the ordinary tests had survived, and a larger explanation might exist.

Vess finally cleaned the sling. The motion was slower than at the station. Fine dust came out of the seam and drifted through the open frame. When the strap was ready, Vess hung it carefully in its usual place.

I should have watched that work and understood what it meant. Eleven years of long routes lived in those habits. Vess cleaned a sling that might carry only messages the next morning because the trade remained Vess's even while the station held part of it back.

Instead, I asked whether maps could contain a route outside the known Reach.

Vess settled onto the old perch. “I have been home before the high light for four days.”

The statement did not answer my question, so I treated it as a change of subject. I told Vess I was sorry about the work and then returned to the western line. That apology cost me nothing and changed nothing.

Light rose from the haze beneath us. Ilm's noise came through three spans and many braces: distant tools, carriers calling, a container set down too hard. Vess listened toward the town where other couriers were finishing the day.

I hovered beside the perch, turning the most exciting problem on the world through my mind. The person who owned the problem sat beside me and said very little.

## Chapter 15: Neighbors

Han's home faced Vess's across twenty body-lengths of air. It hung beneath a different rib, close enough that I could see the rows of green trained along its bright edge. Han tended those rows in the evenings, adjusting ties and checking the shallow channels that carried water. Vess sat on the shaped perch. They spoke across the gap without raising their voices much.

There was no crossing between the homes. To visit by stone, a traveler had to go inward to Ilm, climb around the knot, descend the far side, and come back along two other ribs. The journey used most of a morning. Vess and Han had been neighbors for nine years and had never made it.

When I asked whether this troubled them, Han wanted to know why standing inside Vess's frame was required. I said friends visited. Vess turned one end toward me. “We are friends here.”

The gap had not prevented the friendship. It had shaped it. Han knew when Vess left for work because the empty perch faced the green rows. Vess knew when a water channel clogged because Han complained while clearing it. They shared news, jokes I often understood too late, and stretches of quiet that neither tried to fill.

What they did not share was physical space. Han had a weak joint on the left side, third from one end. The joint had never carried full reach and resisted wide bridges. Han showed me a map where several nearby crossings broke around that limit.

Han had watched hundreds cross the Fell and knew it would never become a route for that body. When I asked whether this felt unfair, Han pressed a green stem into its support. “There is more Reach than any body gets. I have good light.”

This was not resignation. Han was proud of the three rows and willing to explain why the light there produced better growth than the upper market strips. The weak joint closed some routes. It did not turn Han's whole life into a lesson about missing them.

The evening routine continued around my questions. Han checked the far channel. Vess repaired a fastener. They discussed a market price, Nib's latest bend, and whether rain would reach the catching bowls before the next low light. None of it required them to touch.

When Vess went inside for a bowl left near the work brace, Han asked quietly whether the station had found anything. I said the line remained unchanged. Han adjusted one tie and asked about Vess. I began explaining my theory about a map condition without a symbol. Han waited until I paused for agreement. “That was not what I asked.”

Vess returned before I found a useful answer. Han did not repeat the question. The two neighbors resumed their argument about rain, and I stayed out over the gap where neither could go.

From there, the distance looked small enough to dismiss. I could cross it in seconds. I could occupy the exact middle and speak to both homes without effort. The empty air did not restrict me, so I had been treating it as absence.

For Vess and Han, it was part of the relationship. They had built nine years of friendship across it. The long route through Ilm was available, but cost enough time and labor that neither had needed to use it. Seeing each other was simple. Reaching each other was not.

Before the light faded, Han cut a small piece from one of the green rows and set it on the outer brace. “For Vess,” Han said. The cutting lay twenty body-lengths from the person meant to receive it. By stone, it was most of a morning away. I moved toward it before either neighbor asked.

## Chapter 16: Four Seconds

I carried the cutting across in four seconds. *Carried* is not quite right. I have no hands. I placed myself against the small green piece, moved, and kept enough pressure on it to prevent drifting. Vess received it at the perch and thanked Han across the open air.

The exchange pleased me. A morning of travel had become less time than it takes to unfold a map. After that, I became a route between the homes.

Han found a bowl Vess had left years earlier and sent it back. Vess repaired a small water-channel fitting for Han. When Vess came home without eating properly, Han placed dried fruit on the outer brace and looked at me. I moved it without being asked. Each crossing took four seconds, sometimes five if the object was awkward.

I liked being useful. I also liked the elegance of it. The Reach made people calculate bodies, loads, light, and hours. I ignored the missing stone and solved the gap by existing.

Neither neighbor celebrated as much as I expected. They appreciated the objects. Han fitted the repaired piece into the channel. Vess put the old bowl back by the work brace and ate the fruit. Their ordinary evening talk continued. The new service did not transform nine years of friendship because the friendship had not been waiting for rescue.

I missed that fact and filled the silence with the western route.

While moving the bowl, I explained that a map symbol had to come from shared experience. On the return trip with the channel fitting, I proposed that the missing ending might lie beyond the mapped world's known categories. With the dried fruit, I described how a real route could continue toward a place no current traveler had named.

Han answered less often. Vess answered accurately when a factual correction was needed. I treated both behaviors as encouragement.

One evening, Han handed me nothing and asked me to stay on that side for a moment. A row of green had grown into the next row's light, and Han needed to retie three stems. I watched the pads work around the weak joint. Han did not ask for help with what could be done alone.

Han said I liked the line. I insisted that I wanted to understand it. “Those may be different statements.”

I considered this. Liking a problem did not mean wishing harm on Vess. I wanted the restriction removed. I wanted the map proved true. If the western line led to an ordinary road ending, I would have accepted the answer.

I also knew I would have been disappointed. That was the private truth I did not give Han. The lack of a symbol suggested something the Reach had never classified. An ordinary answer might restore Vess's work, but an impossible one might tell me something about the world. I wanted both results and had not admitted they could pull in different directions.

When the stems were tied, Han cut another small piece of green for Vess. I moved it across the gap and began talking before I reached the perch.

“If the route ends beyond the outer ribs, the map may not know which world fact comes next.” Vess accepted the cutting and set it beside the bowl. “I lost the eastern run today.”

I stopped, but only for a moment. I said Semmit would handle it well, because that seemed supportive. Then I returned to the line and explained that the map might be reporting the limit of its own knowledge.

Vess went inside. Han remained by the green rows, looking toward the empty perch. I stayed in the middle of the gap with no object to carry. The trip had taken four seconds. I had spent all of them talking.

The next evening, Han put dried fruit on the brace again. I carried it over and placed it beside Vess without a theory attached. I believed this showed improvement. It showed only that I could remain quiet for one crossing.

## Chapter 17: What the Map Holds

The eastern run left without Vess for the second time the next morning. Semmit took it. Semmit was careful, knew the first half well, and asked Vess about the return light before leaving. Vess answered every question. When Semmit had gone, Terro handed Vess four message strips and a small covered pot for someone's aunt in the lower quarter. Vess completed all five jobs.

That evening, the perch remained empty. I waited outside the frame until the rising light dimmed, then went inside without being asked. I knew this was an intrusion. I entered anyway because I wanted to repair the previous night, and because wanting to repair something can become an excuse for deciding one is allowed near it.

Vess rested in the darker part of the frame. The cleaned sling hung on its rail. The map lay folded beside it. I began with the wrong subject, announcing that I had been thinking about how ending marks began. Vess did not move while I explained that closed and fallen routes must once have needed separate names. The western line might represent another such moment.

“Do you want to know what I carried today?” Vess asked. The question finally interrupted me. Vess listed the messages, the covered pot, and the frames they went to. None required more than local travel. The entire day could have been done by a young courier learning Ilm. Meanwhile Semmit had taken the eastern route Vess had worked for years.

“Semmit will do it well,” Vess said. “That is not the problem.” I said I understood. Vess told me I did not.

The words were quiet. I tried to defend my effort. I had carried food when Vess was not eating. I had stayed nearby. I wanted to prove the map so the long work could return.

Vess turned toward the open side of the frame. Han's green rows were dark across the gap.

“You cross that space all evening,” Vess said. “You bring a bowl, a cutting, fruit. Nine years Han and I have lived this close without a route. You remove the distance in four seconds and act as if you solved it.” I said I had been helping. Vess agreed, and said that was why the next part was difficult.

Vess moved closer to the empty perch but did not settle into it. “You think the gap is nothing because it asks nothing of you. You think the western line is a question because it cannot take anything from you. If the map is wrong, you float home. If the road fails, you stop in the air. If the light changes, you still go where you looked.”

Each statement was physically true. I had been proud of those abilities when I arrived. On this world they removed me from every consequence that made routes matter.

“I know your work depends on the map,” I said.

“Knowing the sentence is not the same as needing it.” Vess indicated the hanging sling. “I have trusted maps with my body for eleven years. Terro has trusted me with medicine, food, and letters people waited for. One line does something nobody understands, and now every person who sees me check a junction wonders whether the next line is wrong too.”

Vess's voice stayed level. That made it impossible to dismiss as anger that would pass. “You call it interesting every night,” Vess said. “It is interesting. I agree. It is also my pay, my name at the station, and whether somebody lets me carry a pot four spans.”

I had no answer that did not begin with what I had meant.

Meaning well had become the shelter I wanted. Inside it, carrying fruit counted as care and wanting proof counted as loyalty. Outside it, I had used Vess's loss to entertain the best part of my mind.

Vess gave me the part I had avoided. “Your life has never required anyone to believe your map.”

The frame held the sentence after Vess stopped speaking. Beyond it, Han moved once among the green rows and then stayed still.

I could have explained that I did not have a map. I could have said that crossings carried their own uncertainty, or that I wanted the restriction ended as much as Vess did. All of that would have been an argument against a statement Vess had not made.

So I said, “You are right.” Vess began to answer, stopped, and went to the perch. I remained inside only long enough to understand that no further sentence from me would improve the evening. Then I moved out over the gap and did not carry anything at all.

## Chapter 18: A Bad Argument for a Good Person

I could have gone west alone. The thought followed me through the night. The map showed enough direction to begin, and the Reach had no gap I could not cross. I could inspect every outer rib in an afternoon, find whatever ended the route, and return with proof before Vess finished another set of messages.

Nobody needed to know I had gone. That was the sentence that stopped me. A secret investigation would have given me the answer while taking the choice away from Vess again. The line belonged to Vess's map, the restriction belonged to Vess's work, and the bodily danger belonged to any Reachfolk who followed. My safety did not grant ownership.

I went to the station instead. Terro was sorting the next day's long jobs. I waited until every load had a place, then asked for the restriction to be removed. My reasons were sincere and badly arranged.

I began with the record Vess had built over eleven years: accurate routes, difficult runs, sound load decisions, and no reason to doubt a report. Terro said none of that was disputed. Then I pointed to the choice that had created the present loss. Vess could have hidden the unfinished line and kept working. Reporting it had protected the station. Terro stopped sorting and gave that point full attention.

“That part worries me,” Terro said. “If honest reporting removes work, the next courier may decide silence is safer.” For a moment I thought I had won. Then Terro added that the problem still did not make the unexplained line safe.

I argued that no other route had failed, that the fresh hides agreed, and that a missing ending was not a false path. These were facts Terro had established personally. Repeating them with greater force did not turn them into an answer.

Then I said the restriction was unfair. Terro agreed. The answer removed another argument I had prepared. I wanted Terro to defend the harm so I could defeat the defense. Instead, Terro named the harm and kept the limit.

“Fair would mean there was a wrong action I could correct,” Terro said. “Vess did the right thing. The map produced a condition I cannot account for. I can regret the cost without assigning that cost to people who bring us their property.”

I asked whether caution could become the greater wrong. Terro said yes. I asked where that point was. Terro did not pretend to know.

The long board remained between us. Grain, medicine, letters, and other private parcels waited under marks showing weight and destination. I could not promise any owner that Vess's map would never produce another unfinished fact. I believed it would not. Belief was the very distinction I had failed to understand.

When I asked what could change the decision, Terro named two things: proof that the line was a real route, or proof that the strange condition could not affect the rest of Vess's map. She had neither.

I left the station without winning anything. Several couriers had heard enough to know I had argued. One gave me a sympathetic turn of both ends. I disliked the sympathy because it made my failure public, which was a small taste of what Vess had been living through.

Vess was on the perch when I returned. Han tended the far row without appearing to listen. I stayed outside the home frame.

I told Vess that Terro had said no and that I had argued badly. Vess had already heard. Ilm could carry a bad argument farther than any courier. Terro had also sent another part of the story: nobody had ever come to the station to argue for Vess before.

I did not use the information to defend myself. The argument had not restored the work. It had only shown Vess that I was finally trying to solve the human problem as well as the map problem.

Across the gap, Han adjusted one of the green ties and said the route was still there. Vess looked at the folded hide beside the perch and agreed. This time, I waited.

# Part IV: The Body's Route

## Chapter 19: Vess Chooses West

Vess opened the map after the light had nearly gone. The western line remained where it had always been, beginning beyond familiar work and running toward the margin without an ending. Vess followed it once with a pad, then folded the hide again.

Vess recalled Terro's two conditions. One was proof that the route was real. I waited while Vess reached the rest: nobody else at the station had the line, nobody else could test it, and leaving it alone would preserve the restriction forever.

Across the gap, Han stopped checking a water channel and faced us.

Vess did not ask what I wanted. That mattered. I had made the western route the center of too many conversations, but this decision belonged to the body whose hide showed it and whose work had been cut away.

“I am going to follow it,” Vess said. I asked whether I should come. Vess wanted the help because I could return quickly if the journey failed. The answer made my strange body useful without placing it in charge, and I accepted.

Han asked about the route, and Vess described only what the map established: it left the known western network beyond the shoulder, belonged to a lightly loaded body, and carried no danger or closure symbol. It might still be difficult. A map said a way could be traveled, not that the traveler would enjoy it.

Han asked how much food the trip required. Vess estimated a full day outward, possibly longer if the outer connections were poor, plus the return. The route was not a station assignment. No receiving frame waited with supplies. Everything needed had to leave Ilm in the starting sling.

That created the first problem. More food meant more weight. More weight could remove the very line being tested.

Vess packed the sling in stages and unfolded the map after each one. Food, water, a spare strap, and a small repair tool stayed. One additional water vessel made the line thin and then vanish. Vess removed it. The route returned.

I suggested I could move supplies separately through the air. Vess considered the offer rather than refusing from pride, then asked whether four-second carrying proved I could keep two containers from drifting for a full day.

I did not know. Four seconds across Han's gap proved very little. If I lost a container in open air, I could retrieve it, but Vess might be waiting on a rib I could no longer identify among hundreds.

We decided I would carry nothing essential. Vess's life could not depend on a skill neither of us had tested. The sling held everything required to return by the route on the map.

Han prepared dried food and divided it into smaller packages that could be shifted along the body if one section tired. Vess checked every tie. I watched without converting the work into a theory.

Han and Vess argued about departure time. Vess saw no reason to enter the outer route in darkness. Han cared about clearing Ilm before morning traffic. They settled on a time neither liked, which appeared to be the ordinary result of sound planning between friends.

The next morning, Han was already at the green rows when we came out. Vess wore the lightest safe sling. The map's western line remained clear. Ilm stirred above us as the first carriers moved toward the station.

From the top of the knot, the outer western ribs looked close. Pale spans thinned into the bright distance beyond the break. If sight measured effort, we could have arrived before breakfast.

Vess said the journey would take most of a day. I had lived on the Reach long enough not to argue. Han's voice came across the gap behind us. “Go early if you mean to take the long way.”

Vess checked the first turn on the hide. The route began by going down and away from the visible west. Then Vess chose it.

## Chapter 20: Away From West

We traveled downward for the first hour. The outer ribs we wanted remained high and west of us, plainly visible whenever Ilm did not block the view. Vess moved the opposite direction along the underside of the market span, following a route that looked wrong from every open angle. I did not offer a straighter one.

We passed the quiet frames where old and unwell residents rested in the upward glow. A sling mender had already begun work at the edge of one frame. The mender recognized Vess, looked at the light travel sling, and asked whether the restriction story was true.

“The part where my map has an unfinished line is true,” Vess said. “The part where the map invents roads is not proved.”

The mender offered regret. Vess accepted it and kept moving. Neither asked where we were going.

Past the quiet frames, the worked surface ended. The rib narrowed and curved down. Vess checked the hide at two close junctions because one of them led back toward the visible west and was wrong. The correct path took us lower again.

I asked how a western route could require so much retreat. Vess pointed one end toward a thick span between us and the west. Its nearest face looked passable, but the far edge ended above an open break. Going toward it would deliver a traveler to a view, not a continuation. The lower route passed beneath the obstruction and reached a connection on the other side.

“The map does not care which direction feels correct,” Vess said.

I had spent my first days treating the Reach as visible geometry. Now I tried to read it as a sequence of grips. The difference was exhausting even though I did no physical work. Every promising line could fail behind a curve. Every apparent detour could preserve the next turn.

At a narrow bridge, Vess shifted two food packages toward the rear sections. The forward part of the body needed freedom to bend around a raised seam. The load weighed the same, but its position changed the route from doubtful to secure. Vess crossed, restored the balance, and checked the ties.

I watched the whole process without moving ahead.

That choice cost me nothing, which made it easy to call patience. It still mattered to Vess. At the next junction, Vess asked me to look through an opening and confirm whether the far rib carried a rain-dark patch. I could provide sight without choosing the route. The patch was there. Vess used it to confirm our position.

The work divided cleanly for the first time. Vess owned the way. I reported what my movement allowed me to see.

Morning traffic thinned behind us. Ilm remained visible as a layered knot high in the lattice, but its voices no longer reached us. The stone carried only the scrape and release of Vess's pads and an occasional low vibration from distant travelers.

At the third hour, the route finally turned west. We moved along a slanted rib where the upper surface was too rounded to use. Vess traveled the shaded side instead, the body curved across the slope so no single line of pads carried all the weight. The light sling still pulled toward open air. A full delivery load would have erased that passage.

“Would the old body have come this way?” I asked.

Vess opened the map at a shallow pocket and compared the approach. The line belonged to the new bend, though not the new length. Before the change, the middle sections could not settle against the slope while both ends held. The way west had become possible through several small agreements, not one heroic gap.

We stopped at a resting frame that faced back toward Ilm. Vess ate one package and drank part of the allowed water. The extra vessel remained at home because carrying it would have removed the route. I understood the fact more sharply now. The map had not asked Vess to travel thirsty. It had defined how much safety could come along.

I looked west. The outer ribs appeared no closer than they had at dawn. I complained that we had traveled for hours without changing the visible distance. Vess secured the food wrapper in the sling. “That is because you are measuring the gap again.”

When we left the frame, I placed myself beside Vess rather than ahead. The next turn went down once more. This time, it looked like progress.

## Chapter 21: The Shape of Enough

The slot looked too small before Vess entered it. A crack opened in the side of a thick rib and twisted through the stone. Light reached the first turn and no farther. The map line went directly into it.

Vess removed the food packages from the main sling and divided them into two tied groups. Water and the repair tool stayed against the center. One food group went inside first.

I worried aloud about leaving half the food unattended. Vess looked along the empty rib. “Then we will meet the only other person who has wanted this route in years.”

The first end entered. Vess folded the next joint, drew three sections into the crack, and changed which pads held each time the slot turned. The body disappeared until only the rear section remained visible. A steady stream of complaints came back through the stone.

I followed outside the rib. The crack did not run straight, so I could not simply hover beside Vess. I moved around the stone to the far opening and waited.

Vess emerged coated in fine dust, secured the first group beyond the exit, and immediately went back empty. The return was faster. Without the packages, the middle joint had more room to turn.

When Vess reached the starting side, I announced that the route had already required two passages. Vess praised my counting and took the second food group through on the third. One crossing on the map required the body to travel the slot three times and reverse twice. The route line recorded the completed possibility, not the labor hidden inside it.

I went through the stone by using the crack only as direction. Nothing touched me. I could have waited on the far side from the beginning, but staying near the starting supplies mattered while Vess was out of sight. That was a form of help the route actually needed.

After the third passage, Vess rebuilt the sling. The food returned to its balanced positions. Every tie was checked before we moved.

I asked why Vess had not moved one full group through. The outer package would catch at the second turn, and pushing it ahead would remove the room needed to reverse if it jammed.

The answer contained the part I kept forgetting. A route was not only the forward motion that succeeded. It included enough room to stop, withdraw, turn, or set down a load without losing it. The map's promise covered the whole act.

We continued through a section where the ribs crossed close. Several possible turns appeared on the hide, but only one preserved the western line under the present load. Vess checked after each steep bend because food and water had shifted by small amounts.

At one junction, the unfinished line vanished. Vess stopped immediately. The stone around us had not changed. Light remained steady. The sling looked balanced.

We checked the ties. The water vessel had slid less than a pad-width toward the newer joint. Its strap had not loosened, but the weight now sat where Vess needed to fold at a coming turn.

Vess moved the vessel back, and the western line returned. I had watched maps respond to whole parcels, large containers, and fading light. This change was smaller than any of those. A slight shift inside a sling had removed a route somewhere ahead, perhaps hours away.

I asked how the map knew which future bend would fail. Vess pulled the strap tighter. “By being a map.”

This time the familiar answer did not feel like a refusal. It was an admission that the hide held more bodily consequence than either of us could calculate. Vess did not need to identify the future turn. The line's return was enough to continue.

By midday, I understood why the journey had to belong to a particular body on a particular morning. Vess was not traveling west in general. Vess was traveling with this new joint, this amount of water, these divided food packages, this light, and enough room to come home.

## Chapter 22: Work Showing Through

Beyond the close crossings, the map took us under a long rib. Vess wrapped around its edge and settled onto the shaded face. The brightness opened below. Other spans crossed far above and far beneath, though those words depended entirely on where I chose to look. Vess's pads held stone, which was the only direction that mattered. We traveled that face for two hours.

The wind strengthened away from Ilm. It moved along the rib, caught the travel sling, and pressed one side of Vess's body outward. Vess angled across the stone rather than fighting straight into it. Each grip took part of the sideways pull before the next released.

I could have moved in still air a short distance from the surface. Instead, I stayed where the wind struck too. It did not threaten me, but it helped me see which shifts Vess had to answer.

Small stone points appeared in a regular line ahead. They came through the rib from the opposite face. Each was the bottom of a deep road cut made long ago. Workers on the unseen upper side had carved far enough that the lowest points nearly broke through. Weather had opened some of them into pale knobs.

I recognized that we were under a road. Vess named it as the Merrin and explained that the points placed the next elbow about forty body-lengths ahead.

Vess used the marks without looking directly at them. People who cut the road had not intended to make signs on the underside. Their work had crossed the stone anyway, and generations of travelers had turned the accident into a measure.

I counted forty-one points before the rib changed. Vess said I had counted one broken notch twice. The elbow appeared where expected.

Beyond it, an old resting frame clung to the shade. Two braces had been replaced in a lighter material; the others carried dark wear where slings had rubbed them. We stopped long enough for Vess to shift food forward and inspect a sore grip pad.

The pad was not injured. Dust from the slot had worked into one ridge. Vess cleaned it with the edge of the spare strap, then tested it against the frame before trusting body weight to it again.

I asked how often Vess repaired the body during long work. Vess corrected the word: this was maintenance. The distinction mattered to a courier. An injury changed the map. Maintenance protected the body the map described.

Vess ate while hanging beneath the frame and looked out toward the west. The ribs had spread farther apart. Ilm was still visible behind us, but only because its knot interrupted the open pattern. Ahead, frames and green disappeared. No travelers moved on the nearest stone.

I asked why nobody used the outer ways when the ribs clearly continued. Vess answered, “Stone continuing is not a reason to spend a day.”

That answer would have offended the earlier version of me. Now I looked at the water in Vess's sling, the dust cleaned from one pad, and the distance already traveled. A way could be real and still have no ordinary claim on anyone's time.

The map line led farther through emptiness. We left the resting frame and followed the underside until the old road points ended. The final one had cracked away, leaving only a shallow pale circle.

After that, the stone carried no sign of work. Its surface was rougher. Vess's pace slowed because every grip had to be chosen rather than expected. The wind continued without a frame to break it.

I asked when the last traveler had come this far. Vess did not know. Terro might have known an old station record, but the question had never mattered before. There was nothing to deliver, collect, maintain, or visit.

The emptiness was not abandonment in any dramatic sense. It was simply the part of the world no present need selected. Then the map told us to leave the underside and climb toward the shoulder.

Vess checked the sore pad once more, tightened the sling against the coming turn, and moved.

## Chapter 23: Past the Shoulder

The shoulder was the first generous piece of stone we had seen for hours. Several northern ribs curved together into a broad flat turn. A traveler could rest there without folding, balancing, or placing a pad with special care. The map showed many bodies could reach it, though the journey from Ilm was long. We arrived late in the day.

Vess crossed the flat slowly, saving the sore pad. Beyond the shoulder, an outer rib continued west and slightly down. A gap separated it from us.

The distance was modest. The landing was not. The far lip rose at an angle, and the first safe grip sat below the visible edge. A body needed enough length to reach around, enough bend to keep the rear pads seated, and enough room in the sling to transfer weight.

Vess checked the map. The line crossed. Food had been eaten since the slot, so the load was lighter. Water had decreased too. Vess shifted the repair tool away from the new joint and tightened every strap. Then the leading end extended over open air.

I moved beneath the gap where I could see both sets of grips. I could not catch Vess, but I could report a missed hold before weight committed.

The first pad reached stone and slid across dust. Vess withdrew it without releasing the shoulder. On the second attempt, the pad found the lower roughness. A second grip joined it. The newer joint turned sharply while the rear half stayed spread across the near lip.

“Hold,” I said. One sling tie had lifted against the edge.

Vess stopped with the body stretched between ribs. I described the tie's position. Vess shifted the middle less than a pad-width, and the strap cleared. Then weight moved forward in stages until the final rear pads released the shoulder.

Vess rested on the outer rib before saying my warning had been useful. I accepted the statement without explaining how useful I had intended to be.

The old body could not have made the transfer. It had nearly enough length, as at the Fell, but the needed bend was different. Semmit had the length and not the angle. Terro lacked both for this approach. Somewhere in Ilm another body might have crossed, but no ordinary reason had ever required finding out.

I crossed without contact and settled beside Vess rather than going ahead. The outer country was empty. No knots interrupted the ribs. No water bowls caught the light. No green followed the brighter faces, and no resting frames showed against the stone. Ilm had become a dark shape behind layers of spans.

Vess unfolded the hide. The unfinished line ran along the rib beneath us. “We are on it.”

That was already evidence. The map had not invented the shoulder gap. It had described a transfer the changed body could make. But one real section could not yet explain where the line went or why it lacked an end.

We followed the rib west. At first, its surface looked untouched. Weather had rounded the highest part and filled cracks with pale dust. Then Vess stopped and swept one pad across the center. The dust moved aside, exposing flat stone.

We cleared a longer section. The flat continued in a narrow band down the middle. Near one edge, a shallow hollow appeared, then another at a regular distance. They were worn and partly broken, but they did not belong to weather.

Farther on, two empty sockets cut the outer margin. A fallen brace lay below them, still caught against a projection in the rib. Vess checked the pattern from several angles. The body went still, though this stillness differed from the station. Fear may have been present; I could not know. What I could see was recognition.

“This is worked stone,” Vess said. The empty outer rib had a road on it.

## Chapter 24: The Road Improves

We stayed near the old sockets until the light failed. The shoulder lay behind us across the gap, but the outer rib offered no resting frame where we first reached it. Vess chose a shallow dip beside the worked strip, secured the sling to two natural projections, and settled every grip before sleeping. I remained above the rib and watched the light rise from below.

The road had been nearly erased near the shoulder. Weather softened its center and broke the edge notches. I could still see the decision inside it: a narrow level surface carried through stone that had no reason to be level.

At first light, we went west. The work became clearer with distance. Dust still filled the notches, but their sides sharpened. The outer grooves remained continuous for longer stretches. We found another pair of frame sockets, then a complete brace lying flat against the road where an old fastening still held one end.

Vess read the construction the way Terro read a job board. At one outcrop, workers had taken the road around the stone instead of cutting through it. The detour added length and required the outer edge to be reinforced.

Vess pointed out that the choice had cost a great deal of time. The builders could have stopped. Nothing in the empty western Reach forced them to keep working. Instead, they had spent months avoiding one hard piece of stone and then continued.

The third resting frame still stood. Its bars were weathered and one brace had twisted, but the structure could hold Vess's light sling. We stopped there, and Vess tested every connection before placing weight on it. A shallow wear mark crossed the central bar where many loads had rested. Vess agreed that repeated use had polished the stone.

The road was not an unfinished experiment abandoned after a few cuts. Travelers had moved loads along it. Somebody had maintained frames. The sharper work farther west suggested either later building or less use, and we had no way to choose between those explanations.

I wanted to ask who had traveled there and what they carried. I did not turn the questions into facts. The road proved labor and use. It did not supply names, dates, or destinations.

We passed a fourth frame and found its bars missing. Only anchor holes remained. At the fifth, one short piece projected from the rib's side. The sequence between frames was close enough that a loaded traveler could rest at regular intervals.

The map line remained steady under Vess's body. Near midday, the rib began to narrow. Other stone spans had already ended behind or below us. Ahead, open brightness occupied more of the view. The Reach's western side did not gather into a wall or coast. Its ribs simply became fewer until no stone continued.

Our rib pointed toward that emptiness, and the road did too. It did not curve toward a neighboring span. No drop mark appeared on the map. The flat ran along the center while the natural stone tightened around it. Edge notches continued in the same measured order.

Vess stopped twice to unfold the full western sheet. The line still reached the hide margin without an ending. Each check placed us farther along it.

I checked the known alternatives with Vess. A natural rib end should carry a fall mark; a transfer should show the next connection. Neither appeared. I left the third possibility unspoken because we did not yet know what physical condition waited ahead.

Late in the day, I moved upward for a wider view while staying level with Vess's position. The rib was thin enough that I could see both edges, and it ended in the distance. Vess could see it too.

Between us and that end, the worked center remained straight. The grooves and repeating cuts continued without turning. Whoever built the road had aimed it at the place where the known stone stopped.

Vess checked the sling, the sore pad, and the map. All remained usable. Then we followed the road toward the end of the world.

# Part V: The Missing Ending

## Chapter 25: Where the Work Stops

The road became better as the rib became worse. Natural stone narrowed beneath it, shedding rough pieces into the brightness. The worked center stayed level. Grooves ran along both margins, and the small cuts between them kept their measured spacing. A builder had forced consistency across a place that offered less stone with every body-length.

Vess traveled slowly because the rib tilted near its end. The safer surface lay inside the road cut, where old tools had taken away a ridge. A courier with a full sling would have used those same few flat widths. The construction had been made for passage, not ceremony.

Ahead, the stone seemed to end in open air. I moved to one side and saw that the final face did not resemble the broken tips of other ribs. It was too even. Light met a single pale plane from edge to edge.

Vess ordered me back before I could study it closely. That was reasonable. The map belonged to Vess's body, and the last stretch carried no room for both my examination and careful travel. I returned to the rear and reported only that the end looked flat.

The road continued until the rib held barely enough width for Vess. The body settled low, distributing grip across both sides. The sling was pulled against the newest joint so it could not swing. Vess advanced one pad at a time and tested each hold twice.

The map line remained strong. It did not fade as we approached the last stone. No fall sign closed it. At the distance of one body-length, Vess stopped and unfolded the hide against the road.

The unfinished line went on.

Vess compared the margin with the stone ahead, then folded the hide and completed the final approach. The leading pads reached the pale face. It rose straight across the road and the natural rib around it, as though a single decision had cut both.

There was no next grip. Vess anchored the rear half and touched the face along its lower edge. Dust had gathered where the vertical plane met the road, but the plane itself carried almost none. Nothing projected from it. Nothing had weathered into a ledge.

I moved beside the end after Vess had secured every pad. The open brightness began exactly at the face. We could look around its top and sides, yet there was no broken stone beyond it and no fallen section caught among lower ribs. The nearest western span lay too far away to connect with this road.

The plane was smoother than any worked surface we had seen. Road cuts carried grooves, small errors, and changes where tools had entered. This face held none of those. I found a shallow dark shape near the center and thought it was a mark until Vess cleaned it.

It was half of a fastening socket.

The socket had once been round. Its surviving curve entered the road side and ended at the pale plane. The missing half would have occupied stone beyond the end. A little farther down, an edge notch had been divided the same way. One side remained precise; the other stopped at nothing.

Vess checked each feature without accepting my first conclusion. We found three divided notches along one road margin and two on the other. Their intervals matched the complete cuts behind us. The flat end did not merely meet the construction. It passed through it.

That order mattered. If builders had begun at this face, they would have placed whole fastenings inside the available stone. No one would design half a socket for a brace that could not exist. The road and its fittings had continued when they were made. The pale plane came later.

Vess traced the surviving curve again. “The way did not fail here.”

The map showed a line without an ending because the route had possessed no ending when its pattern was established. Whatever removed the continuation had not taught the hide a new symbol.

I wanted to name the removal at once. Vess stopped me with one raised pad. We had proved that worked stone once extended beyond the plane. We had not proved who removed it, when, or how far the road had gone.

Vess placed the hide beside the half-socket. The unfinished line and the interrupted construction were visible together. That pairing was stronger than any story I might add.

Behind us, the outer road ran back toward the shoulder, Ilm, and a station where an incomplete map had cost Vess the work of a lifetime. Ahead of us, there was only brightness. We had reached the place where the world stopped, but the evidence said the road had not always stopped with it.

## Chapter 26: Zero Depth

Vess did not begin the return after finding the half-socket. A real route had been established, but real was not yet useful to anyone at the station. Terro had refused the map because its last line lacked a recognized condition. We needed to describe that condition without replacing it with an exciting guess.

We ate at the last complete width of road. Only two food packages remained, along with enough water for the route home if the return took no longer than the outward journey. Vess checked the hide while chewing and listed the endings a courier already knew.

A fall was wrong because the rib did not break or wear down into fragments. A transfer was wrong because no receiving stone lay within reach. A blind face was close, but those occurred where a route met a wall and the traveler could still turn along its surface. Here the wall possessed no depth and no side passage. The whole supporting rib stopped in the same plane.

“Then call it a cut,” I said.

Vess asked what had done the cutting. I had no answer. Tool marks would support ordinary cutting, but the plane carried none. Weather left rounded damage, scattered chips, and surviving weak pieces; none appeared. The surface was simply present wherever the western stone was not.

We tested whether the road might have been assembled against a separate slab. Vess cleared dust from the junction and found no seam. The rib's natural bands ran into the plane and stopped. Worked center, rough sides, and the underside all ended together.

Next Vess checked whether the missing half-socket could be damage from a broken fastening. The intact curve disproved that idea. A fastening pulled free would widen or chip its own edge. This curve remained smooth until the exact place where everything else stopped.

The station could understand those observations. Vess repeated them in working order, beginning with what any witness could see and ending with the conclusion they allowed. I interrupted twice with larger interpretations. Both times Vess made me return to the stone.

The plane bothered me for another reason. I had seen an edge like it before, though never passing through a road. At the place where I entered the Reach, there had been no tunnel extending between worlds. One world ended, the next began, and the distance between their boundaries did not behave like ordinary distance.

I moved past the pale plane.

From Vess's position, I appeared to cross into the brightness beyond the end. From mine, the face remained beside me without acquiring thickness. I moved west, then east, tracing the place where the stone ceased. No hidden back surface appeared. A cut block should have a face with stone behind it. This plane had a face and then absence.

Vess told me to explain what I was measuring. I said that any edge could be described by how far it continued through an object. A road cut had depth. A broken rib had depth. Even a thin wall occupied some interval between one side and the other. I was looking for the interval here.

There was none I could find.

Vess asked whether that meant very little or truly none. The difference mattered. I approached the face at different points: through the half-socket, above the rib, along the road center, and below the final stone. Each attempt gave the same result. I could identify the last place that belonged to the Reach. I could not find a thickness belonging to the ending itself.

“Zero,” I said, then corrected myself. “As close to zero as I can measure by moving.”

Vess made me repeat the limitation. My kind of movement could discover a boundary that ordinary gripping could not test, but I carried no calibrated tool and knew nothing about the scale of the road builders' craft. Zero was a result of my examination, not permission to erase uncertainty.

We considered what Terro could verify. The half-features required only sight and touch. The shared plane across road and rib required sight from several angles. My zero-depth result could not be repeated by a Reach courier whose body stopped at the face. It belonged in the account, but it could not bear the whole claim.

Vess arranged the facts by strength. First came the body-specific route from Ilm. Second came the worked road, frames, and repeated cuts. Third came the plane dividing completed construction. Last came my measurement, marked as the report of a visitor whose movement followed different rules.

The order reduced my most interesting result to the least transferable one. I disliked that for several breaths. Then I understood that evidence became useful by surviving the absence of the person most eager to explain it.

Vess looked again at the unfinished map line. It did not end at the pale plane, because an ending with zero depth might never have belonged to the route the hide knew. That was still an inference. Vess left the margin unchanged.

We had named no new symbol and solved no old history. We had something better for the journey home: an account that did not require Terro to believe me first.

## Chapter 27: A Closed Way

We could have left the plane unnamed. Vess had enough to defend the map: a usable western route, a constructed road, and physical features divided by a later boundary. That proof might restore the work Terro had suspended. My remaining question belonged to me.

Vess understood the difference before I explained it. “You think this is one of your ways between worlds.”

I said the boundary behaved like one. At every crossing I knew, movement did not consume an ordinary distance between the worlds. One condition ended and another began. The end face of the Reach had the same absence of intervening depth.

Vess asked whether matching behavior proved matching origin. It did not. Flatness alone could be coincidence, and my measurement relied on a body unlike any Reachfolk body. The road supplied a second fact: stone and construction had once continued where both now ceased. Together, those facts allowed an explanation. They did not force it.

I tried the explanation aloud with all its limits intact. The western road may have led to a crossing. If crossings were made rather than simply found, someone could have aligned a road with one. Later the crossing may have closed, removing access to everything beyond the plane while leaving the old route pattern on Vess's hide.

The thought changed the other crossings in my memory. I had treated them as places, peculiar but available, like openings in a wall. A closed crossing suggested construction, failure, choice, or age. It suggested that the network I used without understanding could possess a history.

None of that belonged on Vess's report. Vess listened, then separated the claims. A road once continued was proved. A no-depth boundary was observed by me. A world crossing at that boundary was my comparison. Deliberate making and later closure were explanations still seeking evidence.

I wanted the sequence to run the other way, beginning with the largest idea and using each physical detail as support. Vess began with what survived touch. The difference was familiar by now.

We examined the plane one final time for anything that could favor the crossing explanation. I moved along its full exposed edge, above and beneath the rib. Vess studied changes in the road cuts near the end. We found no maker's mark, no material laid into the stone, no unusual pattern beyond the divided fittings, and no second surface waiting behind the first.

The absence did not weaken what we had already proved. It stopped the proof where it should stop.

Vess folded the map and secured it beneath the light sling. The body had stiffened during the long halt. Before turning, Vess flexed the newer joint carefully and tested the sore pad against the road. Both remained usable. Water had to be rationed over the return, so there would be no more time spent making my theory elegant.

We backed along the narrowest stretch rather than attempting to turn at the face. Vess kept the leading grips fixed and reversed each rear section in order until the stone widened enough for the body to curve. I moved behind, watching the ties and reporting one loose food package.

At the fifth frame, Vess stopped to rest. The old bar held. From there, the pale plane had already disappeared beyond the road's gentle changes, but the route on the hide remained.

I asked whether Vess believed my crossing explanation. Vess said belief was not the immediate work. Terro needed a fact that changed the risk of accepting jobs from Vess. Customers needed to know whether a route without an ending meant false information inside the rest of the map. The old road answered those needs more directly than a history of worlds.

That answer was not a refusal of wonder. It placed wonder after food, return water, professional standing, and the safety of bodies carrying loads. On the outward journey, I had finally learned to stay beside Vess rather than racing ahead. I now attempted the same discipline with explanation.

The road may have ended at a crossing built by someone who understood more worlds than I did. The crossing may have been closed. Those possibilities would travel home as possibilities.

The half-socket would travel as proof.

## Chapter 28: What Another Body Can See

The return was not the outward journey reversed. Vess carried less food and water, which made several bends easier, but the long use of the new joint had accumulated. The sore pad remained clean and usable. Other pads began to tire from protecting it.

We slept once more on the outer road and reached the shoulder the following morning. Crossing back required Vess to lead with the opposite end. The first safe grip still lay beneath the far lip, hidden from the body until the leading pads had extended beyond recovery without help from the rear.

I reported the lower roughness. Vess found it on the second attempt, transferred weight across the gap, and rested flat on the shoulder for a long time. The changed body could complete the route in both directions. That fact mattered as much as reaching the plane.

During the rest, Vess studied the neighboring ribs rather than the map. Terro could not make the shoulder crossing. Semmit probably could not bend around the hidden grip. If neither could reach the road, the half-socket would remain our report and nothing more.

I offered to carry one of them. Vess rejected the idea for the same reason we had carried no essential supply. A station decision about ordinary courier safety could not depend on an untested flight with a living body.

The road itself was visible in pieces from the shoulder. Dust and distance made the worked center difficult to distinguish, but one standing frame crossed the stone at a clear angle. Vess asked whether another approach could improve the view.

I moved through the nearby lattice while Vess remained on the broad stone. A northern rib curved within sight of the outer road without meeting it. Its near end joined the shoulder network by a longer route. From most positions, layers of stone hid the western construction. Farther north, an opening formed between two descending spans.

Through that opening I could see the third frame, part of the fourth set of sockets, and the road's level band. The angle also showed the unnatural evenness of the final plane, though the half-features were too small to identify.

I returned and described the location. Vess needed to know whether the northern rib belonged to anyone else before it became evidence. The hide showed several routes onto it, including a long approach suitable for bodies without Vess's newest bend. The route did not reach the road. It reached a place from which the road could be seen.

We traveled partway toward the northern opening to test the relation from a Reachfolk position. Vess could not use the same approach Terro would take, because the personal map favored a shorter connection. At the viewing rib, Vess chose a stable rest and I moved into the exact line between watcher and road.

I pointed to the third frame. Vess found it after several attempts, then identified the straight worked band beneath it. The final plane appeared as a pale upright edge beyond the narrowing stone. Its lack of depth could not be verified from there, and the half-socket remained invisible. The road itself was unmistakable once located.

We marked the viewing position by features that did not rely on my direction: a forked rib above, three dark weather pits in the resting surface, and the last place where a far green strip could still be seen behind us. Vess rehearsed the route from Ilm to that point using Terro's known limits as far as the map allowed.

That did not guarantee Terro could reach it. Vess's hide could show only Vess's routes. The northern approach was common enough to appear on station records, but the final bends would still have to agree with Terro's body on the day of travel.

We kept the plan because it divided the evidence honestly. Terro might not touch the divided socket, but could independently see a road where the station had recorded none. Vess could report the final approach. I could describe the boundary measurement. Each claim would retain its witness and its limit.

The rest of the journey consumed the last food and nearly all the water. At the twisted slot, the smaller load passed in one trip, though Vess still stopped afterward to clean dust from the grip pads. The slanted rib remained difficult because fatigue had replaced the missing weight.

Ilm came back into view before its sounds reached us. Han stood at the green rows across the home gap and noticed our approach. The call that followed contained Vess's name, several questions, and anger at the time we had been gone.

Vess answered only the first question. “The line is real.”

Han did not celebrate. Han told Vess to drink, rest, and explain after the body stopped trembling. That order carried us through the final crossing home.

We had returned with no object from the end. The evidence stayed in the world where we found it. What we brought was a route, a sightline, and an account arranged so another body could begin checking it.

## Chapter 29: The Northern Witness

Terro came to Vess's frame after a full period of sleep, carrying the station slate and two blank hides. Han stayed across the gap within sight but did not join us. Vess could decide what the station heard and when.

The report lasted longer than the journey seemed to while we lived it. Terro recorded the load at departure, every change to it, the routes used in both directions, the condition of the new joint, the sore pad, the road features, the divided socket, and the pale plane. When Dot's observations entered the account, Terro placed them on a separate hide.

I objected until Vess explained that separation protected both reports. If my crossing theory failed, the road would still exist. If Vess had mistaken one worked feature, my measurement could still be examined on its own. A strong account did not force every claim to share one fate.

Terro asked the question Vess had prepared for: what could another courier check? Vess unfolded the western hide, showed the northern approach, and described the forked rib, three weather pits, distant green strip, and line of sight to the third frame.

Terro's own map did not show a complete route to that exact rest. Semmit's did. The approach took longer than Vess's path and arrived from above, but it avoided the shoulder transfer. Semmit agreed to go after hearing only the verification goal, not my explanation of crossings.

Vess wanted to accompany them. Terro refused because the body needed more recovery and because an independent sighting would carry more weight. Vess challenged the first reason, then accepted the second.

I traveled with Terro and Semmit after Vess asked me to point out the viewing features without leading them onto any unrecorded way. This time the journey belonged to two station bodies. Semmit chose the route. Terro checked rests and time against the slate. I answered only questions about the sightline.

The northern approach required four days outward. Semmit's route crossed wide ribs and several maintained roads, then left ordinary traffic near the Merrin elbow. Terro moved more slowly on the last day. At each rest, they compared maps and found different safe grips through the same stone.

We reached the forked rib late in a clear period. Three dark pits marked the resting surface, and the last narrow patch of green remained visible behind us. Vess's description had placed us correctly.

The western road did not reveal itself at once. Pale stone crossed pale brightness, and several nearer ribs interrupted the view. I told them where I had stood during Vess's test, then remained silent.

Semmit found the standing frame first. Terro followed its angle to the level band cut through the outer rib. They moved along the viewing rest until a closer span no longer blocked the fourth sockets. Both watched the road disappear behind a natural rise and return at the same level.

Terro asked whether any known road occupied that part of the station record. Semmit checked the route slate. None did. The nearest registered work ended well before the shoulder.

Farther west, the rib narrowed into the pale plane. At this distance, its smoothness could have been light. Terro did not claim more. The frame, sockets, and worked center were enough to establish construction where Vess said construction would be.

Semmit asked whether seeing the road proved every part of Vess's account. Terro said it proved the account had led independent witnesses to a real, unrecorded feature. The final plane still rested on Vess's observation, and zero depth rested on mine. Those limits did not make the verified fact smaller.

We stayed through one change of light to ensure the level band was not a shadow. The shadow shifted. The road did not.

Terro entered the sighting on the slate with Semmit as second witness. The language was spare: old worked route west of the shoulder, observed from the northern rest; frame and road cuts visible; direct access unavailable to both witnesses; correspondence with Vess's report confirmed.

I asked what that meant for Vess. Terro would decide at the station, in front of the people whose work had changed when the restriction was announced. The answer seemed slow after nine days of travel, but public harm required a public correction.

On the return, Terro asked me about the possible crossing. I gave the sequence Vess had established, beginning with the road and ending with deliberate closure as an explanation. Terro listened without adding it to the slate.

When Ilm finally came into view, Vess waited at the station rather than the home frame. Customers, carriers, and couriers moved around the job board as usual. Terro crossed the final familiar rib, placed the witnessed slate beside Vess's unfinished map, and said, “Your line reaches a real road.”

Vess did not ask whether that meant belief. Vess asked when the long work returned.

## Chapter 30: The Map Stays Open

Terro did not answer privately. The restriction had been spoken at the station, entered on its slate, and repeated by customers who adjusted their work around it. Removing a line in a quiet corner would not reach the same people.

Terro called the couriers together at the job board. Carriers waiting with parcels remained, along with two growers arranging a delivery and the sling mender who had met us on the outward route. No ceremony had been prepared. Ordinary work stopped long enough to hear a correction.

Terro read the original restriction first. It had barred Vess from jobs whose success depended on the disputed parts of the changing map. Then Terro read the new station finding: Vess had traveled the western line in both directions; the line reached an unrecorded worked road; two independent witnesses had observed that road from a route available to other bodies.

The final boundary remained under study. Terro said that plainly. The incompleteness no longer supported treating the usable parts of Vess's map as unreliable.

“Long work is restored,” Terro said. “The station will assign it by the same load, time, and route checks used for every courier.”

Vess asked for the statement to be added beneath the restriction rather than replacing it. Terro understood after a moment. If the first record vanished, the lost work would vanish with it. Both entries stayed on the slate, joined by the evidence that changed the decision.

Customers did not all respond at once. One grower moved a long eastern parcel back toward Vess's side of the board. Another kept a job already promised to Semmit. The restriction had rearranged habits for many days; its removal could not rearrange them in one breath.

Semmit chose the eastern job that required the old high bend and left the returned parcel for Vess. There was no sacrifice in the choice. Each courier took the work suited to the present body, which was what the station should have allowed from the beginning.

Vess inspected the eastern parcel, checked its weight against the hide, and accepted it for the next morning. The action looked smaller than the expedition. It restored pay, schedule, and professional trust, the reasons Vess had gone west in the first place.

Nib arrived after the announcement and demanded the entire account. Vess gave the useful version: the new joint made the shoulder transfer possible; the road was real; the last condition had no established symbol. Nib cared most about the transfer and tried to calculate whether another growth might make it available.

Vess refused to promise. A changed body could gain that route, lose another, or agree with the same stone in a different way. Nib unfolded a smaller hide and showed that the high bend lost weeks earlier had reappeared under a lighter school sling. The disappointment had not become permanent, and neither had growth become simple.

At home, Han had expanded the green rows across two brighter patches while we were gone. The new water channels stopped well before the gap. I offered to carry containers across, and Han accepted four trips after weighing how much could be safely lost if my hold failed. The fifth container remained on Han's side because its contents were needed there.

My ability had become one tool inside another person's decision. That was less dramatic than solving the gap and more useful.

Vess resumed long deliveries. The first run took longer than the old estimate because the route around the Fell had changed with the lighter sling. The second finished early. Customers began bringing distant jobs back to Vess's place at the board, though one continued to ask Terro for confirmation. Trust returned through repeated ordinary arrivals.

The western hide remained unfinished. Vess did not add a fall sign, wall sign, or crossing sign where none had been established. A note on the station copy referred to the old road, the northern sightline, and the plane. The line itself continued to the margin.

Terro organized a future survey for bodies that might reach more of the northern approaches. Nobody proposed rebuilding the outer frames yet. A road with no current destination did not become urgent because its history was strange.

I kept thinking about the divided socket and the ending with no measurable depth. If the crossing explanation was correct, the ways between worlds could close. They might also have been placed, aligned, or built. Every crossing I had passed through quickly now contained evidence I had failed to inspect because reaching the next world had seemed like the whole purpose.

When I prepared to leave the Reach, Vess came to the station with a long parcel secured in the working sling. The western journey had not made the new body finished. It had made one more route known.

I asked whether Vess wanted me to return if I learned how crossings were made. Vess said I should return if I had evidence and enough time to describe how I obtained it. The condition was fair.

Han called from the green rows that I should also return if I learned to carry water for longer than four seconds. Nib called a third condition that nobody could hear over the station traffic.

The way onward waited beyond the lattice. I could have entered it as I had entered the Reach, aiming at the next place and treating the boundary as empty distance. Instead, I went to its edge and stayed.

I examined where the Reach ended around it, where the next world began, and whether either side showed work. I moved above the opening, below it, and along every visible margin. Only after I had followed the crossing all the way around did I pass through.
