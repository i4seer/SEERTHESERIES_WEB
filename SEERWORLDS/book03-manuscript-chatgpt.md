The Unanswered

# Part I: A World That Watches

## Chapter 1: The Forest with No Leaves

I arrived beside a stone that went up farther than I could see.

That was my first fact. My second was that there were thousands of them. Pale columns stood across a level plain in rows that were not quite rows, with narrow ways between them and wider spaces where several ways met. Some columns were thin enough for a Kesh to put its limbs around, though I did not know what a Kesh was yet. Others would have filled the ground under a large house. Haze covered every top.

I went up to find the ends. The haze thickened, the plain faded, and the columns continued. I came down again because repeating a failed test without changing it is only another way of being stubborn. I can be stubborn, but I prefer to notice when I am doing it.

Light reached the plain from no direction I could name. I moved around the nearest column and found no shadow waiting behind it. Its surface looked the same on every side: chalk-colored stone beneath thin layers of something softer. There was no sun. There was no bright patch in the sky. Everything could be seen clearly anyway.

Then the wind strengthened, and the world made a sound. The nearest column gave a note too low for any small creature. Farther away, thinner columns answered higher. The pitch shifted as the air changed course through the gaps. It was not music, because no one had arranged it. It was not noise either. The whole plain seemed to be asking one long question without words, and I stayed where I was to listen.

You might think that means I had already decided I liked the place. I had not. Liking came later, after I had checked whether the stones moved and whether the sound warned of something large enough to eat travelers. I had visited worlds where the pretty part was bait. Still, I listened longer than either test required.

Something struck me from the side.

It bounced away, landed on six limbs, and slid until its broad belly nearly touched the ground. At first I thought its skin was changing in the light. Orange spread across a flat panel on its front, broke into green bands, then vanished beneath a rush of yellow grains. The rest of it stayed a quiet gray-green.

I moved back to give it room. The creature moved forward at the same time and almost hit me again.

“I am sorry,” it said.

There was no sound. The colors moved over the front panel, and I understood them as speech. I checked the air for a second voice and the stones for a reflection. The creature watched me check, getting brighter with every moment.

“I did not see you,” it added. “I could not have seen you. You are hardly anything.”

“I am enough to be struck.”

The colors stopped. Every patch of the creature turned a pale, empty shade. I waited, and when it did not move, I asked whether the collision had damaged it.

Color rushed back so fast that I lost the shapes inside it. “You answered.”

“You spoke first.”

“But where did yours happen?” The creature circled me. Its six feet tapped the hard ground in an uneven hurry. “You have no panel. You do not have a front. There is nothing to look at.”

I turned, although turning makes little difference when you are a dot. “My name is Dot. I speak from me.”

It came around again, searching for whichever side had produced the words. “Every side looks the same.”

“That saves time.”

The creature folded all six limbs and dropped onto the ground. Its front became such a confused storm of color that I could not understand any of it, but the shape of its body helped. It was laughing.

This was Tik. I did not know yet that Tik hated losing control of a color, that the plain had rules for every statement, or that one of those rules could change the thing being spoken about. I knew only that I had found a creature who could fall over because I talked. For a first meeting, that seemed promising.

## Chapter 2: Six Breaths

Tik recovered by announcing that the fall had been deliberate. The announcement came in careful gray-green bands. A bright orange spot appeared at one edge before Tik finished, spread across the entire panel, and spoiled the effect. “That is not fair,” Tik said.

When I asked what was unfair, Tik stood, planted all six feet, and tried again to become one even color. “You cannot see your own talking. Mine keeps happening before I want it to.”

Tik held gray-green while I counted. On the fourth breath, yellow trembled near the lower edge. At five, orange pushed through. Tik snapped around so I saw only a plain back.

“Six,” Tik said from the other side. I heard nothing. I had seen the shoulders move, but no meaning arrived with them.

Tik faced me again and asked whether I had received the word. “You turned away before six,” I said. The answer produced three colors at once. I was beginning to understand that Tik could tell me one thing in words and another thing by failing to keep a color. I did not yet know whether noticing both was polite.

Tik set off between the columns, and I followed. The Kesh we passed worked the pale covering from the stone with broad flat blades. Some carried bundled sheets on their backs. Three had climbed onto one another to reach a patch above them, making a six-legged tower out of three six-legged creatures. I stopped to count which feet supported which weight. Tik ran back when it noticed I was gone.

Tik asked what I was looking at. I told it I wanted to know whether the top worker could fall without bringing down the other two. “It can,” Tik said. “That is why nobody lets me be the top one anymore.”

The workers were mostly the same quiet color as Tik's back. When two needed to speak, they turned their fronts together. Fine colored marks crossed one panel and then the other. When the exchange ended, both returned to gray-green and resumed scraping. The control was exact enough that I began to see why Tik cared about six breaths.

I asked whether adults ever changed color by accident. Tik looked at an older Kesh carrying a heavy bundle. “Not where people can see. Grown Kesh choose when their fronts say something. They do not spill everything just because they are excited or angry or somebody says they turned before six.”

Orange appeared again. Tik ignored it with dignity, which would have been more convincing if the orange had not moved in circles.

Ahead, two workers approached each other in a narrow gap. One turned to give the other room. The second began speaking, saw only a back, and stopped. They shifted until their panels met across the passage. Only then did the sentence continue.

I moved behind Tik and said its name. Tik spun so quickly that two feet left the ground. “Do not do that.”

“You could still hear me.”

“Of course I could. Your speech cheats.” Tik walked around me again, trying to find the part that ought to aim the words. “If I show you my back, I am not speaking to you. If you show me your back, you are still exactly the same.”

“I have no back.”

Tik's entire panel sharpened. “That is the cheating part.”

Tik took me to a water channel cut below the level of the plain. Six Kesh stood inside it, clearing heavy grit with their blades. From the bank I saw six dull backs. Down in the cut, their fronts faced one another, flashing so steadily that the channel seemed lit from within.

They could all speak. Nobody above could receive a word. Tik explained that the channel crew did this all day and therefore knew everyone's business. I pointed out that the unseen conversation might be the reason. “It is because they work very hard,” Tik corrected, already turning an amused yellow.

One worker glanced up, noticed me, and flashed a quick pattern toward the others. Six fronts lifted together. Their interest arrived at once, too fast for me to separate into words.

I understood the practical rule then. A Kesh did not merely speak by making color. A Kesh chose an audience by placing a front where certain eyes could reach it. A turned body could close a conversation. A ditch could become a private room.

My words had no direction at all. Tik had been delighted by that when we were alone. With six channel workers staring up at us, delight gave way to a nervous wash of white.

“Can you speak to only me?” Tik asked. I had never needed to know.

## Chapter 3: The Size of a Claim

I tried to aim my next words. There was no part of me to turn, so I used attention instead. I fixed on Tik and asked whether the channel crew could receive me. Every worker looked up and answered yes in different colors.

Tik went white. I tried again, moving closer and thinking only of the small creature beside me. “Can you hear this?”

Tik's panel showed surprise, but so did all six workers. One of them climbed onto the bank and came near enough to look through me, which was not useful. Then she returned to the channel and reported that I had no speaking surface, as if I could not understand every color. Tik replied that my missing surface was the reason it had brought me.

The crew leader introduced herself as Dellek after correcting two other workers who tried to do it for her. She wanted me in the channel. I wanted to see the channel bottom, so this arrangement suited us both.

The cut was deeper than Tik was tall. Water covered only the center groove, sliding around banks of pale grit that had collected on both sides. The crew scraped the loose material into shallow trays, lifted the trays to the bank, and returned them empty. Their work kept the narrow stream moving. It was slow work, and there was much more grit than water.

“The level is low,” I said. All six fronts went blank. Tik's color vanished too. No stone cracked. Water did not leap into the channel. The wind continued to pass through the columns. Yet every Kesh had stopped, and that made my sentence feel larger than it had when I spoke it.

Tik came down the bank with more care than Tik usually gave any descent. “You cannot put a claim out like that.”

I pointed to the wet mark below the older line and the dry top of the exposed grit.

“That proves what you see here.” Tik's words broke into hard blocks of color. “You have not walked north. You do not know what is coming. You do not know when this channel was last cleared.”

Dellek added that a blocked bend could hold water out of sight. Another worker said the mark might belong to another season. A third disagreed about the season and began to explain the grain at the edge. Their debate did not make the channel deeper, but it did make my sentence smaller.

“It looks low here, compared with the mark, now.” Color returned to Tik in a weak green wash. Dellek approved the correction, though she said I had placed the words in the wrong order and sounded like a north-end wind listener.

I asked why the order mattered if the limits were all present. “People need to see the edge before they step on the claim,” Dellek said. “If you show them the edge afterward, they have already had to decide whether you meant the whole plain.”

That was my first useful lesson in a hedge. I had assumed hedging meant a speaker was frightened of being wrong. It did mean that, sometimes. It also meant the listener could see the exact ground beneath the statement before trusting any weight to it.

Tik had another reason for being frightened. We left the crew and followed the channel until the workers' fronts disappeared below the bank. Tik checked both directions, though any Kesh behind the nearest columns could still have seen us. “A Kesh can make a false thing real. If you know it is false and still say it to somebody, the world may change it.”

Tik did not know whether this happened every time or how long a change took. The lack of answers did not seem to weaken the rule for Tik. It made the rule large enough to cover any result.

Tik told me about water in the east that had become bitter after a family tried to keep neighbors away. Tik had not been there. No living Kesh Tik knew had been there. The water was bitter now, and the account of the lie had passed through more mouths than Tik could count.

I said they had bitter water and a story about its cause. Alarm flashed over Tik. “We have the reason it is bitter.”

“Those are not always the same thing.”

Tik turned its back on me. By then I understood that the movement closed the conversation, or should have. I could still speak into the plain and reach Tik without permission. For once, I did not. The wind filled the silence for us. When Tik faced me again, the front had settled into gray-green.

“Say what you know,” Tik told me. “Say how you know. Leave room for the part you do not.”

I looked back at the thin water moving between the grit banks. The channel still seemed low to me. Now I could see the space between that fact and what I had said.

## Chapter 4: Around the Bowl

The next morning, Tik took me to eat with one of its many family groups.

I say eat because that was what everyone else did. I had no mouth, stomach, or useful way to accept the pale strip offered to me. The cook knew this after Tik explained it twice, but she offered again anyway. I came to understand that food on Ashet could be a question even when nobody expected it to enter you.

The family sat in a ring with their fronts toward a shallow central bowl. That arrangement let every Kesh see every speaker. It also meant the meal stopped whenever somebody reached across the middle, because a limb could cover part of a sentence.

The pale food was scrape, the soft layer I had seen workers cutting from the columns. Fresh pieces bent without breaking and left fine grit on the bowl. Winter scrape was dried into hard sheets. Every Kesh at the meal complained about those sheets with the easy agreement of people who had complained together for years.

Blue-shelled thumblings had been cooked with channel greens and spread through the fresh scrape. Tik found one, lifted it as if it were treasure, and lost it into the bowl again. Three relatives changed color at once. Tik claimed the drop had been a test of the shell.

The talk turned to a nearby column that held thumblings beneath its crust. Two families claimed the column. The disagreement had lasted eleven years, which seemed enough time to decide where a stone stood and who worked around it.

I asked whose column it was. Every front in the ring lit at once. I received the beginning of six different histories, each limited by phrases about memory, family records, present sight, and what another Kesh might reasonably believe. The oldest diner raised one front limb. The others stopped.

“On what we have,” she said, “our claim appears stronger.”

“After eleven years, you must know.”

The ring changed to the same deep green. Nobody looked angry. They looked as if I had set a tool in the food.

The elder explained that certainty belonged to things under direct weight: the ground beneath her feet, the bowl she touched, the column against her side. Old marks could be read badly. A relative could remember wrongly. A stone placed by a dead Kesh could have moved. Eleven years gave both families more evidence and more chances to discover how much they lacked.

“Then the argument cannot finish,” I said.

“It can finish,” she replied. “One family can stop.”

None had.

The cook passed the bowl again. This time I watched what everyone did after taking a portion. Each diner turned fully toward her before speaking. One praised the balance of green to thumbling according to his own taste. Another said it seemed better made than the last batch she remembered, while admitting that memory might not include every batch. Tik announced that it liked this piece right now and might like the next one even more.

The words sounded weak to me. The colors did not. Warm gold spread through the cook's front, slow and even, until the whole panel glowed. She received each careful limit as proof that the praise belonged to the speaker and had not been thrown carelessly at the bowl.

I understood the warmth. I still thought the language took too long. I looked into the pot, inspected the greens and cracked blue shells, and said, “The meal is good.”

The gold disappeared from the cook. Two diners turned away. Tik slid lower until the rim of the bowl hid most of its front. “What?” I asked. “They all said the same thing.”

The elder followed me when the meal ended. She placed herself directly in my path, which I later learned was how a Kesh made sure a bodiless traveler understood that a conversation was not optional.

“They told the cook what happened inside them,” she said. “You told the food what it was.”

“I meant that I liked it.”

“Then that is the shape you should have given us.” Her panel held steady, but a thin disappointed brown stayed near one edge. “You made the cook responsible for proving your praise true to every tongue.”

I looked back. The family was clearing the bowl and stacking the flat serving pieces. The cook worked with them. Nobody had been harmed, and I had not lied. Still, the ease around the ring had changed because I wanted five fewer words.

On Ashet, a sentence did not belong only to the speaker. Everyone who received it had to carry its exact size.

## Chapter 5: The Truth About Tomorrow

Two columns from the meal, a family had gathered without food.

They sat in a tight half circle beside an old Kesh whose panel was almost colorless. Age had faded the rest of her too. The gray of her back looked thin against the stone, and each breath lifted only the front pair of limbs. One daughter held a cup near her, waiting for a movement that never came.

Nobody in the group spoke. That was unusual enough to stop me. Kesh could make silence with their backs, but all seven fronts faced inward. Each one was able to speak and had chosen not to.

Tik tugged at the air beneath me with one limb, forgetting there was nothing to catch. “We should go around.” I followed. At the next column I asked what had happened, and Tik said the old Kesh was going pale.

The phrase meant more than color. As Kesh aged, their panels lost range. Fine shades vanished first. Later, only broad simple bands remained. Near the end, a speaker might have many thoughts and too few colors to place them where anyone else could receive them.

When I asked whether she was dying, Tik gave me the startled look of a creature who had already spent a morning teaching me about the size of claims. “It seems likely from what I saw.” I accepted the correction, then asked why nobody had promised that she would be all right.

Tik stopped walking and asked what evidence the family could use. I said they could be kind. “Kind is not the same as true.” Tik's panel turned a troubled blue. “Tomorrow has not happened. You cannot stand anywhere inside it. There is no safe edge to show first.”

I looked back between the columns. The daughter had lowered the cup. A broad gold band moved slowly across her front toward the old Kesh, and the shape repeated twice. Tik told me the words meant, “I hope you stay.”

That was allowed. Hope described the daughter, not the next morning. She could say how much she wanted, how frightened she was, what she remembered, and what she would do. She could not turn any of those things into a promise about what the old Kesh's body would manage.

I went back far enough to see without entering the circle. The daughter told her mother that the screens were tied, the water jars were filled, and the last scrape bundle had been stored dry. She named everything finished that could be finished, then showed the gold pattern again: I hope you stay.

The old Kesh answered with a faint band I could not read. The family leaned closer. One of them adjusted a screen to keep the wind from moving across her front, and another shifted the cup into easier reach. Their care was exact. It still could not supply the missing sentence.

You may think I wanted them to lie. I did not. I had been warned that a false statement might reach into the world and force an answer, and dying seemed a poor subject for experiments. What I wanted was for the truth to have another shape available. I wanted a sentence that could comfort without claiming tomorrow.

The Kesh had looked for that sentence much longer than I had. Hope reached the old Kesh; she turned the little color she had toward her daughter. The daughter stayed, and the family understood. But hope could not make tomorrow safe or give back the promise they needed.

Tik waited beside me until the wind eased. Its front had gone a steady gray-green, and I thought at first this meant control. Then a narrow blue line escaped near one shoulder and remained there.

I asked whether Tik knew her. She used to slip Tik dried thumblings after everyone else said it had eaten enough. When I asked whether they were good, Tik looked at me with proper offense. “They were dried thumblings.”

The answer was so quick that I understood the rule without a hedge: some facts on Ashet were safe because nobody would argue with them.

We moved on, but I kept hearing the daughter's limited promise behind us. A whole world had built its language around the danger of saying too much. The cost appeared in what love had to leave unsaid.

## Chapter 6: The Empty Circle

The settlement had one place where the columns stopped. I noticed it from above: a wide bare circle cut into the pale forest, open enough that no Kesh could stand in the middle and hide a front behind stone. Paths entered from every side. The ground had been worn smooth by many feet, though only one Kesh stood there when Tik and I arrived. Tik halted before the edge.

When I asked why the Kesh stood alone, Tik dimmed until its colors were hardly stronger than its back. “That is the speaking ground. You only cross the line when you mean to be received by anyone who comes.”

The Kesh in the circle was larger than Tik and held a warm brown-gray without any visible strain. She faced one path, then another, letting each passerby see her front. Every passerby looked. Most turned away too carefully afterward.

I asked what she was saying. Tik said nothing at the moment, then failed twice to explain why she remained there. Its front showed caution, embarrassment, and something sharper. I could have asked the channel crew. I could have followed a passerby. Instead I crossed the boundary.

The open ground changed the sound of the world. With no nearby columns, the wind lost its close notes. The larger hum remained beyond the circle, making the silence in the middle feel chosen.

The Kesh turned toward me before I reached her. Unlike the others, she did not search for my front. She watched the point that was all of me.

“You are the speaker that does not show speech,” she said. I gave my name. “Ilva,” she replied, without changing her level front. “Can you make a lie?”

I had expected questions about travel, body, food, or where my voice came from. On other worlds, creatures often ask whether I am dangerous only after the less useful questions. Ilva began with the danger.

“I do not know.” For the first time, her color changed. It did not brighten or leak. Every mark simply held in place, as if the entire panel had stopped between one word and the next.

Ilva wanted the limit made clear. I could form false words, but I had not tested what followed on Ashet. I had never knowingly lied to a Kesh. “Then do not test it for me,” she said.

That answer made me more interested than a request would have. I asked why she had brought up lying if she did not want one.

Ilva looked past me. Tik remained outside the circle, showing enough alarm for both of us. Several other Kesh had slowed on the paths. None had entered, but Ilva and I were visible from every direction.

“This is not where I would explain it,” she said. I pointed out that she had chosen the place. “I chose to be seen speaking to you.” Her words were flat, without the limits everyone else attached. “If I led you behind a screen first, the hiding would become the only fact anyone remembered.”

I checked the sight lines. She was right about those. Any Kesh near the open could see that she had approached a stranger and asked a dangerous question, though they could not know what I said unless I addressed them too. That difference had not mattered to me until then.

Ilva asked me to visit the channel workers the following day. I was to ask what had happened in the speaking ground four years earlier. I was not to tell them she had sent me.

I called the request hiding and pointed out what she had just arranged in the open. “I arranged for everyone to know that we spoke,” Ilva said. Her brown-gray front remained steady. “I did not arrange for them to own the words.”

There were several reasons to refuse. She had offered no explanation. She wanted me to conceal her part. Every Kesh who passed us seemed to know why she stood alone, and none would meet her front for long.

Those were also the reasons I agreed.

Tik waited until we had left the circle and put three columns between us and Ilva. Then it asked what she had wanted. I could have told Tik. My words would have reached it no matter which way either of us faced.

I told Tik that Ilva had given me a question. Impatient orange filled its front as it demanded to hear the question too. I moved toward the channel. “She did not give it to you.”

It was my first deliberate act of privacy on Ashet. I liked how exact it felt. I had not yet learned that carrying someone's secret can let that person choose your road.

# Part II: The Hand Behind the Route

## Chapter 7: Four Years Earlier

Dellek received Ilva's question before I asked it.

I reached the channel the next morning and found the crew lifting wet grit from a bend. They had braced a tray across the cut. Three workers filled it from below while the other three pulled it along the bank. Their colors moved beneath the tray in a conversation hidden from everyone but one another.

Dellek saw me, passed her rope to another worker, and turned her front up. “You found Ilva.” I corrected the direction of the finding. The whole crew changed color. Nobody asked how Dellek knew. That seemed to count as answer enough among them.

I went into the channel and helped by staying out of the way, which was the only work available to a creature with no limbs and no weight. The crew disagreed. They placed me over the center groove so I could warn them when floating clumps came around the bend. This did not require strength, but it gave me a reason to face upstream while they spoke.

I asked what Ilva had done in the open ground four years before. The next clump passed under me because every worker stopped. Dellek caught it with her blade. “She gave a false statement.”

When I asked whether everyone knew it was false, Dellek called that only one possible account. I asked for the other.

Dellek scraped the rescued clump into the tray. She said Ilva had spoken plainly, without any hedge, before enough witnesses to fill the paths. Ilva claimed to have moved a boundary marker. Other evidence pointed elsewhere. The statement should have been either a confession or a lie, but the world had not supplied a clear answer.

I asked what change followed. No worker named one. That absence had not cleared Ilva. It had stretched the doubt. Some Kesh believed she had moved the marker and admitted it. Others believed she had lied for a reason nobody could prove. A third group expected the consequence to arrive later and did not want to be nearby when it did.

“Four years is a long delay,” I said.

“The eastern water stayed sweet for longer than that after the old lie,” one worker replied.

I asked whether the worker had seen the water change. She had not. Neither had her mother. Her mother's mother had heard the account from someone whose name had been lost. When I pointed out the distance between the speaker and the evidence, she threw a lump of grit through me. It struck Dellek instead, and work stopped again for an argument about aim.

Once the tray was moving, I asked for other changes. The crew gave me a bridge that had become weak after someone called it strong. They described a child declared dead who later died. Each account had survived because its result frightened the next listener, but none came with a living witness to the sentence and the change.

I asked for a recent case. Dellek's front went dull. “Recent does not make a thing safer to tell.”

That was the first time she refused me. I had mistaken the crew's hidden conversation for a willingness to share everything. Privacy let them speak. It did not make the speech mine.

I returned to my place above the groove and watched for clumps. The next one came slowly enough for the worker below to push it apart before it caught on anything. I gave the warning anyway, because I had been assigned the task.

Near midday, Dellek climbed out and asked what I was giving Ilva. I said I would give her what I found. “That was not the question.”

I looked at the six fronts angled toward me. They had answered more than they wanted and now needed to know where the answers would travel. I said I would tell Ilva what they had told me about her own statement and the old public accounts. I would not repeat the recent case Dellek had refused to name.

Dellek returned to the cut. “That seems acceptable to me, given what I have heard.” It was the first time a hedge sounded like trust.

I carried three useful facts away. Ilva had made an unhedged claim about the marker. No clear change had followed. The stories everyone used to prove the world's rule were old enough that memory could not be tested. I also carried a refusal. At the time, I thought the facts mattered more.

## Chapter 8: One Listener

I learned to aim my voice while six Kesh were arguing over a rope.

The rope belonged to the channel crew, but one end had been left on the bank and two passing workers had used it to tie a load of scrape. Dellek wanted it back. The workers wanted time to carry the load home. Everyone had a reasonable claim, so naturally the discussion had become impossible.

One of the passing workers stood behind three others. I needed to ask whether the bundle could be lifted without the rope, but four fronts were moving between us. I fixed my attention on her the way I had fixed it on Tik beside the channel. This time I thought not only of the creature I wanted, but of excluding every other front.

“Can the load stand without the tie?” I asked. The hidden worker dropped her end. Nobody else reacted. Dellek kept arguing. Tik watched Dellek. The worker stared straight through the moving bodies toward me, her panel white around the edges.

I asked again, still holding the narrow line of attention. She answered that the load would stand if two sheets were moved. No one turned. I had spoken across a crowded path to exactly one Kesh.

Then the worker told everybody. The argument about the rope ended because all six wanted me to speak privately to them. Dellek insisted that curiosity did not release the rope, but she said this while shifting into my path, which made her position unclear. Tik demanded to go first on the grounds that the original idea had been Tik's. It had not been, though correcting that would have taken longer than the test.

I addressed Tik alone. “You turned before six yesterday.” Tik flashed bright orange. The others saw the color and wanted the words. Tik refused to give them, and the refusal pleased it enough to produce yellow loops over the orange. Privacy had become something I could hand to one listener.

Ilva heard about it before I reached her home. Her shelter stood between three close columns with woven screens tied across the gaps. Each screen could be shifted to open one sight line and close another. I had thought Kesh built them against weather. The wind passed through freely. The screens controlled audiences.

One gap on the northwest side remained open. Anyone crossing the nearest path could look inside and see Ilva's front. She saw me examining it and said she owned another screen. I called the opening deliberate. “The opening is observed,” she corrected.

Ilva had learned to separate those two ideas better than anyone I had met. She explained that if she closed the final gap, neighbors would call it evidence that she hid her speech. Nobody could know what she said behind a screen, so the screen itself would become a claim about her.

I said she had less privacy because others expected her to misuse it. Ilva allowed that this was close enough.

I told her what the channel crew had said. I named Dellek's refusal too. Ilva did not ask for the hidden case. She asked whether I had repeated her name before Dellek used it. I had not. Her control remained perfect, but one band crossed the lower panel and disappeared.

Then she asked for the private voice. I narrowed my attention until the open gap, the path beyond it, and the Kesh passing there no longer belonged to the sentence. “Only you can receive this.”

Ilva changed. No large color spread across her. A small orange mark appeared in the center of her panel and stayed there, bright and still. I used the same private line to ask whether it hurt.

“No.” She looked toward the open gap, then back at me. “When I was small, adults screened a corner for one child at a time. Someone could tell you a thing that did not belong to the whole family. Nobody has spoken to only me since then.”

I wanted to ask why. The open gap answered part of it. The rest belonged to four years of strangers turning carefully away.

“It feels like receiving an object,” Ilva said. “I know where it is. I know it was meant for me.”

I had treated privacy as control over a route: this voice reaches that creature and no other. Ilva received it as ownership. My ability had not changed. What it cost to use it had.

Outside, a neighbor slowed near the northwest gap. Ilva faced the path until the neighbor continued. Only then did she ask me to return the next day.

I left by the open side. Closing a screen would have been easy for any Kesh with limbs. Leaving it open required work from Ilva every time somebody passed.

## Chapter 9: The Kesh Who Steps In

Virr introduced herself by knocking another Kesh sideways.

The collision happened beside a disputed boundary stone. Two families had gathered in a crooked circle, careful to keep every front visible. One speaker's colors had grown broad and simple. I had learned enough by then to recognize the change: he was leaving the small exact shapes of a hedge and moving toward a flat claim.

Virr came off the bank at speed. She placed her whole body between his panel and the circle. The sentence continued behind her, but nobody could receive it. Every witness looked toward the columns with such sudden interest that I checked to see whether something had fallen. Nothing had. Their looking away was part of the work.

Virr stayed in place until the speaker's colors settled. Then she stepped aside, apologized for interrupting, marked the limit of what she had observed, and apologized again in case her first apology had claimed too much. The other family began a reply. Eleven minutes later, Virr blocked them too.

I waited until the gathering broke apart. Virr was older than Ilva, with a dark line across one shoulder and a front so controlled that colors appeared with hard clean edges. Her back legs trembled after the second interruption. She sat without mentioning them and pulled a strip of dried scrape from a pouch.

“You prevent lies,” I said.

“I prevent audiences.” Virr bit through the strip and worked at it longer than fresh food should require. “I cannot see what anyone knows. I can see when a statement is becoming too large for the room.”

Her title was truthkeeper, though punishment did not belong to the job. She watched gatherings, arguments, namings, and public work. When a speaker pushed toward a dangerous assertion, Virr removed the listeners by placing herself in the way. If no one received the colors, no lie had been spoken.

I asked how often she was certain. Virr looked at the food in her limbs and declared that it tasted bad. When I objected that this did not answer me, she broke off another piece. “It is the only flat fact I intend to offer while eating. Everyone agrees about winter scrape.”

I liked her after that. Virr did not return the feeling at once.

She had been a truthkeeper for nineteen years. I asked how many times she had stepped between a speaker and an audience. Her front showed a small embarrassed brown before she controlled it. She admitted to making one mark for each interruption and keeping the tally at home. She would not give the count. “There are enough to prove I have spent nineteen years making marks.”

The answer was a refusal with a small door left open. I asked whether I could see the tally instead of asking her to count it for me.

Virr chewed. Wind moved through the nearest columns, and the pitch dropped as it strengthened. She watched three Kesh leaving the gathering. Even while talking to me, she kept each public front inside her view.

Virr said Ilva had sent me. I corrected her: Ilva had told me where to find her. Virr called that a hedge she might accept from another speaker. I told her it was exact truth from me.

Virr folded the remaining scrape and returned it to the pouch, which showed that even she had limits. She invited me to see the marks the next day, on the conditions that I not take or alter them and not decide their meaning before I understood the count. I said they counted interruptions. “They count my belief that an interruption was needed,” she corrected. Her back legs took a moment to steady when she rose. “If you cannot hear the difference, Ilva has chosen badly.” Then she left before I could answer.

The boundary argument had ended without a decision. Both families lifted their bundles and took the same property question home. Virr had solved nothing they came to solve. She had kept one dangerous sentence from joining the dispute and making it larger.

I had been looking for facts about Ilva. She had sent me to a Kesh whose life's work was the space between a fact and the decision to speak it.

## Chapter 10: Three Pieces

Tik broke the jar while trying to jump from one low stone ledge to another. There was no reason to make the jump. A clear path ran around the column and would have taken eight more steps. Tik had seen two older Kesh cross the gap carrying empty baskets and decided their method needed improvement.

The jar was not empty.

Tik struck it with a rear foot on takeoff. The jar tipped from the ledge, dropped past Tik, and broke into three large pieces on the hard ground. Water spread under the nearest column and disappeared into the dust.

Tik landed safely. Its front confessed before its feet stopped sliding. Orange guilt covered the panel, crossed by white fear and small frantic green shapes that never became words. I asked whether Tik was hurt. It checked all six limbs and said no.

A Kesh came around the column. She carried a bundle of green cuttings and stopped when she saw the water. Her attention moved from the pieces to Tik's front. Tik tried to hold gray-green. The attempt lasted less than one breath.

“Who broke my jar?” she asked. I knew the answer. I knew that she was looking at the answer. I also knew Tik would have to say it aloud, and I did not want that to happen.

“The jar was already broken when Tik arrived,” I said. The owner's front lost every color. Tik's did the opposite. I made the lie larger because the first sentence left room for Tik to have found the jar during its fall. “I saw the pieces before Tik jumped. Tik did not break it.”

There was nothing on me to betray the falsehood. No orange patch arrived. No edge trembled. The owner looked through me as if a hidden panel might appear if she waited long enough.

Tik had not learned to hide anything. White fear covered the orange now, but fear could mean many things around a broken jar and a stranger's flat claim. The owner turned from one of us to the other. Then she gathered the three pieces, tucked them beside the greens, and walked away without thanking either of us.

Tik waited until she vanished between the columns. “You made a lie for me.” I had. The fact felt different after the owner left. While she stood there, the lie had been a quick barrier placed between Tik and a consequence. Now it remained in her mind, where she would have to fit it beside what she had seen on Tik's front.

“Something may change,” Tik said. We inspected the pieces that were no longer there, the damp ground, the ledge, and Tik's limbs. The jar did not return. Its water did not rise from the dust. Tik remained the creature that had struck it.

We waited through a long shift in the column song. Two workers passed. A family carried bundled scrape toward the west paths. Nothing about them changed. No crack opened. No replacement jar appeared.

I suggested that perhaps the world answered only Kesh. Tik rejected the conclusion at once. We had one lie, one speaker unlike every other speaker on the plain, and no time limit. I was about to make the same large claim Tik had corrected at the channel.

That annoyed me. It also proved Tik had taught me something.

The owner returned near evening. For one moment I thought the world had sent the consequence back in her limbs. She carried the broken pieces and a binding strip. She set them near Tik.

“If the jar was broken before you found it,” she said, “you can still help bind it.” Tik looked at me. I had no front to turn away, but I could refuse to speak into that moment. Tik lowered its body beside the pieces and held them together while the owner wound the strip around the outside.

My lie had prevented a confession. It had not prevented the work. Nothing impossible happened that day, but I recorded the case anyway: one deliberate falsehood, witnessed by two Kesh, followed by no change I could find.

Under the record sat a feeling I had not expected. I was relieved.

You only feel relief after danger has seemed possible. My first test had begun before I admitted I was running one.

## Chapter 11: A Pleasant Kesh

Onnu brought a second strip of binding for the jar. He arrived the following morning while Tik and the owner were checking whether the first strip had dried tight. His base color was a mild green-gray, and the shapes of his speech moved slowly enough for Tik to follow without asking him to repeat. He praised the fit according to what he could see, offered a better knot, and asked permission before touching anything. Tik adored him. That became visible before anyone explained who he was.

Onnu was Ilva's brood-kin. He was also the Kesh protected by whatever she had said four years earlier. I had assembled an idea of him from Ilva's isolation, Dellek's caution, and Virr's suspicion. The idea was much more dramatic than the creature helping Tik secure a cracked water jar.

“You are kind,” I told him. Onnu shifted the binding through his front limbs. “I am being kind now.” He had hedged the praise for me. I noticed and did not enjoy it.

Onnu asked about other worlds. He listened to the answers instead of using each one to prepare a new question. When I said I could not eat, he offered me fresh scrape, remembered his mistake before I answered, and apologized without turning the apology into work for me. He helped the jar owner carry water in two smaller vessels while the binding dried.

I asked what Ilva had done for him. His colors warmed as he said she had made a choice when he needed one. When I asked whether her statement had been true, Onnu checked the repaired jar. The binding had not moved, but he pressed it again. “The work seems sound.”

He did not turn away from me. He redirected attention to the thing in his limbs, a method I had seen Dellek use with grit. I could have asked again. Instead I watched him give the jar back.

That restraint lasted less than an hour.

Two families found me near the third column in the west line. Each had run out of relatives willing to hear the boundary case, and both thought a traveler would be neutral. They showed me a marker stone, old scrape cuts, foot tracks, and family records pressed into thin dried sheets. They spoke for so long that the wind changed pitch twice.

Their hedges did not hide disagreement. They sharpened it. One family believed the marker had been placed before the oldest living memory. The other believed someone had shifted it. Neither claimed to know who. Both supplied careful reasons why the other family's account deserved less weight.

I measured from the marker to the nearest worked column on each side. One distance was thirty-one paces. The other was forty. “The column belongs to the nearer family,” I said. The discussion stopped. Both groups stared at me.

I explained the measurement. They already knew the distances. One Kesh said, with great care, that nearness had never been the agreed rule. Another added that accepting it now would change twelve other boundaries. The family I had favored looked almost as offended as the one I had rejected.

I reminded them that they had asked for an answer. One elder corrected the claim: they had asked what I thought. When I insisted that I had told them, she said, “You placed it on the ground as a fact.”

The two families resumed their argument, united for a brief useful moment by their dislike of my method. Neither thanked me. The column remained disputed.

Tik heard about it before I found a quiet place. It laughed so hard that it failed to show any readable word for several breaths. Onnu heard too. He asked whether I still believed the column belonged to the nearer family. When I said yes, he replied, “Then perhaps you helped yourself.”

That was more annoying than Tik's laughter because it was more accurate. I had wanted the measurements to finish the case. When they failed, I blamed the families for asking the wrong kind of question.

That evening, I wrote down everyone I had spoken to since meeting Ilva. I kept such lists because worlds became easier when the encounters stayed in order. Dellek. Virr. The meal family. The pale elder's daughter. The jar owner. Onnu. Two boundary families.

Beside Onnu's name, I wrote pleasant and evasive. Both descriptions fit. I was beginning to dislike facts that did not cancel one another.

## Chapter 12: The Chosen Route

The list bothered me because the order looked too clean.

Before Ilva, I had followed Tik. Tik led me to the channel, the meal, and the dying elder. After Ilva, I believed I had begun choosing for myself. Yet the next names were Dellek, Virr, Onnu, and the two families who had found me beside their disputed column.

Ilva had named Dellek and Virr. That much was open. Onnu could have brought binding because he was kind to Tik. The boundary families could have heard about my measurements from anyone. A list is only a list until another fact gives it shape.

I went back to the third west-line column. One elder from the losing side was scraping fresh crust near the marker. She turned her front toward me with the careful patience of a Kesh prepared to hear another ruling.

I asked how the families had chosen me. The elder said Ilva had described me as a traveler who measured before deciding. Ilva had done this the afternoon before they approached me. She had not known what answer I would give. She had known they would supply a long dispute and that I would try to reduce it.

At the channel, Dellek admitted that Ilva had asked her to tell Onnu I was near the broken jar. Dellek had done so without asking why. “There are many reasons to help with a jar,” she said, which was true and did not answer mine.

The pattern did not include Tik or the owner. Tik's jump had chosen them. It did include every conversation that had felt like progress.

I found Ilva scraping a column beside her home. She worked alone, bracing the flat blade against the crust with two front limbs and pulling down while four feet held her close to the base. Fresh sheets collected on a mat beneath her. She saw me coming and continued.

I asked whether she had chosen my route. Ilva admitted choosing parts of it. I named Dellek, Virr, Onnu, and the boundary families; she confirmed every one. Then I asked why she had used the families.

Ilva pulled another sheet free. “You use certainty as if it has no weight. I wanted you to feel other creatures refuse to carry it.”

That answer angered me because the lesson had worked. She had sent two families into my path and let me fail in front of them so I would be easier to speak with afterward.

“You handled me.” Ilva set the blade down and agreed. She could have softened it. Any other Kesh on the plain would have surrounded the admission with what she believed, what she intended, and which part she could not know. Ilva gave me the hard center and waited.

I asked how long she would have continued. “Until you asked this question, or until you left.” I said she could have told me what she wanted.

“Could I?” Her front remained calm. “You had been here four days. You believed a measurement settled ownership and a good taste belonged to a pot. If I told you I was suspected of a lie and asked you to hear me fairly, what would you have done?”

I would have asked whether she lied. If eleven Kesh had answered before she did, I would have counted the answers. I might have called that fairness because every voice received the same weight.

I accused her of choosing favorable voices. “I chose voices that would give you enough time to become difficult to fool. That is favorable to me. It is also what happened.” Both facts could stand together. I disliked that too.

I moved above the mat and counted the scrape sheets. Ilva waited through all fourteen, perhaps because she knew counting was how I made space around an answer. I said she had used me. She agreed. “I am still here,” I told her. The first color escaped Ilva's control. A small gold line crossed her front and vanished.

Remaining did not make the steering harmless. Leaving would not have made the things I learned untrue. I had no clean answer, so I asked for the one thing she had been arranging me to hear.

Ilva picked up the blade. “Come inside. I will tell you what I want.”

# Part III: The Lie Ilva Wants

## Chapter 13: Questions Left in Houses

Ilva took me behind two screens and left the northwest gap open.

The arrangement hid her front from the nearest paths while allowing anyone who crossed at the wrong angle to see part of the room. She had chosen privacy with an opening wide enough to prove she was not seeking complete privacy. I would once have called that pointless. Now I understood how much work lived inside the gap.

Ilva set her blade against a column and sorted the fresh scrape sheets by thickness. The work kept her limbs occupied while she spoke.

For four years, she had begun every day by looking for an answer from the world. A roof fell during winter, and she asked herself whether her statement had pushed it down. A child became sick. A column cracked in a hard wind. Water failed to reach a garden row. Each event came with a private calculation: ordinary trouble, or collection.

I suggested asking each family what had changed. Ilva wanted to know what they could inspect. I named the time, the place, and what happened before and after.

Ilva placed a thin sheet on the wrong pile, noticed, and moved it. “Suppose I visit the family whose roof failed. I ask whether my lie broke it. There is no mark in the wood that says yes. I leave without an answer, and they keep the question.”

The question would enter their evening meal, their repairs, and the next storm. A family that had lost only a roof would also have to wonder whether Ilva had selected them for punishment. Asking eleven households would create eleven new versions of the fear while proving none.

I understood why she had never asked. “I asked myself,” Ilva said. “That was sufficient to damage one home.”

I followed the logic. It was careful and cruel. The only creature able to search without placing a visible question in each house was the creature with no front and a voice that could reach one listener.

I said she wanted me to ask for her. Ilva finished sorting the last sheet. “I want you to find what my statement did. Asking may be part of that. I do not know.”

She did not tell me what answer to bring back. She did not ask me to clear her. After steering me through six other creatures, she was finally giving me a question with no chosen route beyond it.

I should tell you why I agreed, because a better version of me would have agreed from kindness. I was not that version. Ilva's problem was the best problem on the plain. It joined an old public act, a rule nobody could measure, evidence spread through private grief, and a speaker who had already proved she could move me without my noticing. I wanted to solve it.

I also wanted to know whether my jar lie belonged in the same count.

I told her the private voice still left the question with its listener; it only prevented the next creature from watching it arrive. I might be unable to search without causing the same harm she had avoided. “Then do not search that way,” Ilva said.

She did not offer comfort or tell me I would find another method. The boundary remained mine.

I asked what she would do with the answer. Ilva's panel stayed flat. “Wake with it.” What came after waking would depend on the answer itself.

I did not like the space in that answer. Neither of us could make a promise about a tomorrow we had not reached.

Outside the open gap, Tik ran past chasing another young Kesh. Both fronts flashed uncontrolled color. They did not see us behind the screens. Their game crossed the path and vanished among the columns.

Ilva watched the empty opening. “I know I used you.” I told her that saying so did not change the act. She agreed.

“It also does not stop me from choosing now.” The gold line appeared again, held for one breath, and went away.

I agreed to investigate on two conditions. I would decide whom to question. I would not put Ilva's name or fear into another home unless the evidence required it and the listener chose to continue.

Ilva accepted both. This time, when I left through the northwest gap, the route belonged to me.

## Chapter 14: A Witness Who Was Not There

I expected Ilva to let me begin. Instead, before I crossed the next row of columns, she called me back. She had moved from behind the screens to the open side of her home, where anyone on the northwest path could see her front. “There is another way,” she said. The sentence arrived flat. Her control hid nothing because she had chosen to hide nothing.

Ilva wanted me to enter the speaking ground while the settlement watched. I was to say that I had been present on the night the marker moved. I was to say I saw Ilva lift it from the ground.

I reminded her that I had not been on Ashet four years earlier and therefore could not have seen her. “That is why it would be a lie,” Ilva said.

I had known what she meant from the first sentence. Making her say it felt necessary anyway. A request can appear smaller while it stays implied.

Ilva's case depended on an empty space. Everyone had seen her make the statement. No one had seen the marker move. If she had moved it, her words were confession. If she had not, they were a deliberate falsehood. Four years of argument had failed because the same public act supported both readings.

A witness would close the empty space. No Kesh could become that witness. A false memory would show itself in color before the first sentence finished. Even a skilled speaker would have to stand in the open with every truthkeeper watching every edge.

I had no edge.

“They have practice reading bodies,” Ilva said. “They have no practice reading you.” I pointed out that they might refuse to believe a voice with nothing attached. Ilva accepted that risk. I said the lie could therefore cost me and change nothing for her. She held my attention and agreed.

She had spent four years building the request. She knew which path each answer opened and had already walked it alone. I did not mistake that preparation for certainty. She thought the lie might work because Kesh tied truth to visible control, and I was a speaker no visible rule could test.

I asked why she could not give the old statement another meaning. “Everyone received it. Meaning is not missing. A witness is missing.”

The logic was narrow enough to hold. It was also a plan to change public belief through a false account. Ilva had taught me how questions could remain in houses. Now she wanted to place one false answer in every house at once.

I moved into the northwest opening and looked toward the speaking ground. From there I could see only one curve of its bare edge between columns. The distance was short. The statement would be shorter.

I reminded Ilva that she had given me the route. “You will choose,” she said without retreating. “This is one road I am showing you. I am not carrying you into it.”

I asked who knew she meant to make the request. Nobody. Not Onnu, Virr, Dellek, or Tik. If I refused, Ilva said she would not tell them. If I accepted, the speaking ground would tell everyone.

That gave me privacy, but it also left no one beside me who could measure what she was asking. I understood why she had chosen the private voice before choosing the request.

Ilva told me to think first. I said she already knew the answer she wanted. “Wanting is not the same as being owed.”

I left her with the screens open and went toward Virr's house. I still had permission to see the truthkeeper's tally. The marks could not decide whether I should lie, but they belonged to work done before I arrived. I wanted one piece of the problem Ilva had not arranged for me that morning.

## Chapter 15: The Answer I Could Give

Virr kept the tally slabs behind a screen tied so tightly that the wind barely moved it.

There were nineteen stacks, one for each year of her work. Flat pieces of dark stone leaned against the base of a column. A short notch crossed the edge of every piece, and when one edge filled, Virr had continued on the next. Some stacks reached past her shoulder.

I began counting. Virr did not help. She sat near the opening and watched the public path while I moved from slab to slab. I counted twice because one stack had shifted and hidden three marks against the wall. The total was larger than I expected and smaller than a record of every dangerous thought in a settlement.

I asked what each mark had prevented. Virr said a received statement. When I asked whether any speaker had intended to lie, her front stayed neutral. “I stopped the words. If I could also inspect intent, I would need fewer rocks.”

The marks held effort, not outcomes. They could tell me how often Virr believed action was needed. They could not tell her whether a changed roof, bitter water, or dead child had been avoided. Nineteen years had produced a physical record of uncertainty.

I asked whether she ever regretted stepping in. “Yes.” The flat answer surprised me more than a hedge would have.

Virr explained that every interruption became part of the dispute. A speaker might have stopped alone. A listener might have recognized a claim as anger. Once Virr placed her body between them, everyone remembered danger. She had protected the room and also taught the room to fear what had almost been said.

When I asked why she continued, Virr said, “Because almost is cheaper than happened.”

She did not know I was considering a lie in the speaking ground. I had agreed that Ilva's request stayed private. Asking Virr for advice without giving the real facts would have used her answer as cover, so I did not ask.

I thanked her, counted the last stack once more, and left.

Ilva was where I had left her. She had finished scraping the column and spread the sheets to dry. Their edges lifted in the wind, so she had placed a small stone at every corner. Fourteen sheets. Fifty-six stones.

“No,” I said. Ilva did not ask which question I meant. I told her about the jar lie and the lack of change. One case proved almost nothing. I could be outside the world's rule. A larger lie could behave differently. A claim about my own past could force that past to exist, and I did not know what else would need to move around it.

“If the world makes it true that I stood beside you four years ago, where was I before that? What did I miss? What must happen to bring me there?”

Ilva said she did not know. “Neither do I. That is the answer I can give.”

Ilva checked one drying sheet and shifted its corner stone. She listened until I finished, which let the refusal remain mine instead of turning it into an argument. “That is fair,” she said.

I had prepared for persuasion. Her acceptance left all the force inside the request and none inside the conversation. She would still benefit if I changed my mind. She did not try to make changing easier.

After a while, she turned her front fully toward me. “I have lived four years inside one lie. I know what I am asking you to enter.” I pointed out that she was asking anyway, and she agreed. I told her my refusal still stood. She agreed to that too.

She returned to the drying sheets. I remained until the wind dropped and she removed the fifty-six stones. We worked beside the refusal, though the work belonged entirely to her limbs.

When I left, the question remained behind the screens. Ilva had not withdrawn it. I had not accepted it. A boundary can hold while both truths stay present.

## Chapter 16: The Past Afterward

I went to the boundary marker after dark. Dark on Ashet was only a dimming. The light that came from nowhere weakened, the columns turned silver-gray, and the paths remained visible. No shadows grew because there was still no direction for them to grow away from.

The marker lay on its side between the two worked columns. During the family dispute, I had measured from it as if its present position answered an old question. At night, without the families around it, the stone gave me less. One end held a dark band where it had once rested in soil. The ground beneath had settled. I could see that it had been moved at some point. I could not see by whom.

I began at the marker and followed the route toward the speaking ground. Thirty-one paces reached one family's column. Forty reached the other. From there, the open circle was far enough away that a Kesh carrying the stone would have crossed several public paths. Perhaps somebody had seen. Perhaps a careful mover had waited. The evidence ended each time I tried to walk beyond it.

Ilva's requested lie did more than supply a witness. If the world heard me say I had watched her lift the marker and made my statement true, then I would have been beside her. My invented memory might become an event. The empty path between marker and speaking ground would gain two witnesses where it had held none.

Then Ilva's own claim would match the changed past. I had been thinking of her plan as public deception. She wanted the settlement to believe she moved a stone. But if my lie took force, belief would become the smaller result. Her old statement would stop being false. Four years of possible punishment could close because, after the fact, there would be no lie left to punish.

She had asked me to make her innocent.

I moved over the bare ground of the speaking circle. Wind passed the distant columns and reached me without the high close notes. I placed myself where Ilva had stood when she made the old statement. The location changed nothing about what I knew, but it changed the size of the audience in my mind. Paths opened in every direction. A lie spoken there would belong to the entire settlement before the color finished moving.

Tik found me during the second circuit of the edge. It accused me of counting an empty place. I said I was counting the exits. “There are all the ways out. That is why it is open.”

Tik sat beside the boundary line, front facing me. I could have asked what it would do if one new fact made an old lie true. That question would have carried Ilva's secret without naming her. I kept it.

Instead I asked whether the past could change. Tik considered this with unusual care. “Yesterday changes when somebody tells me a thing I missed.” I said that only changed what Tik knew. “What else can yesterday do?” I had no answer that did not require the question I was protecting.

Tik remained while I finished the circuit. It did not demand an explanation. Every few breaths a color escaped, mostly curious orange, then disappeared. The company helped without solving anything, which was becoming a type of help I noticed more often on Ashet.

Before leaving, I moved to the exact center. I tried the possible statement silently: I was there. I saw Ilva move the marker. The words were short enough to fit inside one breath. Their cost could reach backward four years.

My refusal did not change. My understanding did. Ilva was not asking only for a false witness in the present. She wanted a new past strong enough to remove the old lie, and she was willing to risk mine to obtain it.

Knowing that made the request worse. It also made four years of waiting easier to understand.

## Chapter 17: When Everyone Leaked

Rain reached the plain the next afternoon. The first drops made dark round marks on the columns. Wind pushed water through the upper haze, and for several breaths the falling lines seemed to begin halfway down the sky. Then the rain thickened, ran over the scrape crust, and filled every path with the smell of wet stone.

Work ended at once. Kesh came out from behind screens, left tools under the columns, and turned their fronts toward anyone near enough to see. Color broke across the settlement. Fine controlled language gave way to broad orange, pink, green, and gold. The plain had always been bright. For the first time, it looked lit by the creatures living on it.

Tik ran past me in a tight circle and shouted that nothing counted.

This was not true, which was a poor thing to shout on Ashet. Tik saw my objection before I spoke. “Lies still count,” it corrected. “Everything else does not.”

What disappeared in the rain was not the world's rule. It was the settlement's habit of saving every visible reaction for later judgment. A Kesh could leak anger, delight, fear, or embarrassment without neighbors treating the color as evidence the following day. Nobody promised to forget. Everyone agreed to behave as though they had.

That agreement freed Tik completely. It joined four other young Kesh in the game where one stood still while the others tried to make color escape. On a dry day, the game had required effort. In the rain, the Kesh in the middle lost before the first challenge and kept laughing until no readable pattern survived. They took turns failing with equal success.

Dellek's crew climbed out of the channel and spoke where anyone could see them. This alone would have marked the day as strange. One worker repeated every rumor she claimed never to have shared. Dellek turned her back on the worker, then remembered that turning away could not hide the yellow amusement spreading around her sides.

Virr came along the bank without watching every panel. Water ran from the dark line on her shoulder. A pink pattern crossed her front, followed by a small shuffle of the rear feet. I had never seen Virr move without a practical reason.

She noticed me. The pink vanished under formal gray-green, and she blamed the movement on rain. I said I knew what she wanted credited. Virr turned a much brighter pink and walked away. I did not follow, which was kindness and also self-preservation.

Ilva stood under no shelter. Rain flattened the loose edges of the scrape sheets around her home. Her front showed colors I had not seen there, but she kept them smaller than the rest of the settlement. Four years of being watched did not wash away in one storm.

I spoke to her alone across the crowded path. “You do not have to answer the request today.” Gold mixed with a tired brown. She called that generous since I had already answered. I said the question remained. “Questions do that.”

She went to gather the wet sheets before they tore. Onnu came from another path and caught one that had lifted against a column. They worked at opposite sides of the pile. Neither faced the other. Rain gave them permission to show feeling, but it did not give them a way to speak without choosing what the other received.

I helped by warning Ilva when a sheet came loose behind her. My private voice was useless for once. Every Kesh was already carrying only what it chose to carry into tomorrow.

The storm lasted through the afternoon. Channels rose. The grit banks disappeared beneath moving water, which proved that my first low-water claim had been both accurate at the time and useless about the future.

By evening, colors still covered the plain, and the columns made higher notes through the wet wind. I watched a whole settlement stop hiding what it felt without making one promise about what was true.

The difference would matter when we began testing lies.

## Chapter 18: Forty Small Falsehoods

Virr agreed to witness the tests because she disliked my method.

I found her clearing rainwater from the edge of her screen. I explained that one lie about a jar had produced no change I could find. I wanted more cases. The false statements would be small, local, and easy to check before and after.

Virr pointed out that I was asking a truthkeeper to receive deliberate lies. I asked her to stop me if a test became unsafe. She considered the first statement unsafe already. I offered to begin with a change that could harm no one.

Virr looked at the water running off the screen. She disliked the plan, the request, and the fact that disliking them did not produce better evidence. “One,” she said. “Then we decide about two.”

We chose an unused patch beyond the north channel. Virr inspected the ground for other listeners. I inspected the objects: five ordinary columns, a shallow puddle, two loose stones, and Virr herself.

“The puddle is dry,” I said. It remained wet. Virr waited long enough for three drops to fall from the nearest scrape crust into the water, then allowed a second test.

I said one loose stone was blue. It stayed pale. I said the narrowest column was wider than the broadest. Neither changed. I said Virr had four limbs. She had six before, during, and after the words.

Virr required me to repeat the limb statement because I might have meant four front limbs. I pointed out that she had only two front limbs. “Then there should be no difficulty saying it properly.”

I repeated the lie with all six limbs in view. Virr remained six-limbed and looked satisfied with the wording, if not the test.

We continued. I called wet stone dry, flat ground sloped, five columns four, an empty path crowded, and rainwater sweetwater. After each claim, we checked the exact object. Nothing moved toward my words.

Virr stopped us at ten. She walked the whole patch, touched every stone and column we had used, and made me repeat the list from memory. At twenty she brought Dellek, who stood on the bank and received the next group without being told the previous results. Dellek complained that the lies lacked imagination. Virr threatened to make her the subject of one.

We reached forty before the light began to dim. No puddle dried. No stone changed color. No column altered width or count. Virr kept all six limbs. Dellek remained one Kesh despite my claim that she was three, which disappointed her because she had already assigned work to the other two.

Virr's colors had grown quiet. She looked across the unchanged patch and began to form a conclusion. I recognized it because I had reached the same one after the jar.

I stopped Virr before she carried the result too far. The test did not show that Kesh lies were harmless. Her front sharpened; I had made forty false statements. I reminded her that I was not Kesh and might fall outside the rule entirely. When she demanded why we had tested at all, I said, “To learn that.”

The answer irritated her. It irritated me too. Forty cases had turned one failure into a pattern, but the pattern belonged to an unknown speaker. We had tested Dot, not lying.

Dellek wanted to know whether this meant we had worked all afternoon to prove I was strange. I said that had already been established at the channel. She said she preferred results that did not arrive before the work began.

Virr returned to the first puddle. The surface still reflected the pale columns. She admitted she would have told the settlement that lies did nothing. I said the evidence did not support that claim. “And you stopped me.”

Virr had stopped hundreds of speakers from making claims she judged dangerous. Nobody had been able to stop her private conclusion because conclusions have no visible front until they become words. I had not interrupted her body. I had interrupted the answer.

She faced me squarely. “We need Kesh cases.” The afternoon had failed to settle the rule. It had done something more useful: Virr no longer treated me as Ilva's chosen instrument. She treated me as another worker who could reject a personal result.

# Part IV: What the Marks Say

## Chapter 19: The Other Notches

Virr knew where proven lies were recorded. She had never thought of the marks as a count.

The next morning she led me to a column near the south path. At the height of her front, one narrow cut crossed the stone. Weather had softened both ends. Scrape grew inside it, making the mark easy to miss unless you knew the level to inspect.

“A turning,” Virr said. When a Kesh was judged to have knowingly made a false statement, the settlement turned away. No one faced the offender. No one received speech from that front. The punishment lasted as the elders decided, but the notch remained beside the Kesh's home after every face returned.

The system required no wall, keeper, or central list. Each household lived beside its own public memory.

When I asked how many there were, Virr looked along the row and said only that there were more. I pointed out that this was not a count. “It has been sufficient for everyone who did not arrive wanting to count shame.”

She was not making a joke. I lowered my attention to the same height and moved along the path. The fourth column held another notch. Two rows later I found three on one stone: one sharp, one weathered, one nearly filled by crust.

Tik joined us and became useful at once. It knew which gaps had belonged to families that moved, which home screens had shifted after storms, and which old Kesh would remember a name. Tik also knew where to find dried food and attempted to charge one thumbling per notch. Virr rejected the rate. They settled on lunch.

By midday, we had found marks in every part of the settlement. The oldest notches told us almost nothing beyond the judgment. A cut did not preserve the statement, the evidence of intent, the thing spoken about, or what happened later. Some belonged to Kesh dead long enough that even their family names had faded.

I said the oldest marks could not answer us. Virr agreed reluctantly. Discarding a mark felt to her like discarding the Kesh who had suffered it. I reminded her that the old cases remained part of the settlement's memory; they simply could not test our question.

We needed four facts for every useful case. A living witness had to remember the false statement. The speaker's knowledge had to have been established strongly enough for a turning. The object, place, or person named had to be identifiable. Finally, some later condition had to be checkable.

Tik called the method a rule with four gates, then asked how many gates stood between us and lunch. Virr sent it to ask the nearest household whether three travelers could join their ring. Tik left before she finished the sentence, which answered the lunch question.

Virr and I returned to the south-path notch. She had been young when it was cut, but she remembered the dispute. A Kesh had denied taking a water blade even though two witnesses had watched him carry it home. The blade had later been returned.

I asked whether the world had made the denial true. Virr said no; the theft remained known. I concluded the false statement had not changed that event. She touched the notch. “Or it changed something else. Or it has not changed anything yet.”

The uncertainty that made every lie frightening also made every result hard to classify. We could not prove a statement had caused nothing. We could only ask whether the exact thing named had changed in a way witnesses connected to the words.

That was narrower than Ilva's question. Narrowness was useful. At lunch, Tik told the host family that Virr and I were counting turnings. Every front in the ring stopped. Virr did not correct the announcement. Instead, she asked the eldest Kesh whether he remembered the fresh notch near the east channel. He did. We had our first witness and our first usable route. The settlement's hidden record had been standing in the open all along.

## Chapter 20: Cases That Stayed Put

The east-channel notch belonged to a lie about a child.

The eldest Kesh from lunch took us to the marked column. Years earlier, a parent had said the child was across the settlement when the parent knew she was hiding behind a home screen. The lie was meant to keep an angry relative from finding her. The relative discovered the hiding place anyway. The child was grown now and worked three rows north.

We asked her permission to count the case. She listened to the full question, then faced each of us before answering.

“I was behind the screen,” she said. “I stayed there until they found me. I did not become elsewhere.”

Virr placed a small stone beside the notch. One usable lie. No matching change.

We developed a method as we walked. A pale pebble meant the named fact had stayed the same. A dark pebble meant witnesses linked a later change to the statement. An empty place meant the case failed one of our gates. Virr carried the stones in a woven sling because I could not. Tik counted them because it had negotiated lunch into a full day's employment.

The next mark failed. Everyone remembered a turned Kesh, but nobody agreed on the exact statement. We left the ground empty.

The third passed. A worker had said a water run was dry while water moved through it in front of several witnesses. The run continued that season and the next. Pale stone.

A notch by the north path concerned ownership of a column. The speaker had denied owning it to avoid a debt. The same family still scraped it, and the debt had been paid from later thumbling harvests. Pale stone.

We crossed rows, checked surfaces, and asked living witnesses only what they consented to answer. Some Kesh turned away when they learned why we had come. We did not follow. Others brought their whole family and corrected one another until Virr removed the case for conflicting memory. Each empty place saved us from a false certainty and cost us an easy number.

At midday, Tik counted the sling. Pale stones knocked together in the bottom. The dark side remained empty.

Tik concluded that changes did not happen. Virr and I corrected it at the same time. Tik went a pleased yellow because having two adults repeat your lesson is one way of winning.

Late in the day, we found a notch almost hidden beneath new scrape. Virr remembered the speaker but not the object named. We cleared the groove, then left no stone. The physical record looked useful from a distance. Up close, every cut opened into a separate problem of memory, consent, meaning, and time.

The last usable case involved a loose roof screen. A Kesh had said it was tied while knowing one corner hung free. The screen remained loose until someone secured it after the turning. The repair created a problem for our categories.

Tik argued that the statement became true later. Virr pointed to the Kesh who tied the corner. Tik asked how we knew the lie had not caused that repair. We did not. We placed no stone and added a new exclusion: an ordinary action that later made the claim true could not prove the world had acted.

By evening, the sling held many pale stones and no dark ones. That did not show the rule was false. It showed that a set of proven lies had been followed by ordinary persistence far more often than the stories suggested.

Virr emptied the pale stones beside her tally slabs. Their small pile looked weak against nineteen stacks of interrupted statements. It also carried something the tall stacks did not: each stone belonged to a lie that had actually been spoken and checked.

Virr said we needed more. I agreed. A pattern had begun, but we had not yet found the fact that could survive our wish for an easy answer.

## Chapter 21: The Roof Virr Saw

Virr brought one dark pebble the next morning.

She had removed it from the sling the day before and kept it under her screen. Now she placed it beside the pale pile. Tik reached toward it, then thought better of touching evidence while Virr watched.

“I know a case,” Virr said. “I did not want to offer it until I saw what you would discard.”

The mark stood beside a home on the far north row. The roof was woven from thick scrape sheets supported between three columns. From below it looked sound, though its color differed across one broad section.

Virr had been in her first year as a truthkeeper when two brothers argued there. One wanted the other to leave the settlement before winter. The roof over the second brother's home had a hole large enough for Virr to put her head through. Six Kesh had climbed up that morning and seen it.

During the argument, the first brother faced the witnesses and said the roof was sound. He knew the hole was there. He intended his brother to trust the claim, stay through bad weather, and be harmed enough to leave.

Tik asked what happened. “The hole was gone.” Virr did not hedge. She took us around the column and showed the route she had climbed all those years earlier. Tik went up first, using old bindings for holds. Virr followed more slowly. I rose beside them.

The discolored section above us was not a patch. Its weave continued into the older roof without knots, overlap, or binding. Virr pointed to the width of the former opening. She had marked two support lines on the day it changed, and the marks remained. Between them, the material was whole.

The current household let us inspect the upper surface. No one living there had witnessed the old event, but they knew the roof had stayed dry for nine winters before being replaced around its edges. The center section had never needed repair.

I asked whether six Kesh could have mistaken a loose sheet for a hole. Virr's front tightened. “I put my head through it.” I asked whether anyone could have repaired it after the claim. “We were looking at the speaker. Then we looked up. The opening was closed.”

I asked every unpleasant question the method required. Virr answered until only one account survived: six witnesses saw a known defect, received a knowingly false claim that the roof was sound, and then saw the defect no longer exist.

The statement had been meant to cause harm. The change prevented that harm. This did not make the rule kind. It made the rule indifferent to the reason for the lie.

We descended. Virr's back legs shook on the last hold. Tik positioned itself below without mentioning the help, and Virr let one rear foot rest across Tik's shoulders until all six limbs reached the ground.

I set the dark pebble apart in my count. It did not erase the pale stones. They did not erase it. “This is why I interrupt,” Virr said. Her work had no receipt. She could not point to one prevented change among the marks at home. But she had seen the world answer a deliberate falsehood once, and every future statement passed through that memory before she stepped into its path.

I had wanted the checkable lies to reduce the danger. This case reduced my comfort instead. Tik carried the sling back toward Virr's home. The pale stones clicked together with each step. Virr carried the single dark one herself.

One change among many unchanged facts was still a change. If the pattern went public, this roof had to go with it.

## Chapter 22: Two Piles

The old wind listeners gave us the final usable memories. They sat in a row where the columns narrowed and the air made a higher note. Listening to weather was their work. They could identify rain two days away by changes I could hear but not sort. They also remembered cases Virr had been too young to witness.

We brought the stones and the rules. The oldest listener rejected our first question because we asked which lies had changed the world. That assumed the answer we wanted.

“Ask what was said,” she told us. “Ask what stayed afterward. Put them beside each other when you are finished.” Virr accepted the correction. We spent the day doing exactly that.

One speaker had denied moving a marker, and the disturbed ground remained disturbed. Pale stone. Another had claimed an absent child was in a work crew; the child returned from a different row that same afternoon. Pale stone. A Kesh said an empty food store was full, and the household still borrowed scrape for winter. Pale stone.

The listeners remembered other changes, but the cases failed our gates. In one, no living Kesh had received the original words. In another, a repaired object might have changed through ordinary work. A third had a statement but no proof that the speaker knew it was false.

We left those cases empty. The listeners objected to some exclusions and defended others with great pleasure. Nothing makes an expert happier than a rule narrow enough to argue with.

Near evening, Virr emptied the sling onto flat ground. Pale pebbles spread into a broad uneven group. The roof's dark pebble sat apart. A few other dark stones represented changes remembered by living witnesses, but their cases were older and less complete than the roof.

We did not turn the piles into a rate. The cases were not all lies ever spoken. They were lies serious and visible enough to lead to turning, recent enough for living memory, specific enough to check, and preserved well enough to pass our gates. Any number divided from them would pretend to know more than the selection allowed.

Still, the shapes meant something. Many Kesh had knowingly made false statements and been publicly judged for them. In most usable cases, the exact subject of the lie had remained as witnesses knew it to be. Some changes had occurred. One stood beneath a roof we had touched.

Virr moved the dark stones closer to the pale group, but not into it. “Most is not never.” I answered that one was not always.

Tik counted both piles again and asked how long a pale case had to remain unchanged before it became safe. Neither Virr nor the wind listeners knew. A change might arrive at once, like the roof. It might come later. The old stories had survived partly because no delay could ever clear a sentence completely.

Tik checked the limits aloud. Every pale stone might become dark tomorrow, and every dark one might have changed for another reason. Virr agreed to both.

Tik went gray with frustration. I understood. We had spent days building a finding that kept refusing to become certainty.

The oldest listener leaned toward the column beside her. Wind moved around the stone, dropped in pitch, and steadied. “Rain in two days,” she said without a hedge. When I asked why she could speak so flatly, she replied, “You can hear the same wind.”

Every listener shared the evidence at that moment. If rain failed, none could later claim the speaker had known better. Their certainty rested as much on equal access as on skill.

Our stones did not offer equal access. Most Kesh had walked past the notches without counting them. Families knew individual turnings, not the whole shape. Virr had carried one roof for nineteen years without placing it beside the unchanged cases.

Now the piles stood together. The rule remained real. The fear had been larger than the evidence. Those truths did not cancel each other, and that was the finding we would have to carry to Ilva.

## Chapter 23: Worse Than Waiting

Virr carried the sling to Ilva's home and emptied it on the scrape mat.

She arranged the pale stones in one group and placed the dark ones where Ilva could see the distance between them. Then she faced Ilva and stated the limit: these were selected living-memory cases, not every lie and not proof of a rate. Ilva thanked her. Virr left the stones and walked away.

That departure was trust. Virr had given me the finding to deliver without turning her presence into pressure on Ilva's answer.

I began with the gates. Ilva listened while I explained why we discarded old stories, uncertain intent, missing objects, ordinary repairs, and unwanted interviews. She asked only practical questions. Which cases had living witnesses? Which statements named a checkable thing? How had we treated delay?

Then I described the pale pile. Many Kesh had knowingly lied. The named facts had remained unchanged as far as witnesses could tell. Four years without a found change was not unusual among the cases we could check.

Ilva looked down at the stones and said her lie had done nothing. I corrected the claim: no change had been connected to it. She called that four years of nothing. “Four years is inside the ordinary shape we found.”

I said each sentence as exactly as I could. She deserved the limits before the comfort, though the limits offered little comfort.

Ilva had woken every day expecting collection. That expectation had hurt her, but it had also given the hurt a purpose. She had made a deliberate lie and was serving the waiting it created. The pale stones did not pardon her. They took away the clean idea that suffering was payment.

Her front moved through colors too fine and quick for me to read. I will not turn them into an explanation she did not give. “That is worse,” she said.

I understood after she said it. A coming punishment can be counted as consequence. Four lost years with no sign from the world are simply four lost years.

I nearly made the finding larger because I wanted the pain to have a useful answer. I nearly told her the danger had not been real.

Ilva's front became completely still. “Do not give me that.” I stopped.

She crossed the mat and separated the roof pebble from the other dark stones. Virr had put her head through the hole; six Kesh saw it, and the roof became whole. I agreed. Ilva pressed the consequence. The danger was real. “You learned that it does not wait behind every false word. You did not learn that it was never waiting behind mine.”

The correction landed exactly where my claim had exceeded the evidence. I had wanted to replace one frightening certainty with a comforting one. The piles supported neither.

“I was wrong,” I said. Ilva did not tell me it was understandable. She moved the roof pebble back beside the others and asked what Virr planned to do.

I said Virr planned to continue her work. “Good.”

The answer surprised me. Ilva had suffered under the fear Virr helped maintain, yet she would not use that suffering to demand the truthkeeper stop. A smaller danger was still a danger, and Virr's body still belonged in the path if Virr chose to place it there.

The pale stones remained on Ilva's mat through the afternoon. She did not ask me to remove them, and I had no way to do so. Before evening, Tik arrived with Virr and the sling. Ilva helped gather every stone.

She kept none. This was not proof that she felt better. It was only what she chose to do with the evidence after receiving it.

## Chapter 24: The Person She Kept

I made one more comforting mistake before the stones left Ilva's mat. I said that at least Onnu had been worth the lie, because she had protected an innocent Kesh from being turned away. Ilva looked at me. “Onnu moved the marker.”

The sentence was as flat as the ground beneath us. I searched her front for some limit I had missed. There was none.

She told me what happened. Onnu lifted the marker after the paths emptied and set it closer to the other family's worked column. The next day he denied moving it before both families. The ground held a fresh hollow. Soil marked the lower stone. Everyone could see that somebody had disturbed it, and Onnu's colors betrayed what he knew.

The elders prepared to turn away from him. Onnu was young. In a settlement built from visible conversations, turning was more than silence. Meals required an inward-facing ring. Work required fronts meeting over tools and channels. A Kesh no one faced could remain alive and still be removed from nearly every shared part of life.

Ilva entered the speaking ground and claimed she had moved the marker. I asked whether she knew he had done it. She had watched him return with soil under two limbs, asked him directly, and received his admission. Her statement had therefore been a lie. Ilva agreed.

The old doubt had never belonged to Ilva. She had created it for Onnu and carried it afterward.

I asked why she had not told me. “You were finding what the lie did, not who deserved it. Onnu's guilt does not change the roof or the pale stones.” I said it changed what she had asked me to do. She agreed.

If my invented witness had made Ilva's claim true, it might also have made Onnu innocent by moving the act into her past. She had asked me to change more than her punishment. She had asked me to replace the guilty person.

Anger made my voice reach every Kesh near the open gap. Two passersby turned. Ilva stepped between them and me, using her body as Virr used hers, though my speech could pass through.

I narrowed the next words to Ilva alone. “You let me believe he had been cleared.” She said she had let me build that answer and had not corrected it. I told her this was no better. She did not disagree.

She did not ask me to understand. She did not offer Onnu's youth as a receipt or his kindness at the jar as payment against the moved boundary. Those facts explained her choice. They did not restore the other family's ground.

I asked whether she would do it again. Ilva turned her front toward the northwest path. A neighbor could see the whole answer if one passed. “Yes.”

For four years she had expected the world to collect. She had accepted suspicion, lost work, and kept one side of her home open. None of that had changed the choice underneath. If the same moment returned, she would still place herself between Onnu and the settlement.

I said waiting had not made her sorry. “I am sorry for what the other family lost. I am sorry I made every creature here wait with me. I am not sorry I kept him.” These were three separate statements. Ilva would not use one to soften another.

I left through the open gap and went to the marker. The distances had not changed: thirty-one paces and forty. Onnu's guilt did not tell me which family should own the column now. Ilva's love did not answer for the family whose line he shifted. Her four years did not pay them back.

The finding was ready. What anyone did with it would carry all of those costs, not only the ones Ilva and I wanted to count.

# Part V: What Each Person Chooses

## Chapter 25: The Smaller Decision

The finding did not tell us whether to announce it. I sat with Virr beside the two empty places where the stone piles had been. We knew what public knowledge might repair. Kesh who had spent years treating every unhedged error as possible deceit could separate those things. Children might learn caution without learning that one wrong word placed a disaster behind them.

We also knew what the finding could damage. An elder had hedged every sentence for ninety years. Virr had thrown herself in front of speakers for nineteen. Families had turned from their own members. The work might still have prevented a change, but there was no way to identify which one. Telling the settlement that most checkable lies had left their subjects unchanged would place every past sacrifice under a question that could never be answered.

The future worried me more. Few Kesh lied because all of them feared the result. Reduce that fear and the number of lies might grow. A rare change given enough chances would stop being rare to the creature beneath the roof, beside the bitter water, or inside the sentence about a child.

I asked whether Virr wanted the finding kept quiet. She watched two Kesh exchange tools on the next path. “Want is not the decision.” I told her she could still have one.

She wanted more time. She wanted to inspect the notches again. She wanted a finding in which the pale pile guaranteed safety or the dark pile proved danger. None of those wants could make the present evidence different.

“Ilva asked the question,” Virr said. “She paid before we knew the price. Give it to her.” I had already given Ilva the evidence. Virr meant the public choice.

I disliked that answer because it removed my chance to finish the problem. I had counted. I had rejected weak cases and overlarge conclusions. A part of me believed doing the work had earned me the last decision. It had not.

Ilva had asked what her statement did. The answer belonged to her before it belonged to the plain. She also understood the public cost in a way I could only measure from outside.

I found her at the boundary marker. She stood on the side Onnu had taken from the other family. Her front faced the disturbed ground.

I told Ilva the public choice was hers. She walked around the marker once and pointed out that I could tell the settlement without her. I said I would not. When she asked whether this meant I trusted her, I answered, “I found the answer to your question. That did not make the question mine.”

She turned a slow brown I could not name. Perhaps it was relief. Perhaps it was the weight of having no one else to blame for the next step. I kept both possibilities out of my claim.

Ilva asked whether Virr agreed. I said Virr had given her the choice and would decide her own words separately.

Ilva nodded. Nobody's consent could answer for another person. We had learned that while carrying pebbles from house to house.

The wind strengthened. High columns sang above the lower tones, and the first dark spots appeared on the stone beside us. The listener's forecast had arrived at two days exactly.

Ilva looked up into rain that seemed to begin in the haze. “Tomorrow,” she said. I reminded her that tomorrow could not be promised. “I can promise what I intend to do in it.” Her front held steady. “I intend to enter the speaking ground.”

The rain spread across the marker and filled the old hollow. Ilva walked home to prepare the words. I did not follow. Giving her the choice included letting her make it without me behind the screen.

## Chapter 26: What Ilva Said

The rain stopped before morning, though water remained in every shallow cut. Kesh crossed the plain carefully, testing dark stone before placing their weight. By the time Ilva reached the speaking ground, the paths behind her held enough wet tracks to show that most of the settlement had followed.

Virr stood outside the circle. She had not taken her usual place at its edge, where one step could block a speaker's front. The distance was small enough to count and large enough to be a decision.

Ilva entered alone. No screen stood near her. The pale morning made her color easy to see from all sides, and the Kesh arranged themselves until every front faced inward. I stayed beside Tik near the last ring. Onnu was across from us, between two families who had left space around him without admitting they had done it.

Ilva began with the question, not its cause. Four years earlier she had made a statement in this ground. Everyone present knew a truthkeeper had judged it false. Since then, Ilva said, she had watched the world around that statement and waited for it to become otherwise.

Her front showed the words without interruption. The quiet around her was not empty. It contained the remembered judgment, the work she had lost, and every Kesh who had avoided standing beside her.

“Yesterday I stopped waiting,” she said.

Several bodies shifted. One Kesh turned half away, then faced her again. Ilva did not ask them to stay.

She described the investigation without giving the private statement. There were old notches that marked known lies. Virr, Tik, and I had followed the usable ones to witnesses and objects. Most of the facts we could test had remained as they were. In one case, the fact had changed. In many cases, no honest comparison was possible. Ilva directed anyone who wanted the count or its limits to Virr.

That last direction changed the listening. Brown moved through the inner ring. A Kesh near Onnu asked whether Ilva meant the feared rule was false.

“I do not mean that,” Ilva said. “We found a change.”

Another asked whether her own words had changed anything. Ilva held still long enough for the question to travel around the circle. “We found no change that could be tied to my statement.”

It was the largest claim the evidence allowed. It gave her less than innocence and more than endless punishment. I had expected relief to alter her color. Instead, she remained in the gray-green range she used when fitting a difficult pattern.

A third Kesh demanded the statement itself. Ilva answered that the investigation required it and the public finding did not. The Kesh insisted that withholding it prevented a judgment about her motives.

“Yes,” Ilva said. “You do not have what you would need to judge those.”

The answer made anger visible in three parts of the ring. Ilva received it without offering the missing piece. She did not confess the boundary marker. She did not make Onnu's act public to improve her own position. Across the circle, his front had gone nearly colorless. She never faced him.

An old Kesh asked what Ilva wanted restored. Her former place among the pattern workers was still occupied. The family harmed by the disputed marker still used the narrower ground. No finding could return four years.

Ilva looked toward the work towers, then at the worn place where Virr had stood during her judgment. “I am not asking you to restore anything today. I am telling you what we learned. I am telling you I will not shape the rest of my life around an answer that did not come.”

Some Kesh accepted that. I knew because they lowered their fronts in the local sign that ended a dispute without agreement. Others remained upright. One turned away before Ilva left the circle. Two stayed facing her but kept the distance between them.

The settlement did not change together. Ilva had not asked it to. She crossed the wet ground toward her house while approval, doubt, anger, and thought continued behind her. Onnu moved as if to follow, then stopped. Ilva did not look back to reward or punish that choice. She walked with the pace she had used the day before, except that she no longer paused at the boundary marker.

## Chapter 27: The Truthkeeper's Count

Virr waited until Ilva left the speaking ground. Then she took the place she had declined before.

She carried three tally slabs against her side. I had seen those surfaces under poor light while we counted old notches. In the morning, the cuts looked less like a record than damage repeated with care. Virr placed the slabs where the inner ring could inspect them and faced the settlement.

She began with the method. A case entered our count only when a deliberate falsehood was known, the fact named by it had been established beforehand, someone could describe a before and an after, and the result remained open to inspection. Many old judgments failed one gate. Some failed all four. Virr showed the rejected cuts as carefully as the useful ones.

The Kesh who had challenged Ilva asked for totals. Virr gave them, separating the unchanged facts from the changed roof and both from the uncertain cases. She did not turn the groups into a rate. The records had been kept for punishment, not comparison. The easiest cases to find were not necessarily common, and many witnesses had died.

“Then what did you prove?” someone asked.

Virr answered that the known rule had acted at least once and had failed to produce a detectable change in many other cases. She added that absence from the count could not prove safety. Neither could fear turn an unobserved consequence into evidence.

The response moved through the ring in harsh color. A young pattern worker wanted to know whether Virr had judged Ilva too quickly. Virr said the judgment concerned a deliberate falsehood, not the later action of the world. Under the settlement's practice, Ilva had been judged correctly. Whether that practice had assigned the right consequence was another question.

An old Kesh near the center had attended the judgment four years earlier. The elder reminded Virr that she had stood between Ilva and the settlement, then asked which version of Virr they should believe. Virr remained the same dull blue she showed during difficult testimony. “The one who tells you what she can support today. If that differs from what I supported before, you should know the difference.”

The elder lowered their front and gave no reply. Silence from a Kesh who had corrected three other speakers that morning drew more notice than an argument would have.

Another voice asked whether Virr regretted nineteen years as truthkeeper. I watched for the pale flash she had shown on the roof. It came and passed.

Virr said she could name judgments that prevented another lie and could name speakers whose isolation had continued long after any protection required it. She could not count disasters that never occurred. She could not return work, meals, or trust to those the settlement had turned. The value of the office and the injuries done through it remained in the same answer.

Tik asked whether she would stop. Every front that could move faced Virr. She looked at the slabs rather than at Tik. “No.”

Anger appeared in Kesh who had wanted an ending. Relief appeared in others and embarrassed them. Virr let both reactions stand before she explained. A deliberate public lie could still place others at risk, and the changed roof remained as real as every unchanged object. Someone still had to preserve the difference between error and intention, interrupt dangerous speech, and keep records fit for questions the settlement had not learned to ask.

She would continue, but not under the same practice. A judgment would name the falsehood and the evidence for intent. It would not claim that the world had changed unless a change was found. Records would include what happened afterward, who had looked, and what remained unknown. Turning would no longer follow from her voice alone. The settlement would have to choose it publicly and state the harm it expected the punishment to prevent.

That proposal broke the circle into speaking groups. Some wanted an immediate vote. Some said Virr had weakened the only barrier between caution and ruin. A family who had lost a member to turning demanded a review of old judgments. The old Kesh in the center stayed silent, facing the tally slabs.

Virr did not try to settle every consequence before leaving the ground. She gathered two slabs and asked Tik to carry the third. Together they walked toward the storage wall, where the old cuts would remain available instead of resting behind her private screen.

I followed long enough to see where they put them. Virr had kept the office, but the office no longer allowed her to be the last person in every answer.

## Chapter 28: Still Probably

The settlement did not abandon hedging after one morning in the speaking ground. At the next meal, a server said the grain was probably cool enough. A builder described a support as holding under every load she had seen. Two children argued over whether a dropped cup had certainly struck the floor before it cracked.

I listened for fear in those limits and sometimes heard it. I also heard accuracy. The grain could burn a mouth. The support had not carried every possible weight. Neither child had watched the cup for its entire fall. The words marked the edge of what each Kesh knew, even when no punishment stood behind them.

What changed first was the response to error. A channel worker predicted that a new gate would reduce the western flow. When the water rose instead, Dellek made him inspect the slope, the wind, and the gate angle. Nobody called for Virr. The worker admitted that he had guessed from an old pattern and had mistaken confidence for measurement.

“Wrong,” Dellek told the crew. “That is the word. Use it before someone turns it into a longer meeting.”

The crew practiced the distinction because it let them return to work. A mistake could still break a channel. It could still require repair or apology. But it did not become a deliberate falsehood only because a listener had suffered from it. Intent needed evidence too.

Virr disliked how quickly Dellek claimed ownership of the idea. She said the distinction had existed before her gate failed. Dellek replied that the failed gate had made it useful. By the third retelling, she had personally separated error from deceit while standing knee-deep in water and had saved the whole western plain from a week of unnecessary hearings.

Tik corrected the depth to the lowest joint of Dellek's body. Dellek amended the story to include a witness whose excessive precision proved the event important.

The younger Kesh did not learn the new practice in one lesson. At first, they used probably in every claim, including their own names. Their instructors made them inspect the difference between uncertainty and disguise. A child who said a tool was probably not hidden behind a screen still had to explain why it was there. A child who reported a tower as taller than another could measure both and revise the answer without being treated as a deceiver.

Later, after Virr and the settlement had argued through procedures, older children attended judgments. They saw the evidence for intention placed beside the evidence for the fact itself. They also saw a blank space reserved for what followed. Virr did not fill that space with a predicted disaster. If nothing observable changed, the record said so.

Some adults considered the blank an invitation to lie. They kept their fronts narrow around Ilva and refused meals in her house. Others resumed work with her but watched every pattern she made twice. The family with the reduced boundary did neither. They asked the speaking ground to review ownership of the taken strip, and the argument began again without Ilva's private statement entering it.

Ilva returned to pattern work in small pieces. She repaired a rain cover for an elder who had not attended her announcement. A tower crew rejected her first design, accepted the second, and paid her the ordinary amount. No one announced that this restored her. She went home tired and complained that ordinary pay should be larger.

Tik found me counting the spaces that had changed around her at meals. One Kesh sat closer. Two remained far away. A third crossed both distances during the same meal to borrow a cutting tool.

“Does it mean they trust her?” Tik asked.

I said it meant they needed the tool. Tik waited, so I added that trust might have made the crossing easier. I could not separate those causes from one walk across a room.

Tik showed satisfaction anyway. It had become fond of answers with visible edges.

The settlement continued to qualify forecasts, recipes, praise, blame, and plans for tomorrow. I stopped hearing every limit as evidence that the Kesh were trapped by fear. Sometimes probably was where fear had taught them to live. Sometimes it was the exact size of an honest claim. The word sounded the same. The work was learning to ask which use stood behind it.

## Chapter 29: An Unanswered

Onnu went to Ilva's house three days after she spoke. I knew because Ilva had invited me to be present and had told him I would hear the conversation. Her new screen was not finished. The frame stood beside the doorway with two dark panels fixed and a third still on the work mat.

Onnu stopped outside the open frame. He had brought a covered bowl, the kind exchanged after an injury or death. Ilva looked at it without taking it.

He said he had come to thank her. Ilva asked which act he meant. His answer widened until it included keeping the old statement private, refusing to name him in the speaking ground, and preserving the boundary question for the family rather than turning it into his public judgment.

Ilva listened. “Those were three choices,” she said. “You do not get to make them one favor.”

Onnu set the bowl down. He said he knew that. Then he amended the claim and said he was beginning to know it. The correction was careful enough that I would once have treated it as proof of his kindness. Ilva did not.

She asked whether he intended to return the strip of ground. Onnu said the public review had not finished. Ilva observed that his own act did not depend on the review. He moved from the uncertainty of the original marker to the needs of his family, then to the danger of appearing to confess before the settlement decided.

None of those statements were necessarily false. Together they built a route around the question.

Ilva did not chase him along it. She said he could take the bowl when he left. She would not discuss gratitude while the other family still lived with the loss he had caused.

Onnu faced her for several breaths. His color held the soft green he used when asking someone to see his good intention. Ilva's front remained plain. At last he lifted the bowl and left with it.

Afterward, Ilva fitted the third panel to the frame. She did not say the visit had ended their relationship. She also did not follow him or send a kinder explanation. The hollow between them kept its old shape, though both could now see what had made it.

I left her working and climbed to the highest listening platform. The old wind listener was there, comparing the tones after the rain. I told her that Onnu had said many things the world might never prove or reject. I wanted a Kesh word for a false speaker whose claim produced no change.

She considered the request while a low column sounded beneath us. She said I wanted a name for what the world had decided about the speaker. When I agreed, she angled her front toward a higher note. “That assumes the world decided. We call such a speaker an unanswered.”

The word did not mean safe, forgiven, or honest. It meant reality had not supplied a result that anyone could read. The listener said an unanswered could still have caused harm through the speaking itself. A listener might act on the claim. A family might divide. A truthkeeper might be wrong. The absent answer removed none of those events.

I kept the word because it held uncertainty without making it empty.

Below us, Tik was attempting seven breaths in the open channel. Its first try ended at five when spray struck its front. Its second reached six and part of another. On the third, it braced its lower joints against the wet wall and held its color through the seventh count.

Dellek announced that her instruction had produced the result. Tik replied that Dellek had spent the final four breaths shouting. The crew accepted both claims as entertainment rather than evidence.

Tik climbed out and found me above the channel. I had told it I would leave soon, though not exactly when. It faced the route toward the outer columns and said it could guide me beyond the last work tower. It no longer said probably when it meant to ask.

We started before the stone dried. Tik chose the higher path because the lower one still held rain, and it described every turn as if I could lose sight of it. I did not correct that assumption. At the final tower it paused, counted seven slow breaths without being asked, and continued with me toward Ilva's house.

## Chapter 30: The Shape Between

Ilva had finished the screen. Three dark panels stood inside the frame, fitted closely enough that no color escaped through the joins. She had placed it beside the entrance rather than across it. Privacy was available there, not imposed on every visitor.

Tik inspected the fastenings. It found one edge that moved under pressure and offered to repair it. Ilva told Tik the movement was intentional so the panel could release in high wind. Tik tested the explanation against the hinge and accepted it with visible reluctance. It preferred improvements that required its help.

I told Ilva I was leaving. She did not ask whether I meant probably. She had prepared a narrow packet of dried grain for Tik's return walk and nothing for me. This was more useful than a gift I could not carry.

We stood outside the screen. Ilva could have stepped behind it to make the conversation private from Tik. She asked it to remain instead.

“I used you,” she told me. Her front held the controlled gray-green I had seen in the speaking ground. “I approached you because you did not know our punishments. I expected your ignorance to make you willing to ask what a Kesh would refuse.” I said that expectation had been correct. A warmer color crossed the gray before she added, “I also liked you. That did not prevent the use.”

The two statements fit the evidence better together than either did alone. Ilva had selected me because I was useful. She had protected my access when Virr questioned it, taught me how to read a front, listened when my measures made her angry, and let me see the part of her claim she still withheld from the settlement. Affection did not erase the design. Design did not make every later kindness false.

I did not ask her to withdraw either statement. I had wanted relationships to sort themselves like the pebbles: chosen or accidental, true or false, caring or useful. The investigation had taught me what happened when a clean division required throwing out the difficult cases.

“I used you too,” I said. “You had a question large enough to keep me here.”

Tik objected that this was a poor farewell. Ilva told it that it had not heard many departures. Tik replied that it had heard enough to know they should include praise. She considered this and said it had become better at staying visible in water. Tik accepted the claim as insufficient but accurate.

Ilva faced me again. “Did you find what you came for?”

I had entered Ashet because a people who feared false speech seemed likely to know what truth was for. I could have answered that they used it to keep the world fixed. The roof prevented that. I could have said they used it to trust one another. Onnu prevented that. The speaking ground, the screens, and every careful probably offered another answer, but none of them held alone.

“I found what you do when no answer arrives,” I said. Ilva's color brightened, then narrowed with correction. “Some of what we do.” I accepted the limit. Tik gave Ilva the packet back by mistake, retrieved it, and led me toward the outer columns.

At the settlement's edge, the stone dropped in broad shelves toward the leafless forest. Tik stopped at the last place where a Kesh path remained visible. It had promised to guide me beyond the work tower, and it had done so. It did not enlarge the promise by taking a route it did not know.

Below us, the Kesh moved through their day. A channel crew lifted a gate after checking the wind. Two children crossed the speaking ground without entering its center. Virr carried a new slab toward the storage wall while another Kesh walked beside her. On the disputed strip, three figures measured from the old hollow in both directions. Ilva's finished screen appeared as a dark angle beside her house.

The settlement was not healed. Onnu had not returned the ground. The turned had not returned to their meals. A proven lie could still alter what it named, and nobody knew why one did while forty others left no result they could find. Yet a wrong forecast could now remain an error, an old judgment could acquire an aftermath, and a person judged false could decide which truth became public.

Tik faced me until I rose beyond the height at which its color remained readable. Then it turned toward home. It did not wait to see whether I had vanished. The path required its attention, and it had learned that departure did not need its watching to occur.

From above, the channels joined into a pale branching shape across the stone. Beyond them, the forest made a darker one. Between those forms ran a thin white line I could follow but not explain. It might have been mineral, dried water, or only the angle of the light. I left it unnamed.

The line continued past the last tower and the last screen, pointing away from Ashet without promising where it went. I followed it until the plain became a mark behind me. What the Kesh had changed would appear in choices too small to see from that distance, and what the world would answer remained beyond my measure.
