# Part I: What the Light Shows

## Chapter 1: A Town Drawn in White

By the time Seth came into focus around me, the sun had already made the place difficult to look at. White walls caught the light and threw it back. White streets did the same. Even the dust seemed to have chosen the brightest color available, which felt unnecessary.

I stayed high until I could see properly. That is one advantage of being a floating point with no eyes anybody else can find. I do not have to shade them. I do, however, have to wait while whatever part of me sees gets used to a new world. Nobody explains these arrangements to me. I arrive as a dot, I move without legs, and I continue.

Below me, the town sat alone on a flat plain. Its houses were low, with open yards between them and straight lanes that let the wind travel through. The wind was hot, but at least it was moving. An eastern road left the last building and ran across the bare land until heat swallowed it. I could not see where it began before the town or where it finished after. Roads often make more sense when you have somewhere in mind. I had neither a place behind me nor one ahead.

This was my eighth world. On the seven before it, I had found parts of a question I could not yet ask well. A mark had turned up more than once. A word from an old account had returned where it should not have belonged. The crossings that carried me from world to world had begun to feel less like separate accidents. Those facts fitted together at the edges, but they did not give me a picture.

I mention that because I arrived ready to count clues. Instead, I counted shadows.

There were thirty-seven people in the first three streets I crossed. They were tall and narrow, with two legs, two arms, and faces I could read well enough. Each had a pair of long fins rising from the head. Calm fins rested backward. Interested fins leaned toward whatever held the person's attention. I learned that before I learned the name Seth, because bodies explain themselves faster than people do.

Their shadows should have been easy too. The sun stood west of straight overhead, so every dark shape fell east. A person's fins made a clean fork in the outline. Their arms and posture made the rest. If you knew who stood in a yard, you could have chosen that person's shadow from a row of them.

Except one outline had its fins aimed the wrong way.

I went lower. Two Seth stood beside stacked sacks in a yard and discussed how many more would arrive before evening. The first person's shadow copied him. When he shifted one foot, the dark foot moved. When his hand rose toward the sacks, a long arm crossed the ground.

The second shadow occupied the right place but behaved like a different moment. Its body faced the speaker, as its owner did, yet the head had turned toward a doorway behind them. The shadow-fins pressed forward with attention. Nobody stood in that doorway now. A third person had been there when I entered the yard, then carried a basket inside.

I checked the sun. Then I crossed above the two speakers and checked again from the other side, though moving around a light source has never persuaded it to change its mind. The dark outline remained where the sun required. Only its attention was wrong.

You see why I stayed. A road with no visible end was interesting, but roads are meant to leave. A shadow watching an empty doorway while its owner discussed sacks was breaking its one job.

I waited for the person in the doorway to return. The owner never looked back. The shadow did.

That gave me a fact. I did what I always do with one fact: I went looking for a second before deciding what the first one meant.

## Chapter 2: Four Courteous Escapes

The second fact was crouching beside a wall.

Its owner was tying a strip of cloth above a stall. She stood with both arms raised while somebody below told her the left side was lower. On the wall, her shadow had folded itself small and seemed to be reaching beneath the stone. I could not see anything there. I went close enough to check, which meant the woman tying the cloth had to lean away from me.

“Sorry,” I said. “The shape behind you is doing something near the ground.”

She glanced at me, then at the person helping her. Neither looked behind. The woman told me newcomers often found the light painful and asked whether I had crossed the plain alone. I answered because the question was reasonable. She asked where my food was. I explained that I did not eat. She wanted to know whether I slept. By the time I had answered that, the cloth was tied and both people had gone inside to fetch another length.

It was a neat escape. I did not yet know it was an escape.

I found a third shadow outside a house. Its owner waited alone, so I expected fewer interruptions. The dark shape leaned around the corner while the person faced the street. When I pointed this out, he made a thoughtful sound, said he was late, and became late somewhere else.

The fourth attempt lasted longer. A person carrying a coil of rope listened to my whole first sentence and then told me about the seasonal caravans. They came twice, she said, and this year's first one might be early. She also told me where to find water, who repaired cracked pots, and why Debb's opinions about rope had caused an argument that morning. I asked again about the shadow beside her feet. She explained that Debb had been wrong about the rope.

Nobody called me foolish. Nobody denied the moving outlines. They gave useful advice, asked friendly questions, or remembered an urgent journey. That seemed stranger than anger would have been. If a floating point told you that your shadow had wandered off within itself, surely you would at least check. Wouldn't you?

I was arranging those four replies in order when a child ran into the street and supplied a fifth.

The child was small enough that the head fins had not reached their full length. It moved as if slowing down were a rule invented by adults. Halfway across the street, it stopped so sharply that another child nearly struck it from behind.

“That one's hiding!” it shouted.

I followed the pointing hand. A shopper stood beside a doorway, holding up two pieces of something flat for a seller to judge. The shopper's shadow had pressed itself behind the door frame. Only the tips of its fins showed on the wall.

An adult walking beside the children did not follow the hand. She placed her palm on the first child's head and turned it toward the street while continuing a conversation about a ladder. The movement was practiced and gentle. The child accepted the new direction for almost one step, then began shouting about which of them would reach the well first. Both children ran away.

I stayed by the doorway until the shopper chose one of the flat pieces and left. The hidden shadow came out because the body casting it changed position, though I could not tell whether that was the same as choosing to come out.

Here were the facts as I then had them. Shadows did more than copy. Children noticed. Adults did not look, even when told exactly where to look. Their refusal was so calm that it seemed less like refusal than absence.

I tested one more thing. I asked the seller whether children on Seth often invented games about shadows.

The seller studied the empty doorway. “Children invent games about everything,” he said. Then he offered me water.

I did not need water. I did need an explanation, and by evening I believed I had one.

## Chapter 3: My Excellent Explanation

My explanation was that grown Seth could not see what their shadows did.

It had the useful quality of fitting every fact I had collected. Children called out what they saw because children still possessed the ability. Adults changed the subject because they believed the children were making things up. The hand on the child's head was the patient correction of somebody who had heard the same foolishness for years. The four adults had not checked because there was nothing for them to check.

I liked this answer immediately. That should have warned me.

When an idea explains a great many things at once, I usually test it harder. On Seth I admired it. I spent the last bright hour moving from street to street and watching shadows sit when their owners stood, reach toward shut doors, turn toward people who had already passed, and walk several steps without leaving the patch of ground they occupied. Every new sight became support. I did not count the adults who placed themselves with walls behind them or the number who shifted a lamp after somebody else entered a yard. Those did not fit, so I treated them as ordinary behavior.

By sunset, I had promoted my answer from likely to certain.

That created a duty, or what felt like one. If an entire town had lost a way of seeing, somebody ought to tell them. I had crossed enough worlds to learn that an interesting fact about a person is not automatically public property. This seemed different. Nobody was hiding a fact they could not see. I imagined the astonishment when I showed one patient adult what had been happening beside them all along.

Look, I did not plan to embarrass anyone. I planned to give something back.

The town prepared for night while I prepared my lesson. People carried low lamps into the courtyards and set them near tables, benches, and doors. Their yellow light caught the white stone and made small warm rooms without roofs. I chose a yard where five people were bringing out bowls. One man placed three lamps around a table, moved the nearest one, sat down, stood again, and moved the far lamp as well.

When he finished, the five shadows lay under the table. Anyone who wanted to study them would have needed to crawl between several pairs of legs.

That was evidence. I remember seeing it. I remember deciding the lamp positions kept the light out of people's faces.

At the west end of town, children shouted in a repeating rhythm. One voice asked something, several answered, then the group broke into argument and began again. I could have gone closer. Instead I stayed above the yard and worked out how to explain moving shadows without frightening anyone.

The lesson needed three steps. First, establish that a normal shadow matches the body. Second, wait until one of the unusual movements began. Third, keep the adult's attention on it long enough for whatever lost ability they had to return. I did not know whether a lost ability could return through instruction, but that is why you run a test.

The man at the table shifted another lamp. A woman told him it had been fine where it was. He said the bowl cast an annoying shape. She said bowls always did. He moved the lamp back by half the distance, which satisfied neither of them but ended the discussion.

Every person's shadow remained buried beneath the table.

I left to find a high corner where I could wait for morning. The eastern road held the last light longer than the town did, a pale mark across the plain after the walls had turned grey. Somewhere near the west end, the children completed their chant and ran. I listened to the running and not the words.

In the morning I would find one adult with time, patience, and a useful wall. Then I would show Seth what Seth had forgotten.

## Chapter 4: The Fourth Stall

The work row looked promising because it had one long wall.

Eleven open stalls stood beneath it, all facing south into the street. They repaired the things that kept the town ordinary: pots, hinges, shutters, lamp frames, straps, brackets, and tools I could not name. The air there held stone dust and hot metal. Every few steps something scraped, rang, or refused to fit while several people explained why somebody else had caused the problem.

I began at the west end and asked whether anyone had a moment. The first repairer said no without looking up. The second offered to mend me, then admitted that was a joke. The third sent me toward the fourth because Hannit had time for everybody whether they requested it or not.

The fourth repairer worked close to the floor. His bench-seat lifted him only high enough to bring the block beneath his hands, and he kept both legs extended into the clear space at the stall's edge. Later I understood that standing hurt. In that first moment I saw an old Seth with worn fins and steady fingers, pressing a warped bracket to the block until its bad angle revealed itself. One tap corrected what I had needed three views to notice.

“You are the floating visitor,” he said.

“I am beginning to think that may be my name here.”

“It is accurate. Names have survived on less.” Hannit pointed his tool toward the shaded space beside his bench. “Come out of the heat. I would offer you a seat, but you appear committed to managing without one.”

I liked him before I had learned his name. This was convenient, because he gave me very little room not to learn it. In the next several minutes he told me that the wind carried grit into every useful joint, the caravans encouraged careless packing, and Debb at the seventh stall had been wrong about rope since well before I arrived. He said all of this while repairing the bracket.

A younger person moved around him. Tavi was thirteen, though I learned that later. That morning I first knew them as the one who fetched whatever Hannit named half a breath before he finished naming it. Tavi brought a pin from a divided box, returned a tool to the wall, answered a customer, and placed one hand on the back of Hannit's frame when he leaned sideways. Neither mentioned the touch.

Hannit tested the bracket. “This town does not understand how it appears to sensible outsiders.”

Tavi set down the pin box. “White.”

“I mean its habits.”

“Hot and white.”

Hannit looked delighted. “No depth of conversation. None. I have offered years of guidance.”

“Mostly about wind.”

That settled something between them which had plainly been settled many times before. Hannit returned to the bracket. Tavi handed him the next tool without being asked.

I forgot my planned lesson for nearly an hour. Work has that effect on me when I do not know how it is done. Hannit could find a bad angle by pressing a joint once. Tavi knew which customer needed an answer and which one only needed to complain near somebody skilled. A pot arrived with a loose handle and left firm. A shutter fitting arrived with a crack and stayed because the crack ran deeper than the customer had said.

Only after the morning rush eased did I remember the wall. Sun reached beneath the short awning and cast three clean outlines behind the stall: Hannit's seated shape, Tavi's standing one, and a customer's shadow beside them.

The customer's shadow had both arms buried in the wall up to its dark elbows. The customer was holding a lamp frame in plain view.

At last, I thought. A patient adult, time, and the best wall in town.

I moved closer and began, “There is something behind you that I think you cannot—”

Tavi stepped between me and the wall while raising the lamp frame. The movement was so well timed that I had to rise to avoid passing through the object.

“Do caravans travel between worlds?” Tavi asked.

It was a very good question. I answered it.

When I finished, the customer had gone, the wall was empty, and Hannit was explaining why Debb's rope had once delayed a cart. My lesson had disappeared without anybody refusing it.

I decided the fourth stall would be an excellent place to try again.

## Chapter 5: What Helping Meant

By midday I had learned the arrangement at the fourth stall. Hannit repaired anything that could be brought within reach of his bench. Tavi brought the rest of the world to him.

That included tools, parts, customers, water, messages, a ladder, two shutter boards, and Hannit himself whenever he rose too far forward and needed the frame held still. Hannit knew the work. His fingers found warped edges that looked straight to me, and he could tell which metal had been heated badly by its color near a join. Tavi knew everything around the work: what waited, what had been promised, which shelf held the right size, who needed an answer, and how long Hannit could stand before the answer had to come to him.

They called all of this helping.

No one had announced the arrangement. I could tell because announcements leave edges. Somebody remembers the day before. Someone objects to the new rule or praises it too much. At this stall, the work had moved toward Tavi one upright task at a time until the pattern had no visible beginning.

I counted because counting is how I see a load. In one hour Tavi crossed the stall front twenty-six times. Hannit left his frame twice. On the second attempt, Tavi was beside him before he asked. Hannit complained about the height of the shelf, the person who had placed the box there, and the decline of sensible storage. He did not notice that Tavi had already taken down the box.

This did not make Hannit cruel. It did not make Tavi unhappy every minute. They laughed over a customer who had repaired a hinge backward and blamed the door. Tavi corrected Hannit twice and won both times. But the stall depended on a thirteen-year-old being available whenever standing was required, and nearly everything beyond the bench required standing.

I stored that fact without knowing where it belonged.

My shadow lesson went badly. The first time I began to point out a dark shape sitting on the ground while its owner stood, Tavi asked whether I had a name. I explained that I was called Dot. This led Hannit to offer six better names and Tavi to reject each in one word. The customer left during the fourth suggestion.

The second time, Tavi placed a bracket directly in my path and asked whether I could pass through solid objects. I could not, so I went around. By then the owner of the shadow had moved away from the wall.

The third attempt almost worked. Debb came from three stalls east carrying rope and an opinion about the ladder. Debb's shadow was pulling hard at something beyond the wall. I waited until Debb finished telling Hannit that the ladder would fail because it had been stored badly.

“There is a shape behind you pulling toward—”

Tavi dropped a box.

It did not fall far, but the contents spread over the floor. Hannit objected to the treatment of good fittings. Debb objected to their storage. Tavi crouched and began sorting them, and three people passing the stall stopped to offer advice without bending down. By the time the box was full, Debb had carried the rope away.

“Did you do that on purpose?” I asked.

Tavi checked the box corners. “It slipped.”

The words allowed no useful next question. I might have asked one anyway, but Hannit wanted the ladder moved and Tavi went to move it.

I reconsidered Tavi's behavior. Adults ignored shadow talk because they could not see. Children spoke freely because they could. Thirteen stood between those ages. Perhaps Tavi still saw and hated being treated like a younger child. Perhaps every interruption was kindness toward the adults, saving them from a visitor who would not stop discussing nonsense.

It was a gentle theory. It made Tavi thoughtful and me harmless. I preferred it.

Near the end of the day, Nub arrived at speed. I knew the child's name because four people shouted it within ten seconds. Nub pointed at Debb's shadow on the wall and announced that it was upside down. Debb sent Nub to ask about the ladder. Nub took two steps, spotted another shadow tugging at nothing, and announced that too. An adult turned Nub by the head without interrupting her own sentence.

Tavi watched me instead of the wall.

I thought that look meant, *You see what children are like.* So I said nothing, pleased that I had understood.

## Chapter 6: A Journey Without Distance

The next afternoon, Hannit began listing work for the coming weeks.

He did this while fitting a pin into a shutter hinge. The hinge had been promised to Merrat, who had already asked for it twice and would ask again before it was finished. After that came the shutter boards stacked at the back. Hannit wanted new racks before the caravans returned. There was also a door that required a full day, though Hannit believed the owner would describe it as a small adjustment until the bill appeared.

Tavi stood at the bench sorting fasteners. Each size went into a different square of a wooden tray. Hannit named the future as if it were another shelf: this job here, that one there, everything stored in its proper place.

“When the dry weather turns,” he said, “we can start the long door. You'll still be here, so we'll take one side each.”

Tavi made a sound that could have meant agreement. The real Tavi did not stop sorting.

The shadow did.

It lay cleanly on the wall behind the bench. I saw its hands leave the dark tray. Beside one foot was a small bundle that had not been there a moment earlier. The shadow bent, lifted the bundle to one shoulder, turned toward the east end of the row, and began walking.

It travelled with effort. One leg reached, then the other. Its shoulders tipped beneath the bundle. The outline stayed fixed behind Tavi because a shadow cannot leave its owner, but everything inside the outline said distance. Beyond the last stall, the eastern road ran into the white heat.

I forgot my promise to be patient.

“Look at—”

Tavi swept the tray off the bench and held it in front of me. No parts fell this time. “Tell me how you cross between worlds.”

“I told you yesterday.”

“I want the full answer.”

There was no full answer. I told Tavi that too, then explained what I knew: a crossing began, I went through it, another place replaced the first, and I could not choose when or where. Tavi asked whether I felt motion. I said not in a way I could measure. Tavi asked whether I ever returned to the same world. I had not. The questions were careful and serious, though Tavi did not look at me while asking them.

When we finished, Hannit had reached the wind again. The shadow still held its bundle, but Tavi stood directly between me and the wall.

I put the event beside the earlier interruptions and drew the wrong boundary around it. Tavi was interested in leaving. Of course a young person in a town beside a caravan road might imagine travel. Tavi was also ashamed of being seen watching shadows. Those were two facts, and I kept them separate because joining them would have required me to question my excellent explanation.

After the stall closed, I floated toward the west end. A group of children had formed a ring in the uneven space where three lanes almost met. One older child pointed from face to face while calling questions. The others shouted answers together. I heard *worlds*, then *hands*, then something about home. Their voices had the flat speed of words repeated too often to need thought.

The round ended with one child chosen. Everyone ran, including the chooser. Whatever game followed had rules I could not see and a great deal of noise.

I could have stayed. The word *worlds* should have held me. Instead I returned to the row because Tavi's shadow had supplied the most interesting fact on Seth, and I wanted to see it happen again.

That was the trouble with my attention. Once I found a pattern, every other fact had to wait outside it.

At the fourth stall the next morning, the tray sat in its usual place. Tavi worked. Hannit talked. The eastern road shone beyond the row, empty for as far as I could follow it.

On the wall, the bundle was gone.

# Part II: The Fact Said Aloud

## Chapter 7: Five People in the Shade

The row became crowded just before midday. Everyone had reached the same conclusion about finishing errands before the worst heat, which meant they all arrived together and stood beneath the narrow awnings breathing one another's hot air.

Hannit's stall held five visitors before I counted Hannit, Tavi, or myself. A woman had brought another lamp bracket. Two men shared a hinge and disagreed about which door had damaged it. An older woman waited for a pot Hannit had finished that morning. Debb leaned against the side post, needing no repair at all, and explained why the hinge had been installed badly.

I stayed above the awning. From there I could follow the work without occupying the only clear space in front of the bench. Tavi moved through the crowd with a care that looked effortless until I counted the near collisions. Hannit wrapped the pot, answered one hinge question, ignored the next two, and told Debb that a person who came to watch should at least bring useful silence.

Beyond them, the row opened toward the east. The road began after the final buildings and crossed the plain as one pale stroke. In the midday glare it seemed to rise into the heat rather than continue over the ground. The caravans travelled that way twice each season. They brought supplies, gossip, and people who had spent long enough somewhere else. When they left, they accepted passengers.

I knew those facts because Hannit had explained them several times. I also knew Tavi looked toward the road whenever wheels were mentioned. That might have meant longing, worry, interest, or only the sensible habit of looking at the place under discussion. A moving shadow was information. It was not a sentence. I understood that much even before I understood the rest, though I did not apply it when it mattered.

The two men ended their hinge argument by paying Hannit to decide. Hannit showed them a worn hole around one pin. Neither man liked his answer because it gave both of them work. They left carrying the hinge between them and began a new disagreement before reaching the next stall.

The older woman examined her pot. She ran a thumb around the repaired edge, held it toward the light, and asked whether the join would survive heat. Hannit answered the exact question. She asked it a second way. He gave the same answer more loudly. Debb said volume did not improve metal. Hannit said Debb had been improving rope by volume for years.

It was a good morning. I mean that plainly. The work moved. People knew one another's habits. Tavi smiled twice and corrected Hannit once. Nobody sent me away when I reported that the lamp-bracket woman had been waiting longer than the hinge men. Hannit told me I had discovered the purpose of a queue and put her job next.

On the wall, Tavi's shadow sorted dark fittings into a dark tray. No bundle appeared.

I became less careful because I was comfortable.

That is one of the more expensive ways to stop paying attention. Fear at least tells you that your next action may matter. Comfort lets you believe everybody knows what you mean.

The shade held eight people. The eastern road showed beyond them. Hannit completed the lamp bracket and began reviewing the next jobs aloud, as he always did. Nobody moved away because nothing had happened yet.

I floated lower so I could hear.

## Chapter 8: The Space of One Breath

Hannit's list reached the racks.

He had already covered Merrat's shutters and the long door. He turned the finished bracket over in his hands while he spoke, checking the repaired join as if the future work were another part of the same object.

“We need the new rack before the next caravan,” he said. “After that, when the season turns, you and I can take the door apart properly.”

The sentence contained no question. Hannit placed Tavi in that future with the same confidence he used when placing a pin in a hinge.

Tavi said, “Mm.”

Behind the bench, the shadow stopped sorting. The bundle formed beside its foot. It lifted the bundle, settled the weight against one shoulder, faced east, and walked without moving from the wall.

Five visitors saw it.

I know because every one of them changed at once. The woman with the bracket examined her empty hands. One hinge man looked toward the street and the other suddenly found Hannit's shelf interesting. The older woman lowered her face over the pot. Debb studied the top of the side post. The whole movement occupied less time than one breath.

There was my missing second fact. Adults could see.

That should have stopped me. Instead it thrilled me. I had been wrong about the inability, but right that something real was happening. Five witnesses made the sight public, I thought. If everyone had already noticed, then speaking could not expose anything.

Do you see the mistake? I did not. I confused shared sight with shared permission.

I lowered beside Hannit's bench. “Tavi's shadow takes a bundle east whenever you plan work past the caravans.”

The walking stopped.

Nobody argued. The woman with the bracket said she could collect it later, though Hannit had just finished it. One hinge man remembered another errand and the other agreed with surprising speed. The older woman paid for the pot without asking her last two usual questions. Debb told Hannit something ordinary about a ladder, received an answer, and left.

Within a minute, the crowded patch of shade held only Hannit, Tavi, and me.

Hannit kept the bracket in his hands. He looked across the street at a blank wall. This was the person who had spoken through every task, every wait, and several moments that did not need speech. Now even the wind escaped comment.

Tavi picked up the real sorting tray. One fastener had rolled into the wrong square. Tavi moved it back, then moved it again, though the first square had been correct.

On the wall the shadow stood eastward with the bundle still raised. The public words had not changed whatever produced the sign. They had changed the three people in the stall.

I wanted to repair that change by being more exact. Exactness was the tool I trusted. If I explained the repeated timing, Hannit would understand that I had only reported evidence. Tavi would understand that I had not invented anything. The people who left would return and agree that facts were facts.

“I meant that it happens when—”

“Don't,” Tavi said.

The word was quiet. It did not need help.

I rose above the awning and moved away because Tavi had given me one instruction I could finally understand. Beneath me, the row continued working. A hammer struck at the east end. Somebody called for cord. A customer entered Debb's stall and came out again.

At the fourth stall, Hannit still held the repaired bracket. Tavi stood beside the bench. Their shadows touched on the wall because the sun had shifted, though neither person did.

I had wanted them to see.

They had seen before I arrived.

## Chapter 9: Work Done Without a Name

Tavi did not come to the stall the next morning.

I arrived early because I had expected somebody to explain my mistake. Hannit was already there. He had opened the front, pulled two boxes within reach, and placed the day's first job on the bench. A third box remained on the high shelf. He looked at it once and began with something else.

Hannit did not ask me where Tavi was. He did not ask anyone. Customers came, received short answers, and left with repaired things or later promises. Between them, Hannit moved from his bench-seat to standing and back again. The first rise took both hands. By the sixth, one leg shook before he straightened it. He complained about none of this.

His silence changed the shape of the stall. When Hannit talked, the work seemed to follow his voice. Without it, every tool strike sat alone. I kept expecting the next sound to become a sentence. It did not.

The row responded without making a plan I could hear. A repairer from the fifth stall came to borrow a clamp. Hannit's clamp was the wrong size and both of them knew it, but the man carried three shutter boards from the rear while pretending to compare them. He returned the clamp untouched an hour later.

Debb visited four times about the ladder. The first visit ended with a heavy box moved below the bench. The second put water within Hannit's reach. On the third, Debb adjusted the awning cord while describing the adjustment as proof that nobody else tied a useful knot. The fourth produced no ladder discussion at all. Debb stood near the stall until Hannit finished a difficult rise, then left.

At midday, a child delivered a covered dish and announced that it came from somebody who had not given permission to be named. The child forgot this instruction immediately and began to say who. An adult called from the street, and the name vanished into the answer.

Nobody spoke Tavi's name.

That silence was not empty. It required work. Each person had to approach without asking the obvious question, offer help without calling it help, and leave before Hannit could refuse properly. They were protecting Hannit from being watched in one way while watching him very closely in another.

On the second day, the pattern continued. Two people at the west end discussed whether the caravans would be early. They agreed early caravans took more passengers. Then one said a season passed quickly and the other answered that it passed at the usual speed. Both looked toward the fourth stall and turned to a roof problem.

Hannit told a customer the racks could wait. He said he had never been certain about needing them, though I had heard him plan their position and the number of shelves. When somebody offered help with the shutters, he said the boards were not as heavy as they looked. He did not try to lift them while that person was present.

I stayed mostly above the row. Shame does not give me weight, but I felt lower than the awning. I wanted someone to tell me the exact rule I had broken. Then I could admit it, learn it, and place the event behind me in a neat sequence. Nobody provided that service.

Near closing, Hannit rose for a tool and had to stop halfway. I moved toward him without knowing what I could do. I could not brace the frame or lift the tool. Debb arrived from the side, reached past him, and put the tool on the bench.

“Wind's worse,” Debb said.

There was almost no wind.

Hannit sat. “It gets into everything.”

That was the longest exchange at the fourth stall all day. Debb left, and Hannit returned to the job.

I finally understood that the row's silence was not permission to ask louder. Whatever came next had to begin with me learning how to stay inside an unanswered question.

## Chapter 10: Too Fast for Accident

I rebuilt the event from its smallest movement.

Five people had looked away before I spoke. They did not turn in a sequence. No first person warned the second. One moment their attention held the wall; the next it broke toward five safe objects. That kind of agreement can be planned, practiced, or learned so early that nobody remembers learning it. It cannot be random.

Once I accepted that, earlier facts changed their names.

The woman tying cloth had not failed to understand me. She had built a conversation wide enough to carry my question away. The man outside the house had not suddenly discovered lateness. The seller had not found water more urgent than an impossible shadow. Adults moved lamps low because low light pushed outlines under tables and benches. A stranger's hand had turned Nub's head before thought should have been possible.

Tavi had never been embarrassed by childish nonsense. Tavi had been protecting every person I tried to expose, then protecting themself when the eastward bundle appeared.

I reviewed the cost of the habit. Every adult on Seth watched where other people stood, where light fell, and when a child pointed. They found new subjects without pausing. They arranged yards and lamps so private signs became difficult to study. At the row, people worked beside a long bright wall and trained themselves not to use it.

This was not blindness. Blindness would have been free.

The town was paying attention in order to appear not to.

I still did not know why. The shadows did not speak. They showed reaching, turning, hiding, pulling, and travel. Perhaps Seth considered them false. Perhaps looking caused harm. Perhaps generations of people had agreed that the shapes belonged to something else. I could produce explanations all afternoon, which had recently proved to be one of my least useful abilities.

So I stopped at the fact I owned: they saw and chose not to look.

Near sunset, I went to the fourth stall. Hannit was putting tools away. The high box had come down at some point, though I had not seen who moved it. He lifted each tool only as far as needed and slid it into place.

“I spoke about Tavi in front of everybody,” I said. “I made something private public. I am sorry.”

Hannit kept working. “It's all right.”

“It is not.”

He put away the last tool and closed the box. “Have you eaten?”

I do not eat. Hannit knew that. On other days the question had been a joke, and I had liked it. This time he asked in the same words without offering the smile that completed it.

“No,” I said, because I did not know how else to answer.

“Then you've saved time.” He checked the stall front and sat again for a moment before beginning the effort of standing. “Off you go.”

I wanted anger. Anger would have given me a place to remain while it happened. Forgiveness would have given me an ending. Hannit offered neither. He told me the exchange was finished because he was closing his stall, not because the harm had closed.

I went above the roofs after dark. In the courtyards, low lamps pressed shadows beneath tables. People spoke and ate around them. From that height the lamps seemed like orderly points on a plan, each one placed where it had always been placed.

I had seen the arrangement on my first night.

This time I gave the fact its right name.

## Chapter 11: The Smallest Shadows

At the center of the day, Seth changed its habits.

I had noticed the change before and blamed heat. The sun climbed nearly above the town, the shade retreated beneath awnings, and people crossed open courtyards faster. That explanation was ordinary enough to survive until I began watching the shadows instead of only their movement.

Near midday the outlines shortened. Long wall-shapes pulled down to the ground and gathered close around feet. For a while, a person could stand in the open without placing a readable version of themself across the nearest stone.

That was when difficult things happened.

At the well, a man who had avoided one woman for several days crossed the yard and spoke to her. I had seen him choose longer routes, pause inside doorways, and wait until she left a stall before entering it. Under the high sun he approached directly. Their exchange lasted only a few minutes. She answered with both hands on the well rope. He looked at the ground once, though there was almost nothing there to read. They laughed in the uncomfortable way of people discovering that a feared conversation is still a conversation, then separated.

Before the shadows lengthened, three more hard acts followed. One person asked another for money. Two women completed a disagreement they had been carrying in pieces through several days. A boy near fifteen delivered a short message to a stall owner and left at once. The stall owner remained still after him, holding the same tool without using it.

I did not know the words in every exchange. I did not need them. The approaches told me enough: slowed feet, false errands, the extra moment before speaking. My mistake with shadows had taught me not to promote those signs into complete stories. I could still see that people chose this hour for things they had delayed.

When the sun shifted west, the outlines grew. The courtyard returned to ordinary buying, carrying, and complaint. Nobody announced the change. Nobody said the safe part of the day had ended.

The custom had no bell, rule board, or keeper. People had simply discovered that a smaller shadow left less for others to misread. Then they built private courage around the light.

I wanted to tell somebody I had solved it. The want arrived with such perfect timing that I almost found it funny.

I kept the answer to myself.

That was a small act. It repaired nothing. Tavi remained absent, Hannit still did the upright work badly, and five people still remembered what I had said. Silence after harm is not automatically wisdom. Sometimes it is only the first task you should have managed earlier.

I stayed near the well until the next hour began. A child asked why I was doing nothing. I said I was practicing. The child asked what. I said nothing, which was an answer and also the practice. The child studied me, decided I was poor company, and ran away.

On the way back to the row, I passed a wall holding a clear shadow. Its owner stood in a doorway speaking to somebody inside. The dark hands folded and unfolded behind the owner's still back.

I saw the motion. I did not test it, name it, or wait for a second sign.

At the fourth stall, the high box sat on the floor and Hannit was finishing Merrat's hinge. He looked tired. He was also complaining to the metal because the person who had made it had chosen the wrong thickness. The complaint was not quite conversation, but it was closer than silence.

I settled above the stall front. When Hannit needed a message carried to the fifth stall, I went. When he wanted the answer, I brought it back. Neither of us called that help.

The shadows lengthened behind us, and I left them alone.

## Chapter 12: Because the Work Was There

Tavi returned on the third morning.

There was no gathering. Debb did not stop at the fourth stall. Hannit did not ask where Tavi had been, and Tavi did not offer the answer. I saw them arrive from above the row, cross the street, and lift the front box before Hannit could begin the slow process of reaching it.

“You're late,” Hannit said.

Tavi set the box by the bench. “You opened early.”

“That cannot be proved.”

“The stall is open.”

Hannit considered this unfair use of evidence and began telling Tavi what remained from the previous day.

The familiar exchange did not restore the previous morning. Tavi kept to the far side of the bench when I floated near. Hannit spoke again, but his lists stopped at the current day. The row greeted Tavi with exactly the amount of attention it gave anybody arriving for work. Debb called good morning from three stalls away without turning it into a question.

The first job needed a shutter board held upright while Hannit marked the fitting. Tavi planted one end on the floor and steadied the top. Hannit adjusted the board, complained that Merrat's doorway had never been square, and drew the mark. It was ordinary work. That did not make it easy. Ordinary work was the place where all three of us had to stand with what had happened.

I stayed near the front post. I did not tell Tavi that I understood now. I understood more than I had, but Debb had not yet given me the rule and Tavi had not given me anything beyond one word. Claiming understanding would have turned my progress into another fact about Tavi.

When a customer left a broken pot at the stall, Hannit needed to know whether the replacement handle at the ninth stall would fit. I went to ask. The repairer there sent me to the fifth stall for a measurement, and the fifth sent me back because Hannit had not provided the old pin. I made the trip three times.

Tavi watched the second trip. On the third, they held up the pin before I asked.

“Take this.”

“I can't.”

Tavi looked at me, then at the pin, and shut both hands around it. “Right.”

The fact that I lacked hands had not changed. Something else had. Tavi walked with me to the fifth stall, answered the measurement question, and came back without speaking about the public moment. The task lasted four minutes. It was the first shared work we had done since my mistake.

Later, the wall behind Tavi brightened. The real body leaned over the bench. The shadow stood a little apart inside its outline, facing the east end. No bundle appeared. I saw it and returned my attention to the pot handle.

That cost me less than I expected. I had imagined restraint as holding a door shut against myself. In practice it felt more like choosing the next useful fact. The pin was too wide. The new handle had a narrower socket. Hannit would need to alter one or find another.

I carried that answer back because answers about pot handles belonged to the person repairing the pot.

By late afternoon, the stall sounded almost like itself. Hannit had reached the wind. Tavi told him the wind had not changed since yesterday. Hannit said that proved its stubbornness. I made two more journeys along the row and was sent on a third before I returned from the second, which seemed to mean I had acquired a place in the work.

Nothing between us had been forgiven or solved. Tavi had returned because the boards still needed lifting and Hannit still could not lift them alone. Work created a narrow path through the stall. For that day, we stayed on it.

# Part III: What a Sign Cannot Tell

## Chapter 13: The Fear I Had Missed

For most of the next morning, Tavi and I spoke only through jobs.

Hannit needed a count from the fastener tray. Tavi counted, I checked, and we disagreed about whether one bent pin belonged in the total. A customer wanted to know when the shutters would be ready. Tavi asked Hannit, Hannit answered Tavi, and I carried the answer three steps to the customer, who had been near enough to hear it the first time.

This was not efficient. It was still more useful than forcing a conversation because I wanted relief.

Near midday, Hannit went with Debb to inspect a fitting at the next stall. Going meant standing, arguing that he did not need an arm, accepting the arm, and continuing to argue while they moved away. Tavi remained at the bench with the sorting box. I had a clear path to the street if they asked me to leave.

“I said what your shadow did in front of everyone,” I said. “I took away your choice to speak or stay quiet. I am sorry.”

Tavi turned a bracket so its open side faced the right section. “I know.”

I waited. The old version of me would have filled the space with proof that I had not meant harm. The proof was true and useless. Good intentions had not given Tavi back the moment.

Tavi sorted three more pieces. “You thought I was ashamed of seeing them.”

“Yes.”

“And that I was stopping you for the other people.”

“That too.”

“I was doing that too.” Tavi found a cracked bracket, placed it beside the box, and kept one hand there. “But not the way you thought.”

I did not ask what the shadow meant. The question stood between us anyway.

“I wasn't frightened of you when you came,” Tavi said. “You kept treating the wall like it was talking. I knew you would say something if you saw enough. Every time I moved in front of you, I was trying to stop this.”

Tavi tapped the cracked bracket once against the bench.

“This happened.”

The words were not loud. They hurt because they were exact.

I wanted to say that now I understood. I wanted to promise that no one would discuss it again. I had no control over everyone, and understanding one fear did not give me the rest of Tavi's story. So I said nothing.

Hannit and Debb were still arguing at the next stall. Debb said the fitting was worn. Hannit said everything was worn if Debb examined it with enough dislike. Neither of them looked toward us.

Tavi slid the cracked bracket across the bench. “That goes to Debb. It can be cut down for something smaller.”

I could not carry it. We both remembered at the same time.

Tavi picked it up. “Come on, then.”

We crossed the three stalls together. Tavi delivered the bracket. I reported that the customer with the shutters had accepted Hannit's time. Debb said customers accepted time only until time passed, which was a very Debb answer. Hannit began explaining why the shutters would be done when he had said.

Tavi went back to the sorting box. I followed at a distance that left the wall clear, though that may have been unnecessary and may have been noticed. I could not control either fact.

The apology had not repaired trust. It had given Tavi the truth of what I knew I had done, without asking them to make me feel better. The next thing between us was a broken bracket and a walk of three stalls.

That was enough for one morning.

## Chapter 14: Something Is Not Everything

Debb answered my question because Debb generally answered the question asked, even when the answer was unpleasant.

I waited until Tavi had returned to Hannit's stall and until no customer stood near Debb's wall. Waiting had become a new part of asking. Debb was beneath a bench with both arms inside a metal frame. Every few seconds the frame gave a small protesting click.

“Why does everyone avoid looking at the shadows?” I asked.

“We don't avoid seeing them.” Debb's voice came from under the bench. “That would take closing our eyes whenever the sun came out.”

“Then why does everyone act as if—”

“Because people get to keep part of themselves.” The frame clicked again. Debb muttered at it and reached deeper.

I moved closer to the bench, keeping clear of the opening where Debb would need to come out. “Do the shadows always tell the truth?”

Debb slid back until head and shoulders appeared. “They show something. If you think something is everything, that's your mistake.” Then Debb disappeared beneath the bench again.

I wanted a larger rule. I wanted to know whether shadows showed wishes, plans, fears, memories, or impulses. I wanted to know whether a hiding shadow meant the person wished to hide or feared being found. The categories multiplied quickly. If Debb had answered them all, I could have built a fine system and caused harm with greater accuracy.

The answer I had was smaller and more useful. A visible sign belonged to a person whose full meaning remained private.

Debb struck the frame twice. A loose piece dropped onto the floor. I could not retrieve it, so I reported that it had rolled behind Debb's left shoulder. Debb said the sound had already given its position. I moved sideways and corrected the distance: the piece had travelled farther than the noise suggested. Debb came out, found it, and told me the report was useful and should be repeated. That was the entire lesson.

I spent the afternoon checking it against the town. A shadow reached toward a closed door. I could say it reached. I could not say why. Another turned away from two people greeting its owner. I could report direction, not dislike. Tavi's shadow sometimes stood east, sometimes worked at the shadow bench, and sometimes lifted a bundle. None of those signs granted me Tavi's plan, if there was a plan.

The more exact I became, the less I could claim. This was difficult for me and probably good. Counting usually enlarges what I know. Ten repeated events become a pattern; a measured distance becomes a route; two matching marks become a connection. On Seth, careful observation created a boundary. I could collect more signs without getting across it.

Near closing, Debb brought the repaired frame out and tested it in the light. The shadow behind Debb pulled toward the east wall so hard that its arms seemed stretched. I saw the motion and also saw Debb glance toward me.

“The frame sits square now,” I said.

Debb checked the corners. “It does.”

Nothing else passed between us. I could not tell whether the glance had been a test, a warning, or ordinary attention. That was the point I would once have chased.

I carried the useful fact back to Hannit: the frame was ready.

## Chapter 15: The Useful Direction

For three days, I stopped offering solutions and carried information instead.

Carried is not physically accurate. I cannot hold a note, a pin, or the smallest useful scrap of cord. I went to a person, remembered what was said, and returned. If an object had to travel, someone with hands came with me. This made every errand slightly less convenient than it would have been for almost anyone else, yet the row kept sending me.

The first morning I made seven trips. By the third, I stopped counting at twenty because Hannit sent me toward the east end while Debb called me west before I reached the fifth stall. Being expected in two places at once was the first strong proof that I belonged there.

The work gave me a better use for attention. I learned which repairer kept narrow pins, which stall had the long cord, and which answer had to be repeated in exactly Hannit's tone before a customer believed it. I could see over crowds and cross a yard without following its paths. Those skills did not require me to name anybody's shadow.

Tavi noticed. There was no single moment when they approved. On the first day, Tavi handed messages to Hannit rather than me. On the second, they told me a measurement while continuing to work. On the third, I returned from the ninth stall and found the next two questions waiting.

That was trust in the size Tavi offered. I did not ask for a larger piece.

The eastward shadow remained. Once, Hannit discussed the long door and said Tavi could start stripping its fittings after the caravans passed. On the wall, the bundle appeared and rose. Debb was at the stall. A customer waited beside the post. Both adults moved their attention to the real hinge in Hannit's hands.

I did the same.

The second time, Hannit planned work for the dry season. Tavi's shadow took two hard steps toward the road. I was reporting a price from the sixth stall, and I continued the report. Hannit argued that the price was too high. Tavi said it was the same price as last season. The customer said both facts could be true. The work moved on.

Nothing had been solved. In some ways, the silence had grown heavier. Before my public words, each person could pretend their looking-away belonged only to them. Now everybody at the row knew the others had seen, knew Hannit had heard me, and knew Tavi had been absent. They had to build the custom again over a hole I had made in it.

They built it by continuing to look at hinges.

I wanted to help with the larger trouble. Hannit needed Tavi's labor. Tavi's sign still faced east. The caravans would return whether anyone spoke or not. Those facts pressed together, but none gave me the right to choose the conversation, the hour, or the answer.

My useful work was smaller. At the seventh stall, Debb needed to know whether Serrat at the east wall had a certain hook. I went. Serrat said no and directed me somewhere else. Debb had predicted both parts and sent me anyway. When I asked why, Debb said being right about Serrat was no reason to skip asking Serrat.

That sentence did more work than Debb intended, or perhaps exactly as much.

Evidence could tell me where a hook probably was. It could not replace the owner of the wall. A shadow could show a journey. It could not answer for the person whose outline contained it.

At the end of the third day, no one needed another message. Tavi closed the front box. Hannit complained that all useful work had finished at once. I drifted toward the west end because the children were shouting again, and for once I had enough room in my attention to hear them.

## Chapter 16: The Words Before the Game

The children played where the row failed to meet the rest of the town.

Three streets approached one another at the west end and missed. The leftover ground was too uneven for a stall and shaded earlier than the main road, so adults left it alone and children claimed it. Six of them occupied it that evening. Nub was the loudest, which did not help me identify who was in charge because leadership changed every few breaths.

They were deciding who would start a running game. This caused more argument than the game itself. One child claimed not to have started last time. Two others remembered the opposite. Nub said the previous time did not count for reasons Nub could not state without shouting over everybody.

At last, an older child made them form a ring.

The choosing words came before the game. The older child pointed to one face for each question, and the ring answered together while the finger moved on. Their voices lost all expression. These were not words they were considering. They were steps on a path their mouths already knew.

“Could anyone number the worlds?”

“No counting gets that far.”

“Were there hands enough?”

“Enough to draw every line.”

“Did the workers travel home?”

“Nearly all went back.”

“Then who kept watch?”

“One remained behind.”

The last word landed on a small child at the edge. The child screamed with delight. Everyone scattered, including the one who had done the pointing, and the real game began around me.

I stayed in the empty center of the ring.

The children had used *worlds*. Not lands, towns, or roads. Worlds. They had put hands beside lines, travel beside home, and a remaining watcher beside all the others leaving. Each word touched the question I had carried from seven crossings into the eighth.

I wanted them repeated slowly. That was not how children used them. The words were a tool for choosing who started, no more important to the players than a hinge is to the person opening a door. You do not pause during a chase to discuss the metal.

I asked Nub to stop. Nub was pursuing somebody twice Nub's height and did not accept the request. I asked the older child, who said they had already chosen. When I explained that I wanted only the words, the older child told me the words were finished until the next round.

“Then begin another round.”

Six children objected from six different positions. The game had started. Starting again would erase the first chooser, reward the person currently losing, and violate at least two rules that seemed to have been created during the objection.

I followed them until they ordered me out of the playing space. Then I rose above it and listened. The game had its own shouted calls, but none mentioned worlds or lines. The rhyme did not return.

The sun lowered. Shadows stretched from the children across the uneven ground, running in directions their owners did not. Nobody paid attention because the game required watching bodies. Nub's shadow hid behind another child's outline while Nub charged after the same child in plain sight.

For once, I let the shadow keep its own activity. I waited for the round to end.

It ended only when somebody called from a doorway that evening had arrived and six separate arguments began about whether that was true. The children left in pairs and singles.

“Tomorrow,” the older child told me. “If we play tomorrow.”

It was not a promise. It was enough to keep me near the west end.

## Chapter 17: Not the Same Word

The children did play the next day. They objected when I asked to join the choosing.

Nub's first reason was that I had not played yesterday. The older child's reason was that I did not know the words. Another child pointed out that I could not point, which was true and had somehow not occurred to me. A fourth said I was too old. Nobody knew how old I was, including me, but the claim gathered support.

I listened until they ran out of new reasons. “Could I stand in the ring and answer?”

This created a separate argument. At last the older child decided that answering was allowed because an answer did not require hands. Nub disagreed on the general principle that I should not get what I wanted. The others had become impatient to run, so I took a place between two children.

The older child began.

I answered the worlds line correctly. I repeated that no count could reach them all. I answered the hands line and said there were enough to draw every line. Then came the question about the workers going home.

“Almost all returned,” I said.

The ring stopped.

“Nearly,” Nub said.

“That means almost.”

Six faces looked at me as if I had proposed choosing the starter by throwing the smallest child over a wall.

The older one shook their head. “It isn't the word.”

Meaning was not enough. The path through the rhyme required the same stones in the same places. We began again because I had stepped on the wrong one.

On the second round, I used *nearly*. The last answer chose the same small child who had been chosen the day before. The child shrieked just as happily, which suggested either excellent luck or a game in which being caught carried no lasting burden. Everyone ran away from the ring.

I stayed behind with the exact words.

The correction mattered. Old language changes in mouths, especially when nobody is thinking about meaning, yet a choosing rhyme punishes one altered word at once. The children might not have known why the answer was *nearly*, but they knew it was not *almost*. That made the wording a kind of record.

It was still a poor record. I had no date, maker, place, or proof that the lines in the rhyme were the crossings I used. *Worlds* could mean distant lands. *Lines* could be roads, marks, ropes, or rows in a field. The watcher might be a person, a job in the game, or nothing beyond a sound that fitted.

I listed those doubts because I needed them. The old me would have taken four matching ideas and announced the builder of the crossings before the game ended.

Nub returned at speed to tell me I was standing in the escape path. I moved. A moment later the chosen child ran through the space where I had been, with three others following. Nub said this proved the instruction had been necessary.

“Who taught you the choosing words?” I asked.

Nub named an older child. That older child, when caught between rounds, named an older sibling. The sibling was not there. The trail ended because the game began again and my question lost to running.

I could have gone through the town asking adults. I did not. The rhyme belonged to the children's ordinary play, and adults on Seth had already taught me what happened when I carried another person's sign into public and demanded an answer.

I waited until the next choosing. This time I answered every word as given. The finger landed on Nub. Nub objected to the entire method, then took off running before anybody could replace the result.

The words had become mine to remember because I had joined the ring under its rules. Their meaning was still not mine to enlarge.

## Chapter 18: As Far as the Words Went

I remained over the empty ring after the children ran home.

The ground kept their routes for a little while. Dust hung where feet had turned. One shoe lay near the edge, thrown off during the last chase. The long shadows of the buildings reached across the place and joined until the separate outlines became one patch of evening.

I repeated the rhyme in order and divided what it gave me from what I wanted it to give.

It spoke of more worlds than a count could finish. I knew at least eight worlds, because I had occupied eight, and I suspected far more. The rhyme did not prove that its worlds were mine. It did make the word too exact to ignore.

It spoke of hands sufficient to draw every line. Hands suggested work. *Enough* suggested a number of workers measured against a task. The answer did not name one maker, a power, or a great machine. It sounded like people assessing whether a job had enough help.

It said nearly all returned home. That placed the workers away from where they began, then sent them back. A job had ended for most of them. The words did not say the lines closed, failed, or remained in use.

One stayed behind to watch.

That was the farthest edge. I did not know whether one meant one person, one group, one kind of being, or only the child chosen by the finger. I did not know what watching required. I did not know whether the watcher still watched.

Here is what I could keep: the crossing question might belong to labor rather than accident. Many workers from many places might have made the lines and gone home when their part ended. Someone, in some sense, might have remained.

Might mattered. Seth had taught me the size of that word.

The small child returned for the lost shoe. They searched the wrong side of the ground until I told them it was behind the broken stone near the lane. The child found it and sat down to put it on.

“Will you play tomorrow?” the child asked.

“I do not know whether I will be here.”

The child tightened the shoe. “If you are, don't say almost.”

“I learned that.”

“You learned it after making us start over.”

This was accurate. The child ran toward a doorway where an adult had called twice.

I travelled east through the work stalls while the last daylight climbed their walls. Hannit's front had already been shut. Debb's remained half open, the usual result when closing tools led to examining one final repair. No one stood where Tavi usually worked.

The rhyme gave me the largest clue I had found on Seth. I could have gone straight to Debb and asked who made the lines. I could have told Hannit that children's play remembered workers between worlds. I did neither. Debb's rule applied to evidence as well as shadows. A sign could be true without being complete.

For the first time since the crossings began to feel connected, I had a possible picture of the makers. They were not one great unknown shape in the dark. They might have been workers arguing about tools, numbers, routes, and whether someone had measured correctly. They might have finished and gone home.

The thought comforted me more than a grand answer would have. Work leaves errors, habits, repairs, and things nobody bothered to write down. It also leaves games.

At the fourth stall, the wall held only the building's evening shadow. I stopped there, repeated the rhyme once more, and kept every uncertain word attached.

# Part IV: The Work of Making Shade

## Chapter 19: Eleven Years of Glare

The awning began with Debb's back.

Debb had complained about the late light for eleven years. I know the number because three people supplied it when Debb began again the next morning. One said eleven years. Another said twelve, then corrected it after being reminded which roof had failed that season. Debb insisted the count began on the first day the sun faded a roll of good cloth, making it eleven.

The complaint itself had several parts. Afternoon light entered from the west beneath the short stall awnings. It struck Debb's third shelf, showed every grain of dust, bleached anything left there, and forced Debb to turn sideways while working. Turning made the back hurt. The back made Debb irritable. Hannit said the last step had existed before the first three.

That morning the glare caught a metal edge and flashed into Debb's face. Debb put down the tool.

“We should cover the whole row,” Debb said.

Nobody answered for a moment. The proposal itself was simple. Replace the eleven short covers with one long sheet. Its first rope would attach beyond the opening stall, its last would meet the stone at Serrat's corner, and the lower wall-side edge would catch the sinking sun before the glare entered the work spaces.

The difficulty was contained in one word: poles.

Everyone knew this without saying it. A continuous cover needed supports tall enough for head fins, strong enough for wind, and spaced so the cloth did not sag onto the work. Poles needed holes, fittings, rope, a ladder, and people willing to disagree about every one of those things.

The repairer from the fifth stall finally said, “Proper poles.”

“Obviously,” Debb answered.

That was the decision. The argument began after it.

Somebody said the west end could use an existing post. Somebody else said that post already leaned. Hannit claimed it had leaned for twenty years without getting worse, which was not the same as being straight. The needle worker four stalls down asked what kind of cloth Debb intended to hang over work that required light. Debb said cloth that stopped glare. The needle worker said that answered only Debb's problem.

She was right. The new shade would help backs, faded stock, and anyone waiting through the hottest part of the day. It might also dim fine work, trap heat, block wind, or pull a weak fixing from Serrat's wall. The row did not pretend one useful change was useful in every direction.

Tavi listened while measuring the height of Hannit's front post. No one said Tavi's name until Hannit asked for the number. Tavi gave it. Debb asked for the west post too. The discussion moved toward cord, anchors, and who had cloth long enough.

On the wall, Tavi's shadow stood beneath an awning that did not exist yet. Its outline was hard in the morning sun. I saw no bundle. That meant only that I saw no bundle.

I asked what I could do. Debb tested my ability to lift a pole, hold rope, and dig. I had none of those abilities. Hannit called the range impressive.

Debb pointed east. “Find out whether Serrat will let us fix to the wall.”

I could do that. I went at once, carrying no hook, plan, or certainty, and prepared to ask the owner before deciding the answer.

## Chapter 20: The Wall Owner's Answer

Serrat's wall formed the eastern end of the row. It looked solid from above and less certain at arm's length. Pale render covered the stones, hiding whether a hook would bite into a sound course or a pocket of old repair.

Serrat listened to the question from his doorway. I explained that Debb wanted a continuous cover fixed at the corner, that the pull would run west, and that several people had already formed opinions about the wall without asking its owner.

“Do what you like,” Serrat said. “If it falls, it falls toward Debb.”

“It may take part of your wall.”

Serrat looked at the wall as if meeting it after a long separation. “Then don't make it fall.”

That was permission, though not confidence. I returned with both parts.

By then a cart stood at the middle of the row. Nobody agreed who had fetched it. On it lay four poles, three different colors of cloth, a coil of rope Debb distrusted, and a ladder everybody distrusted more. The orange cloth was the only length close to sufficient. One end was newer because an older damaged section had been replaced. The needle worker called the color terrible. Debb said color was not load-bearing.

The plan moved from talk to objects. A pole placed at the west end revealed that the existing post could not be trusted. The base shifted when two people pushed it. Cloth laid along the street fell short until a folded section was brought from a back room. The ladder reached the middle fixings but not Serrat's upper corner. Every measured difficulty created one more job.

Hannit sat beside the cart and reviewed the fittings. He rejected two hooks, approved three, and said the rope would do if nobody expected it to behave like better rope. Debb said nobody did. This was not agreement, but the rope stayed.

Tavi and the fifth-stall repairer carried the first pole to the west hole. The pole was longer than either person and awkward in the narrow street. I went ahead to clear a path, which meant asking four customers to move and informing Nub that walking beneath a moving pole was not a game. Nub said it had not been a game until I mentioned it.

We reached the hole without striking anyone. That was a successful first trip by the standards then available.

At the east end, six people gathered around Serrat's wall and examined the place where a hook might go. Serrat watched from his doorway. Debb tapped the render. Hannit, who had made the trip slowly and refused to admit it, said the sound changed near the corner. Somebody claimed that proved good stone. Somebody else said it proved an empty place.

I asked whether they could test before drilling. Three answers arrived at once. Two contradicted each other, and the third concerned whose tool should be used.

Serrat said, “You all wanted the wall.”

Debb said, “We wanted one part of it.”

“You're getting the whole thing at this rate.”

The argument continued until Hannit chose a spot one hand lower. A small test hole found sound stone. The good news immediately caused another argument about whether the first spot would also have been sound.

I left them to it and travelled back over the row. From above, the cart, poles, cloth, and groups of workers looked less like confusion. Each object had acquired a next place. Each person had acquired somebody to disagree with.

The awning was no longer an idea. It had become a set of obligations that would still be there the next morning.

## Chapter 21: Three Measures and One Ladder

The first rope measurement was wrong because Debb used paces.

Debb's paces were consistent, according to Debb. Nobody disputed this directly. They disputed the size of a Debb pace, the effect of stepping around the cart, and whether the line should end at Serrat's wall or at the hook that would eventually be placed in it.

The second measurement used a marked cord and produced a different answer. The people holding the ends blamed each other. One had pulled too hard. The other had allowed the cord to dip. They repeated the work under the supervision of a third person, who did nothing except watch and still managed to be accused of standing badly.

The third answer became official because everyone was tired of measuring.

That number determined the cloth. Two lengths had to be joined before raising. Hannit marked the overlap while seated, and the needle worker agreed to stitch it only after examining the edges and explaining that the older cloth had been cut by someone hostile to straight lines. Tavi carried each end between the bench and her stall. The new section was brighter than the rest. No amount of discussion changed that.

The ladder caused a second branch of trouble. It had come from somebody's cousin. Everyone who saw it said it was too short for the east hook. This fact had apparently been known before anyone fetched it, yet the ladder had still completed the journey and now occupied the center of the street like proof of a mistake nobody owned.

Nub climbed the first rung while four adults were looking at the wall. Tavi lifted Nub down. Nub returned from the other side. Debb removed Nub a second time and assigned the important task of counting poles. There were four. Nub reported five, including the front post, and argued that nobody had said new poles.

I admired the defense. Debb did not.

The hook question returned in the afternoon. Hannit wanted the top pull low enough to reach and high enough to clear fins. Debb wanted it higher to block the west glare. The needle worker wanted the whole strip higher so more light entered beneath it. Serrat wanted the hole made before the next group arrived at his door.

Tavi stood on the ladder's safe rung and held a length of cord at the proposed height. I rose to the level of the east hook and looked west. From there the cloth line would slope too far. Water, if Seth ever received enough at once, would collect in the center.

I reported the slope. Three people said the line was level.

“From here it drops,” I said.

The repairer holding the west end fetched a weighted cord and hung it beside the pole. The cord did not match the pole. Everybody studied this result. Debb said the ground fell. Hannit said the pole leaned. Serrat said both conditions had existed before breakfast and should not have surprised anyone.

They shifted the west base a small distance and tested again. The line improved.

Nobody said I had been right. By then the slope belonged to the job, which was better. A useful fact does not need to continue belonging to the person who noticed it.

At closing time the holes were marked, the cloth joined, and the poles lay in order beside the stalls. Nothing had risen. Two full days of work had produced preparation that looked like an untidy street.

Hannit examined the joined cloth. “Tomorrow it becomes an awning.”

Debb looked at the poles. “Tomorrow it becomes an argument at head height.”

Both were correct.

## Chapter 22: Help Without Hands

The poles went up one at a time.

Three people lifted while a fourth guided the base into its hole. Somebody held each pole upright while loose stone was packed around it. I kept above the moving end and called if it drifted toward an awning, a wall, or a person's fins. This happened often enough that my lack of hands became less important.

The west pole leaned again. I said so before anyone released it. Debb asked whether I was certain. I rose until the pole lined against the corner of a distant roof, then moved sideways. The roof edge changed and the pole did not. That was not a proper measuring tool, but it showed enough for them to fetch the cord.

The cord agreed with me by a smaller amount than I had hoped. Everyone corrected the pole while explaining that they had already meant to.

At the second pole, the base would not settle. A stone blocked the hole. I could see it from above but could not reach it. Tavi lay down, put one arm into the gap, and worked the stone loose while two people held the pole above. The work was safe because nobody shifted. It was also unpleasant, hot, and exactly the sort of task that vanishes from the story once the pole stands.

When the stone came free, Tavi's hand was scraped. Hannit cleaned the cut and wrapped it without turning the job into a speech. Tavi flexed the fingers, found they worked, and returned to the pole. I carried the report to Debb that the second base would be delayed.

“I can see that,” Debb said from five steps away.

“Hannit sent the report.”

Debb considered this. “Then report back that I received it.”

I did. Hannit said Debb had wasted my time. I pointed out that Hannit had begun the waste. He told me not to become difficult simply because I was fitting in.

By midday, three poles stood. The fourth lay near Serrat's wall because the cart could not pass the ladder. I guided two carriers around the back of the stalls, then returned to find the ladder had moved and the direct route was open. Nobody had told us. Nobody accepted blame.

The work continued in that fashion. A hook needed a smaller pin, so I found one. The rope coil had been left west while the tying was east, so I sent somebody back for it. A cloth edge caught on Hannit's awning, and I saw the snag before pulling tore it. Tavi climbed the safe section of the ladder and freed the edge.

I could not lift any part of the awning. I helped keep a dozen small errors from becoming larger ones. It was not the kind of work I had imagined doing on a world with impossible shadows. It was better suited to me.

Near the fourth stall, Hannit sat with the fittings spread in order. His advice travelled by voice. He called the wrong hook before somebody carried it away, warned that one knot would tighten badly under load, and told Tavi not to climb with the wrapped hand if another person could do it.

Tavi ignored the last instruction once. Hannit repeated it without raising his voice. Tavi came down and sent the fifth-stall repairer up.

That small obedience mattered because Hannit rarely asked Tavi to do less. Tavi rarely accepted less without an argument. Neither mentioned the hand again.

By evening, all four poles stood. They did not form a perfect line. They formed a line good enough to carry cloth, once everyone stopped looking for new reasons to adjust them.

Debb found one more reason. The row adjusted the third pole before closing.

## Chapter 23: A Name Used for Work

The cloth went up the following day, and Tavi's name travelled the length of the row.

People called for Tavi to take the west corner, bring the good rope, check the overlap, move the ladder, hold one edge, release another, and tell Nub to leave the cart alone. Each call concerned the task in front of them. Nobody used the name to ask about caravans, seasons, absence, or the direction of a shadow.

That difference changed the air around the work.

Tavi carried the cloth with four other people. The long orange fold rested on their raised hands while they moved east beneath it. I floated above and watched for snags. The brighter repaired section passed over Hannit's stall, then stopped when somebody at the fifth pole called that the rope had twisted.

There was no fifth pole. This caused a delay while everyone identified which support the caller meant. Nub had won that argument without being present.

Hannit remained at the fourth stall with the tying plan. He could not climb, but he knew how the load would travel. Each time somebody proposed a knot, Hannit asked what would happen when the cloth pulled west. If the answer involved the knot tightening into a shape nobody could release, he rejected it. Debb rejected two of his answers in return. Between them, the rope became secure.

Tavi's wrapped hand stayed mostly below shoulder height. When the cloth needed lifting near the center, another worker took that place without discussion. Tavi moved to the loose end and kept it from dragging. The change did not make Tavi less useful. It made the work fit the worker who was there.

I noticed that because the fourth stall had spent years doing the opposite. Work had arrived, and Tavi had fitted themself to it.

Hannit noticed too, though I could not say what he made of it. When someone asked Tavi to climb, Hannit answered that another person had the ladder. He kept his eyes on the rope. Tavi did not argue this time.

The whole length rose after midday. Orange cloth lifted from a wrinkled bundle into a low roof. Air caught it at once and made the center billow. Six people held rope while Hannit shouted that pulling harder was not the same as pulling evenly. Debb shouted a different set of instructions. The cloth settled between them, which may have been the only available way to satisfy both.

At Serrat's end, the corner would not sit square. The fixing held, but the cloth pulled diagonally and left one edge loose. Tavi stood below with the loose rope. Hannit asked for the line to be returned through the lower loop, then around the hook. Tavi followed each step. The corner flattened.

Hannit was right and mentioned it immediately.

He mentioned it again when Serrat came out. He told the needle worker. He told a customer who had arrived after the correction and could not have appreciated the difficulty. Tavi said the corner would loosen if Hannit continued talking near it. Hannit called this disrespect for technical achievement.

The exchange sounded almost like the first morning I had met them. It was not the same. Hannit had directed other workers instead of letting every upright task settle on Tavi. Tavi had accepted a limit without vanishing from the work. The row had used Tavi's name many times without turning Tavi into the subject.

The sun dropped toward the west. Light struck the raised cloth and changed color before reaching the ground. The old awnings beneath it looked like darker shelves. Shadows softened, then sharpened when the cloth lifted in the wind.

The east corner still needed one more tie. Debb refused to call the work finished. Everyone else refused to continue hungry.

They agreed to return after the morning rush, which meant nobody agreed and everybody went home.

## Chapter 24: Light with No Edge

The final tie took most of the next day.

This was not because the tie itself was difficult. It took ten minutes once everyone accepted Hannit's method. The delay came from morning customers, a missing hook, a new argument about the ladder, and Serrat's belief that the corner should be raised after he had spent two days wanting the drilling to end.

At last Tavi passed the rope through the lower loop. Another worker climbed the ladder with the upper end. Hannit directed from below. Debb pulled the west line until the cloth lost its deepest sag. The needle worker called from four stalls down that the center was too low. Nobody adjusted it because the center was no longer moving and success had made them cautious.

The knot tightened. The cloth held.

People stepped into the street to judge what they had made. The awning ran above all eleven stalls from the west support to Serrat's wall. Its orange was uneven, with one newer patch and several faded lengths. The poles were close to straight. The east corner sat square enough that only Hannit pointed out how it had been achieved.

Debb walked beneath the whole length and back. Near the seventh stall, the late sun reached the outer edge but did not strike the third shelf. Debb stood in the usual work position, turned once to test the back, and said the cloth should have gone up eleven years earlier.

The needle worker held dark thread beneath it. “I told you.”

“You have not told us the thing you're about to tell us yet,” Debb said.

“It's dim.”

That problem could wait until morning, when the light would show its full habits. For the moment, the people who had lifted, tied, measured, argued, and watched began going home. The cart left. The cousin's ladder left after another argument about who had borrowed it. Loose cord disappeared into stalls.

Soon the row was empty.

I stayed beneath the cloth as the sun lowered. Orange light filled the walkway. It had the enclosed color of a room, though there were no doors and the hot plain remained visible at both ends. Dust still moved in the air, but the grains did not flash.

The ground changed most. Where clean bodies had once cast hard outlines onto the white wall and street, soft grey shapes now pooled beneath the cloth. Fins blurred into heads. Hands dissolved at the edges. When the fabric lifted, a shadow briefly sharpened, then became uncertain again as the cloth settled.

The awning did not remove the shadows. A person standing close to the wall under a raised fold could still be read. A careful watcher might follow movement inside a soft outline. Privacy had not become complete.

Certainty had become more expensive.

I floated the length of the row and tested sight from several heights. At Hannit's stall, the bench and wall remained visible. At Debb's, the third shelf no longer caught the west glare. At the needle worker's stall, the shade was deeper because two layers overlapped near one pole. The cost was not imaginary.

The row had built one practical object that changed several lives in different directions. It helped Debb. It complicated the needle work. It reduced heat from direct sun and held warm air beneath the cloth. It made private signs harder to claim without making them disappear.

Nobody had named that last use.

On my first night, I had watched people move lamps and decided they disliked glare. This time I stood under a much larger piece of cloth, looked at its useful flaws, and let glare remain the reason anyone gave.

The final light left Serrat's wall. The softened shapes vanished with it. Above me, the awning gave one slow pull against its ropes and held.

# Part V: Enough Uncertainty

## Chapter 25: The First Complaint

The first complaint arrived before the row had finished opening.

The needle worker carried a length of dark thread to Debb's stall and held it beneath the orange cloth. In full sun the thread was blue. Under the awning it looked black. She moved it from shade to street and back again several times, as if the color might become ashamed and correct itself.

“I cannot work in this,” she said.

Debb had prepared. “Last spring you said the glare faded your thread.”

“It faded the rolls on the shelf. I need light on the needle.”

“You put a sleeve on backward in the cold season.”

The needle worker stared. “Once.”

“The light was excellent then.”

That broke the first round because three people laughed, including the needle worker. It did not end the complaint. She returned to her stall and moved her work table closer to the street. For the rest of the morning, anyone entering the row had to step around one corner of it.

The problem gathered proposed repairs. Somebody suggested cutting a window in the cloth above her. Debb said a hole would pull open. Hannit said it could be reinforced. The needle worker said direct sun through one opening would be worse than the old awning because the light would cross the table as the day moved. Someone proposed a pale lining. Nobody had pale cloth.

Tavi suggested rolling the outer edge higher during the morning and lowering it before the western glare. The idea required two new ties and somebody willing to adjust them every day. Three people said that would be simple. None volunteered to be the person.

This mattered because the awning had already begun to feel finished. The poles stood. The ropes held. Everyone had gone home once. A complaint after that point sounds to the people who built a thing like an attack on all the work, even when it is only a fact about thread.

The needle worker had helped join the cloth. Her objection did not become smaller because she had taken part. Debb's back hurt less. That did not answer for her eyes.

I watched the light through the morning. At the needle stall, the overlap made the shade deeper, but moving the table outward gave direct light for several hours. Later, the sun would angle under the western edge. The fix was incomplete and usable, which described most fixes I had seen.

I reported the hours. The needle worker asked whether I intended to remain there every day and announce them. I said I did not know how long I would remain on Seth. She said that made the report less useful.

She was correct.

By midday, the air beneath the cloth had warmed. Without the high sun striking people directly, the row felt easier at first. Then the trapped heat gathered above the stalls and the wind passed over instead of through. Hannit complained that the awning had solved the sun by keeping its temperature. Debb said the lower side could be tied back. The needle worker said that would restore the glare.

Nobody removed the cloth.

They adjusted one side. They shifted the work table. They argued about a future pale panel. The needle worker continued sewing and continued objecting. Debb worked without twisting at the waist and continued claiming this proved the design.

Under them, the softened shadows moved with no clean edges. I could have described that as the benefit worth every other cost. It was not my benefit to price. Privacy did not make the lost work light unreal, and one person's relief did not settle another person's complaint.

Near closing, the needle worker brought the finished piece past Debb's stall. The stitches were straight. Debb pointed this out.

“I can work in bad light,” she said. “I should not have to.”

Debb looked toward the cloth instead of answering. The orange surface lifted once in a weak wind, then dropped.

The awning held, and so did the complaint.

## Chapter 26: What the Cloth Did Not Fix

Three days beneath the awning established its habits.

Morning light entered from the open street and gave the needle worker several useful hours. Near midday the whole row became evenly dim. Late in the day, the western edge blocked the glare that had troubled Debb for eleven years. The cloth warmed, the air slowed, and someone complained about heat at least once each hour.

The lower side was tied back twice and lowered twice. No permanent answer appeared. The needle worker kept her table near the street. Customers learned to step around it. Debb stopped working at an angle. Hannit found that the orange light made one kind of metal harder to judge and another easier. Serrat came from the east doorway each morning to inspect the hook and deny that he was inspecting it.

Ordinary life fitted itself around the new object.

The shadows changed too. Under steady cloth, most became patches with blurred arms and fins. A person had to stand close to the wall before a separate movement showed clearly. When wind lifted an edge, direct sun cut one sharp outline across the soft ones, and every adult nearby became interested in some other task until the cloth settled.

Children did not change as quickly. Nub pointed at a dark shape under a raised corner and announced that it was climbing. The nearest adult placed a hand on Nub's head and redirected the whole child toward a coil of rope. The action worked under orange shade exactly as it had in white glare. A habit older than the awning did not vanish because privacy became easier.

I wondered whether the town would teach children differently now. That was another question I did not own. The cloth had existed three days. The custom had shaped lives. A better condition does not erase the reason a habit formed, and it does not automatically tell anyone how to stop.

Tavi worked. The hand wrap came off on the second day. The scrape had closed enough for ordinary lifting, though Hannit kept the highest shelf for somebody else. Tavi noticed this and said nothing. Hannit said nothing too loudly about a box that clearly needed moving until the fifth-stall repairer moved it.

The long door returned to the bench. Hannit examined its fittings without saying when Tavi would start the later work. He listed what had to be done today: clean two pins, mark the split edge, ask Merrat whether the old boards could be used. Tavi completed the first two and sent me with the third question.

This was not a decision about next season. Hannit might only have learned to speak about the current task while Tavi stood under a cloth that made the wall difficult to read. Tavi might still intend to leave, might be testing the thought, or might carry a bundle in the shadow for a reason neither the road nor caravans explained.

The cloth did not give me a better answer. It made certainty harder to steal.

On the third afternoon, the wind lifted the newer orange section. Direct light crossed Tavi's wall for less than a breath. A bundle appeared at the shadow's feet. Before the dark hand reached it, the cloth dropped and the outline dissolved.

I was the only person facing that way. Or I thought I was. Under the awning, knowing where anybody else looked had become difficult too.

I returned to the message I had brought from Merrat. The old boards could be used if Hannit replaced one split brace. Hannit objected that the brace had not been split when he last saw it. Tavi asked when that had been. Hannit changed the subject to poor storage.

The work continued.

Look, it would be easy to say the row had solved privacy. It had not. Anybody determined to watch could stand close, wait for wind, and gather pieces. Children still pointed. Adults still turned them. Tavi still lived with a private sign that had once been said aloud.

What changed was smaller. The town no longer had to succeed at looking away every second. The cloth made room for uncertainty, and uncertainty gave people a little rest.

## Chapter 27: The Small Fitting

On the fourth morning under the cloth, Hannit needed a small fitting.

This was not unusual. The fourth stall needed something it did not have several times each day. What made the errand familiar was that Serrat almost certainly did not have the part either. Asking Serrat was the accepted first step toward being sent to the person who did.

Hannit held the broken fitting beside the long door's lower hinge. “Go see whether Serrat has one of these,” he told Tavi. “He hasn't, but ask him.”

Tavi looked at the fitting. “If he hasn't, why am I asking?”

“So he can tell you who does. This is how trade works.”

“We know who does.”

“Then you will arrive there faster after Serrat confirms it.” Hannit placed the fitting on the bench. “When you come back, we'll move the long bench. I cannot shift it alone, and the racks need the space.”

The words put Tavi in a near future measured in minutes. Go east. Ask. Return. Move the bench together.

Tavi picked up the fitting. “All right.”

They walked toward Serrat's end under the orange cloth. Their soft shadow crossed the ground without a readable direction. The new section lifted once, but Tavi had already passed the wall where I stood. I did not follow.

Hannit turned back to the hinge. He removed the worn pin, set it beside the plate, and complained that whoever last repaired it had used the wrong size. I asked whether he had done the earlier repair. He said that was unrelated and moved the old pin farther from view.

Ten minutes passed. Tavi returned from the east carrying the same fitting and a different answer. Serrat did not have one. The repairer beyond the well might. Hannit said he had known this. Tavi said that made two of them.

Nothing dramatic happened.

That is the important part, though important is probably the wrong word. Hannit had once described months of Tavi's future without asking. Tavi's shadow had walked east beneath the weight of a bundle. I had made the conflict public, and no conversation on the page had settled it. Now Hannit sent Tavi east on an ordinary task and built his next ten minutes around Tavi returning.

Tavi did return. That did not promise next season.

They moved the long bench together. Hannit took one end from a seated position and pushed. Tavi lifted the other just clear of the floor. The bench caught on a rough stone. I saw the obstruction and told them. Tavi shifted left, Hannit pushed again, and the bench slid into the open space beneath the awning.

The move took less than a minute and required both of them. Hannit breathed hard afterward. Tavi rubbed the hand that had been scraped, then stopped when Hannit looked over. Neither asked whether the other could continue. They waited until both were ready and began arranging the rack pieces.

I helped by checking that the long side cleared the awning pole. It did by the width of two fingers I did not have.

The eastward road remained visible beyond Serrat's wall. The caravans would return. Tavi might leave with them. Hannit might already understand more than his words revealed. None of that became certain because one errand ended at the same stall where it began.

What I could see was enough for the morning. Hannit no longer spoke of a whole season as if Tavi were one of the tools stored on his wall. Tavi answered the task in front of them. Together they moved the bench.

The racks now had a place, and the future beyond them remained open.

## Chapter 28: The Reason Debb Gave

I asked one question I should have left alone.

By then I knew the rule. I knew the awning had practical causes that were true: Debb's back, faded cloth, heat, glare, eleven separate covers that no longer worked well. I also knew what the orange shade did to shadows. One reason did not cancel the other, but neither gave me permission to demand a connection.

I demanded it anyway. Debb was repairing a hinge beneath the bench. I waited until no customer stood nearby, which showed improvement without good judgment.

“Was the awning also for Tavi?” I asked.

Debb turned the hinge over. “It was for the glare.”

“I know that. I mean, after everyone saw—”

“The west light came in too low.” Debb tested the pin. “It hurt my back. Now it doesn't.”

I could have continued. The awning had been proposed after the public harm. The whole row had supported it. It produced exactly the uncertainty Tavi had needed. Those facts made a pattern, but they did not make the pattern mine to announce.

Debb slid the hinge toward the light, frowned, and redirected me toward a useful problem: the other end needed holding, which I could not do, so I should find somebody who could. I went to the fifth stall. By the time we returned, Debb had repositioned the hinge and claimed never to have needed help. The repairer stayed anyway, and together they pressed the two sides into line while Debb replaced the pin. No further answer arrived.

Above us, the orange cloth shifted with the weak wind. It might have been raised only for glare. It might have been easier to build because a whole row had seen what happened at Hannit's stall. It might have served a need nobody would ever name because naming it would repeat the harm it reduced. All three could be true. I had no way to sort them, and Debb had refused the sorting. That refusal helped me decide to leave Seth.

I do not choose when a crossing takes me. Leaving a world usually means moving until the world changes, following a road or open country without knowing whether the next step matters. On Seth I had stayed because the shadows caught my attention, then because the fourth stall gave me work, then because I had caused harm and did not want departure to become escape.

The awning had been standing six mornings when I chose the next one for the road. The timing mattered to me. I had remained through the building, the first complaint, the adjustments, and several days of ordinary work. I had not waited for Tavi to answer a question I no longer had the right to ask.

I told Hannit I would go the following morning. He said that was too little notice for anybody to prepare food, remembered that I did not eat, and became annoyed that this removed a proper complaint. Then he asked whether I expected to use the east road.

“It is the only one that continues past the town.”

“Road builders do like to encourage people.”

He returned to the long door. I waited for him to say something about returning. He did not. He asked me to tell the needle worker that her small fitting was ready.

I carried the message. The row had given me a final useful direction, and I took it.

## Chapter 29: The Side Tavi Chose

I left the town early, but the work row opened earlier.

Hannit had one front box out when I arrived. Tavi was untying the lower edge of the awning to let morning light beneath it. The needle worker's table waited near the street. Debb had not opened, which gave Hannit a chance to say this proved the seventh stall depended on everybody else's example.

I said goodbye before he could turn that into a longer argument.

Hannit answered with enough words for three goodbyes. If I ever returned, I should send warning so somebody could prepare food. Since I could not choose a world or send warning between worlds, this was impossible. He knew that and became annoyed with travel in general. He also wanted me to remember that the awning's east corner was his method, no matter what Debb later claimed.

“I will remember,” I said.

“Not as one of your mysteries. Properly.”

“Lower loop, then around the hook.”

He seemed satisfied. Then he told me to keep out of direct sun, remembered that direct sun did not trouble me the way it troubled a Seth, and said there was no point giving advice to something with no sensible needs.

Tavi finished the tie and carried a small fitting toward the east. Hannit called that Serrat did not have the matching piece. Tavi called back that Serrat was not the destination. This left Hannit without a complaint, though only briefly.

Nub was absent. Somewhere beyond the west lanes, a child shouted about being first, and I could not tell whether it was Nub. The choosing rhyme had entered my memory in the children's exact rhythm. I did not repeat it aloud. The west-end game did not require a farewell from me.

The needle worker arrived and moved her table half a pace farther out. She told Hannit the small fitting from yesterday was the wrong size. Hannit said that could not be true because he had selected it. She said his involvement was not proof. Their disagreement followed me as I moved down the row.

People said goodbye in the middle of other work. The fifth-stall repairer raised one hand while holding a clamp in the other. Serrat came out to inspect his wall hook and said he was checking whether the road remained where it had been. Debb opened late enough for Hannit to notice and early enough to hear about it.

At the seventh stall, Debb looked at me, then at the orange cloth. “No lifting today.”

“There was no lifting yesterday either.”

“Then you're well practiced.”

That was Debb's goodbye.

Tavi waited near Serrat's corner with the fitting still in one hand. The road began beyond the last buildings. For several days, every eastward sign had gathered meaning at that edge. Now Tavi stood there in the softened morning shade with a real object and an ordinary delivery.

“I'll walk to the end,” Tavi said.

I did not ask which end. We moved together beneath the awning.

Tavi stayed on my left. The long wall was also on my left. On earlier days, Tavi had placed a body, tray, bracket, or question between me and any clear view. Now they walked where the path happened to put them. When the cloth lifted and the wall brightened, Tavi did not change sides.

I noticed every step.

My old attention wanted to count them and say what they proved. I counted anyway, because I am still myself. I did not turn the number into a claim.

At the fourth stall, Hannit had begun explaining the needle fitting. At the seventh, Debb pulled one tie tighter. At the east end, the orange shade stopped and white morning light began.

Tavi and I crossed that line together.

## Chapter 30: What I Left Unsaid

Outside the awning, both of our shadows became sharp.

Mine was almost nothing. I am a point, and the morning sun produced a mark so small that it disappeared whenever the road surface changed color. Tavi's outline reached west across the street. The long fins lay back. One hand held the fitting. The other arm moved with the walk.

Inside the shadow, something shifted near Tavi's feet.

I did not lower myself for a better view.

That choice sounds easier now than it was while the sign remained beside me. I knew how to test it. I could have changed height until the dark movement separated from the road, then watched whether a bundle formed. No one stood between me and the evidence. Tavi had stopped managing my attention, which meant the decision had finally become mine.

I kept pace instead. The useful fact was the person walking beside me, not the shape that might explain them. Tavi had chosen to come to the end of the row. The fitting had a destination. The morning work continued behind us. All of that was available without stealing an answer from the ground.

We crossed the last stretch of the work row at the pace Tavi chose. The east wall passed on our left. At Serrat's corner, the new hook sat firm inside its test hole. A short piece of rope had been tied around the lower part, though I did not know whether that belonged to the awning or to something Serrat meant to do later. Once I would have stopped and asked. That morning I let one unexplained rope remain unexplained.

Tavi carried the fitting to a narrow ledge beyond the corner and set it beside a second piece of metal. The errand had a destination after all. No one came out to receive it. Tavi did not wait for anyone.

For a moment we stood where the buildings ended. Behind us, the row worked beneath orange shade. Ahead, the pale road crossed the flat country with no turn I could see. Heat had already begun to disturb the far surface. The town's wind reached the open land without walls to guide it and spread until I could hardly feel it.

Tavi looked at the road, then at me. “Do people get told where you're going?”

The question could have meant the town. It could have meant Hannit. It could have meant people on the next world, if another world found me. I had learned enough not to choose among those meanings for Tavi.

“I am not told where I am going,” I said. “Nobody else can be told more than that.”

Tavi considered the answer. “That will do.”

The awning lifted behind us. A bright strip crossed the last part of the wall, then narrowed. Tavi could see the change in my position if they wanted to; I held the same height and looked toward the road. When the cloth settled, Tavi's fins eased back. I cannot tell you whether those facts were connected. I can tell you I did not turn to find out.

They picked up neither fitting. The delivery was complete. Then Tavi turned east along the outside of the row, toward whatever small work waited beyond Serrat's corner.

I almost called after them.

There were many things I could have said. I could have promised to keep Tavi's story private, though I had already failed once and promises are not repairs. I could have asked whether the caravan mattered, whether the bundle would return, whether Hannit knew. I could have told Tavi about the children's rhyme and the workers who might have gone home. Each sentence would have given me a final answer-shaped object to carry away.

I said none of them.

Silence had meant several things on Seth. Hannit's silence after my mistake had held hurt I could not repair. The row's silence had protected Tavi while practical help reached Hannit. My silence at the well had been practice. This one was a choice made in front of the same pressure that had defeated me before: a true pattern, a person I cared about, and the chance to say it where it did not belong.

The words remained available. That was how I knew the choice was real. I had not forgotten the questions or solved them. I was leaving them with the person whose answers would change a life.

Tavi had asked one question. I had answered the part I owned. The rest travelled east with Tavi, out of my knowledge.

I turned toward the road.

At the first edge of the plain, the ground changed from packed street to pale dust. No post marked the boundary. A cart had left two shallow wheel tracks, and footprints crossed them before both faded in the hard surface. I followed the road because it was the only route that kept going. I still could not choose a crossing, but movement had found the previous seven worlds, and standing beside the last house had never made one appear.

The road did not become more informative because I knew people at one end of it. It had no signs. The wheel marks divided, joined, and disappeared where the wind had crossed them. I tried the smoother center for a while, then moved above one track because the reflected heat was less sharp there. This was unnecessary for my body and still made the journey easier to attend to. I have picked up stranger habits from worlds.

At my arrival, the road had looked like a line leaving for no clear reason. Now I knew what travelled on it: caravans twice a season, supplies for shelves, news, visitors, and anyone the drivers agreed to take. That knowledge did not tell me whether Tavi would ever use it. It made the road ordinary enough to hold the possibility without becoming a promise.

The sun rose behind my left side. The town shortened slowly. First the separate courtyards disappeared. Then the roofs joined into a white line. The orange awning remained visible because it was the only color strong enough to survive the glare.

As I travelled, I checked what Seth had given me.

The children's words suggested that many hands from more worlds than anyone counted had made lines, that most workers had returned home, and that a watcher had remained. Suggested was the correct word. I had no maker's name, no date, no purpose, and no proof that their lines were the crossings. The clue was real. Its uncertainty was also real.

The shadows had taught me the same thing at the size of one person. A sign could show movement without giving me a destination, show reaching without naming the wanted object, show hiding without explaining fear. Accurate sight did not turn another person's inner life into my report.

I had learned this by harming someone, which made it less satisfying and more useful. I would have preferred to learn it from a game before I spoke at Hannit's stall. That path had existed. I had heard the children on my first evening and chosen my own theory instead.

The cost remained behind me. Tavi had stayed away two days. Hannit had worked through pain and silence. Five people had carried knowledge they had tried not to expose. An awning could make future signs uncertain, but it could not remove the public sentence from their memory.

Look back with me from the road. It is tempting to call the orange cloth a solution because it is large, visible, and held up by four new poles. Solutions are easy to point at. The smaller changes mattered too: Hannit planning ten minutes instead of a season, Tavi accepting a different climber, the row using a name for work, and me seeing a bundle begin under lifted cloth and returning to Merrat's message.

None of those changes promised what came next. They were only behavior in the days I saw.

About half a mile from town, I stopped.

I do not usually look behind me when leaving a world. The next direction is already uncertain enough without measuring what grows smaller. This time I turned because the awning gave me a mark I could use.

From the plain, the cloth looked straighter than it had from beneath. One orange band ran along the north side of the white town. The newer section showed as a brighter square near the middle. I could count the four poles if the wind lifted the edge, though the stalls and people beneath had blended into shade.

Distance performed the same trick as the cloth. It softened separate lives into one visible shape. From half a mile away, the row could have seemed united, finished, and simple. I knew better. Under that narrow band were eleven different work spaces, several arguments still active, a table in everybody's path, and a third shelf that no longer faded. A single outline could hold all of them without explaining any one.

I knew what was there without seeing it. Hannit had the long door open on his bench. Debb's third shelf was out of the glare. Serrat's hook held. Tavi had carried a fitting east. Children would return to the west ground and choose a starter with words older than their interest in them.

I did not know who would board the next caravan.

Nor could I see whether Tavi had gone back to Hannit after leaving the fitting, or whether Hannit had noticed the extra distance in that errand. The town hid those facts simply by becoming small. I found I could bear that. Earlier, not seeing had felt like an obstacle I should rise above. Now the hidden work below the orange strip belonged to the people doing it. If Tavi spoke, the words would enter the row by Tavi's choice. If no one spoke that morning, the hinges would still need pins, the shutters would still need fitting, and the day would remain real.

The road remained empty between the town and the haze. I turned east again. Behind me, the orange cloth rose once in the wind and fell, hiding every hard outline beneath it.

Somewhere in that shade, the needle worker was still complaining.
